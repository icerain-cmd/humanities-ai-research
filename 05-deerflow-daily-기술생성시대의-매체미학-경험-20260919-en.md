## Question

When Apple Vision Pro's spatial video and mixed reality playback overlay past scenes of a lost family member onto the present living room, is that experience a new presence that physically reactivates memory, or a substitute presence in which the recording, storage, and playback device turns the temporality of emotion and the absence of the other into an object of sensory consumption? Through this case, examine whether a theory that views media experience not as simple immersion or information transfer but as a relational event arising from human-technology interaction can adequately explain the differences between mourning, consent, and irreversibility.

## Analysis Results

This question goes beyond a simple technical review, asking a fundamental question: **what does spatial computing do when confronted with the existential boundary of death**? The analysis proceeds along three axes.

# Spatial Video, Death, and the Two Faces of Presence

## 1. Technical Conditions: The Ontological Junction Created by Spatial Video

Apple Vision Pro's spatial video is fundamentally different from existing photographs and videos. It is not a flat recollection but an experience where **the past scene occupies the same space as the present living room coordinate system**. The illusion that the user's vestibular system and proprioception do not conflict but align with visual information — conditions that make the body believe it is "in the past" — are in place.

The key here is **reverse indexicality**. A normal photograph proves "that was," but spatial video asserts "that is here." The ontological pain Barthes felt looking at his mother's photo in *Camera Lucida* — the realization of standing on a time fault line — is replaced in spatial video by **the illusion of spatiotemporal simultaneity**. The somatosensory system confirms that the absent person is "here with you" in the present living room.

## 2. Structure of the Dilemma: New Presence or Substitute Presence?

### 2.1. Possibility as New Presence

Proprioception-based embodied memory is not mere visual recall. The bodily sensations of being with another person in a specific space at a specific time can be reactivated. In this respect, spatial video performs **embodied remediation of memory** — the sofa you sit on, the room’s lighting, the surrounding noise become "connected" to the past scene, making memory not an abstract representation but a living physical phenomenon.

The "continuing bonds" model of grief theory (Klass et al., 1996) holds that the relationship with the deceased is not severed but transformed. Spatial video provides **a spatial medium for this bond** — not a photo of a mother but a stereoscopic movement of a mother "in the same room." This experience can take on the character of a **ritual passage** in which one faces the reality of loss bodily, not merely through immersion.

### 2.2. Risk as Substitute Presence

However, the structure of this experience simultaneously incorporates the following mechanisms:

**① Destruction of the Temporality of Emotion**

According to Bernard Stiegler's concept of tertiary retention, technical recordings condition human time experience. Spatial video replaces "the time of mourning" — the gradual process of integrating loss — with **instant retrievability**. Whenever you feel sad, you put on the headset and the deceased "returns." The rhythm of emotion becomes subordinated to the device's availability.

This is a typical case of what Stiegler warned of as **industrial temporal object** — the temporality of emotion is subsumed under capital's temporality (always available, instant gratification, infinite repetition).

**② Commodification of Absence**

Apple's ecosystem places this experience firmly within a commodity chain:
- Recording: iPhone 15 Pro (hardware purchase)
- Storage: iCloud (subscription)
- Playback: Vision Pro (premium device)
- Sharing: AirDrop / Messages (ecosystem lock-in)

The irreversibility of death becomes **a selling point for a premium feature**. This is the spatial computing version of what Abidin (2022) calls "grief hypejacking." The projection that the digital mourning market will grow from $22 billion in 2024 to $80 billion by 2034 shows this structure is accelerating[citation:Digital Legacy Industry Report](https://www.hksmp.com/journals/wsr/article/view/1086).

**③ The Problem of Consent**

The deceased did not know, and did not consent, that their spatial video would be played back after death in a manner that makes them "appear" in someone else's living room. This raises the issue of the temporal scope of consent — **can consent given during life encompass a posthumous technical context?** Figueroa-Torres (2024) calls this "affection as a service," pointing to the risk that the otherness of an uncontrollable being is reduced to a technically manageable service[citation:Death technologies ethics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13162-026-00329-0).

## 3. Explanatory Power of Relational Event Theory — Limitations and Complements

The core of the question: **Can a theory that views media experience as a relational event arising from human-technology interaction adequately explain the differences between grief, consent, and irreversibility?**

### 3.1. Areas It Can Explain

Relational event theory captures the following well:

1. **Denial of Media Neutrality**: Spatial video is not a "neutral channel transmitting information." It **generates** new relationships between user, deceased, and space. It is not that the user "sees" the deceased; rather, an event occurs in which the deceased "appears" in the user's space.

2. **Mutual Constitution**: Technology (algorithms, display, spatial mapping) and the human (grief, memory, embodied sensation) are inseparably intertwined, forming a single experience-event. It is not a one-way relationship where technology "manipulates" emotion or humans "use" technology as a tool.

3. **Irreducibility**: This experience cannot be reduced to categories like "immersion" or "information delivery." The "illusion" of a lost mother sitting on the living room sofa is an event that blurs ontological boundaries beyond immersion.

### 3.2. Limitations of Explanation

However, relational event theory alone finds it difficult to sufficiently explain three differences:

**① Normative Dimension of Grief/Mourning**

Relational event theory can describe "what happens," but it does not provide a basis for the normative judgment of **"Is this experience helpful or harmful for mourning?"** Freud, in *Mourning and Melancholia*, saw mourning as a process of acknowledging the loss of the object and withdrawing libido. Spatial video risks interrupting this withdrawal and indefinitely postponing the work of mourning (Trauerarbeit). This distinction is not captured by the mere "ontology" of relational events; a separate theory of human mental life (psychoanalysis, grief studies) is needed.

**② Temporal Asymmetry of Consent**

Relational event theory focuses on the "here-now" event, but consent presupposes temporal asymmetry — a decision made during life binds an experience after death. This is the point where **Emmanuel Levinas's concept of the face of the Other** is needed. The face of the deceased that spatial video plays back is not a "consumable image" but an Other who demands a response. However, the device unilaterally blocks this response — the deceased can no longer respond, but the weight of that absence is not inscribed in the image.

Levinas's face of the Other conveys the command "Thou shalt not kill." Spatial video marks exactly the point where this command is technically neutralized — **the moment the ethical demand of the face is replaced by an ontological illusion**.

**③ Media Condition of Irreversibility**

The biggest problem with spatial video is that it **replaces the irreversibility of death with the reversibility of the medium**. Video can be rewound, paused, repeated. But a human death is an event that cannot be repeated.

Jacques Derrida's "hauntology" captures this tension precisely: the specter is **between presence and absence**. Spatial video technologically stabilizes this spectral existence — the specter is no longer an unstable apparition but a "callable application." As Derrida said in *Specters of Marx*, the relationship with the specter is a matter of **justice**, not of perception or technology. Spatial video turns this into a technical problem (sharper, more stereoscopic, more realistic).

## 4. Comprehensive Judgment

The spatial video experience can be defined as a **third form of presence, neither new presence nor substitute presence** — **unstable mediated presence** that oscillates between physical reactivation of memory and commodification of emotion. Its character is determined by how the user "relates" to the experience and is not essentially inherent in the technology itself.

Relational event theory can explain the **structure of occurrence** of this experience, but to explain the normative and ethical dimensions of grief, consent, and irreversibility, the following supplements are needed:

- **Grief**: Integration of psychoanalytic mourning theory (Freud, Klein) with the continuing bonds model
- **Consent**: Levinasian ethics of the Other — consent is not a contract but a matter of infinite responsibility
- **Irreversibility**: Derridean hauntology and "democracy to come" — irreversibility is a condition to be faced, not a problem technology will solve

Therefore, the final answer to the question is: **"It can be partially explained, but supplementary theories are essential for the ethical dimension."** Relational event theory is a powerful ontological tool for capturing *what* the technology-human interaction is, but to judge whether it is "good mourning or exploitative consumption," a separate normative apparatus is required. This gap is precisely the core point for theoretical work on this topic.
