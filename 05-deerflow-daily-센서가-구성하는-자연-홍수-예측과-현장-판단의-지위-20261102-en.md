# Flood Prediction Systems and the Partial Constitution of Nature
## — The Formation of “Manageable Nature” within the Chain of Detection, Classification, Prediction, and Response, and the Status of Situated Judgment

## Ⅰ. Theoretical Coordinates of the Case

A system that combines water-level sensors, satellite imagery, and records of past disasters to designate risk zones and guide evacuation is a typical **case of an apparatus of measurement**, but it is also more than that. This system is not a tool that merely “reads” nature; it is a **field of conditions that establishes flooding as an administrative and experiential reality**. At this point, Atura’s central insight operates directly: the question is not “What has been represented?” but rather, “**What has been preconfigured as experienceable?**”

The categorical system of past disaster records pre-draws the horizon of “what kinds of floods can exist”; the threshold values of water-level sensors determine “when something is dangerous”; the resolution of satellite imagery limits “which terrain can be seen”; and the evacuation-guidance interface coordinates “to whom and at what rhythm an alert reaches.” These four mechanisms reproduce Atura’s structure of linked conditions: the accumulation and classification of data, the selection and prediction of algorithms, the rules and arrangements of platforms, and the guidance provided by interfaces.

## Ⅱ. Nature’s Reality: Given Prior to Measurement or Constructed within the Chain?

### 1. Breaking Down the Either-Or

An honest answer to this question cannot lean entirely in either direction. This inability to lean is not theoretical indecision; it results from the fact that the two answers make different claims.

The **minimum realist requirement** cannot be denied. Rain falls, water flows, rivers overflow, and a once-in-a-century flood arrives in ways that can shatter the system’s predictive horizon. No matter how sophisticated the sensors are, the deaths of residents who fail to evacuate cannot be reduced to something merely “constructed.” Nature’s **ontic resistance**—that is, physical forces operating independently of human and systemic intentions—exists prior to measurement. A constructivism that denies this minimum requirement risks turning the reality of disaster into a morally incomprehensible language game.

At the same time, the **requirement of construction** cannot be denied either. A “risk zone” is not the flood itself but a *particular mode of making the flood real*. Even with the same amount of rainfall, the boundaries of a risk zone change according to how thresholds are set. Terrain that cannot enter a hydrological model normalized into streamlined forms—such as steep slopes, poor drainage, or drainage-channel alterations caused by newly built housing complexes—is treated as though it did not exist in the system’s world. In Atura’s terms, the system forms a “manageable river” through the **prior arrangement of experienceability**, while **marginalizing** terrain, organisms, and residents’ experiences that sensors fail to capture: beaver dams, sediment deposits, measurement points covered with weeds, and embodied knowledge of past flood routes.

### 2. The Argument for Partial Constitution

The formulation must therefore be as follows: nature’s reality is neither **entirely given** prior to measurement nor **entirely created** by measurement. Nature is **partially constituted** within the chain of detection, classification, prediction, and response. Here, “partial” points precisely to three things:

1. **What is constituted is not nature’s existence but the mode of nature’s reality.** What is constituted is not the existence of water molecules, but the reality of flooding as *manageable*, *graded according to risk*, and *subject to evacuation directives*. The system does not create the flood, but it creates “what the flood is as real for us.”

2. **A surplus always remains outside the process of construction.** Sensor blind spots, variables that the model does not weight, and events that go unrecorded do not enter the system’s world, but they nevertheless exist. This surplus later returns to the system as a failure under the names of “misjudgment” or “missed detection.”

3. **The boundary remains open.** The threshold of a water-level sensor, in the language of Barad’s agential realism, is an **agential cut** drawn as the world and apparatus intra-act. Yet this cut does not become fixed once it has been drawn; it is continually re-bordered through interventions in the field and collisions with events. It is precisely this openness that leads us to the second question.

## Ⅲ. The Judgments of Field Workers and Residents: External Supplement or Condition of Joint Constitution?

### 1. What the Expression “Correcting Misjudgments” Already Assumes

The expression “situated judgment that corrects the model’s misjudgments” itself conceals an important ontological fact. For a misjudgment to be recognized *as a misjudgment*, a standard for verification must already exist outside the system. When a water gauge is blocked by sediment, when satellite imagery is obscured by clouds, or when an artificial structure absent from past disaster records is built, the only place from which this can be *known* is the field. In other words, field workers and residents are not merely assistants; they are **bearers of the minimum reality capable of discovering that the system has mapped the world incorrectly**.

In Latour’s terms, this is not the status of an intermediary but of a **mediator**. An intermediary transmits an input as it is, whereas a mediator transforms the world in the course of transmitting it. Situated judgment translates nature into the system’s language—reducing the physical fact of a malfunctioning water gauge to an inconsistency in the system’s output—and then retranslates the system’s output into the language of life—enabling residents who receive an alert to reconsider whether to evacuate on the basis of their homes’ actual histories of flooding. Without this **double translation**, the system becomes a closed circuit that takes only its own outputs as its inputs—a classic failure mode of flood prediction.

### 2. Joint Constitution and Asymmetrical Responsibility

Situated judgment is therefore part of the conditions of action through which nature is jointly constituted. The system (a nonhuman actor), field workers (technical sensibility), and residents (embodied knowledge) jointly establish what a “flood” *means in this neighborhood on this particular night*. This is close to the stage of relational resonance described in Document 2’s topology: a generative wonder in the form of an alert, an unsettling unfamiliarity expressed as “this alert does not fit our neighborhood,” critical reflection on the system’s premises, and finally a resonance in which residents reorganize evacuation together with their neighbors within the context of their own lives. Resonance is not the passive acceptance of system output but **a joint rearrangement through the recontextualization of that output**.

However, one point must be protected decisively here: **joint constitution is not symmetrical collusion**. As the discussions of Lee Yong-uk’s framework and the WE/resonant subject repeatedly emphasize, the conditions of forming a relationship may be collective, while the attribution of responsibility remains asymmetrical. The power to design and operate the system, set thresholds, and order evacuations is concentrated in institutions, while the bodies and lives exposed to risk belong to residents. The corrections made by field workers are functionally indispensable but institutionally marginalized. When their judgments are not entered into official records, compensated, or converted into decision-making authority, “joint constitution” degenerates into an exploitative concealment in which shadow labor is rendered invisible.

The normative implications of eco-digitality emerge precisely here: if joint constitution is not to remain an empty declaration, the feedback loop of situated judgment must be **institutionalized through the design of conditions**. Examples include recalibrating thresholds to reflect feedback, establishing official channels for residents’ experiential knowledge, and pluralizing alerts through numerical warnings as well as human networks.

## Ⅳ. Synthesis: The Two Questions as Two Sides of One Event

The two questions converge. If Question 1 concerns the conditions under which “whose nature is real,” Question 2 concerns, from an actor-theoretical perspective, “who can redraw the boundaries of that reality.” The agential cut drawn by the flood system—the boundary between the manageable river and the marginalized surplus—hardens into closure without intervention from the field, but it is re-bordered when situated judgments enter the process.

Nature’s reality exists within this process as **an ongoing, never-completed partial constitution**—between the resistance of the real and the arrangement of construction, between Atura’s field of conditions and its ruptures. In ordinary situations, the field of conditions dominates; in moments of crisis—misjudgment, missed detection, or residents’ refusal—the rupture reopens the field of conditions. The latter moment is precisely what theory must capture, and incorporating this rupture into the design of conditions is the practical responsibility of the technogenetic age.

## Ⅴ. Stated Limitations of the Khoj References

- **Document 1 (Atura)**: The conceptual resource of the “prior arrangement of experienceability” has been directly transferred to the flood system, but this is an **interpretive extension**. The original text does not address disaster management cases, and the dynamics of the field of conditions and rupture (Sections 4 and 6) are also described in the context of aesthetic experience. Extending them to include the coercive character of disasters—that is, the question of survival—is a decision made in this analysis.
- **Document 2 (Four-stage topology)**: Reading residents’ evacuation judgments as “relational resonance” is a **metaphorical appropriation**. The original account of resonance presupposes aesthetic and cognitive contact with AI, so the concept has been modified through “recontextualization” in order to account for the distinctive conditions of disasters, including pressure to survive.
- **Document 3 (PLM)**: The ontological position of the “relational emergence of intelligence”—that intelligence is not an internally fixed property but a product of interaction—has been borrowed for the relationship between the system and the field. However, PLM’s context of personalization and the context of hydrological reality do not connect directly.
- **Overall limitation**: The local literature provides ontological and media-aesthetic vocabulary, but it contains no empirical or case-based material on the science and technology studies (STS) of flood prediction. Barad’s agential realism, Latour’s concept of the mediator, and a Heideggerian reading of *Bestand* are **standard theoretical resources introduced additionally** in this analysis. They have been used as supporting axes, with the Lee Yong-uk framework (Atura, WE, and eco-digitality) serving as the primary variables. If this topic is expanded into a paper, empirical verification based on the practical setting of hydrological model thresholds and operational records of early-warning systems will be required.
