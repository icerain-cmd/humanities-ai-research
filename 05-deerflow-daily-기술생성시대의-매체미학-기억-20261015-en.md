# The Dismantling and Reconstruction of "That-Has-Been": Indexicality, Memory, and Temporality in the Media Aesthetics of AI Photo Restoration

## Introduction: Framing the Problem

Services that colorize and restore black-and-white family photos using AI go beyond mere technical convenience; they fundamentally reconfigure the ontological status of photography and the temporality of family memory. Generative models probabilistically fill in colors of clothes, skin texture, background textures, and even the expressions of blurry faces—details that were absent in the original. At this point, Roland Barthes's **"that-has-been (ça a été)"** —the thesis of indexicality asserting that the photograph proves the very presence of the referent, defined in *Camera Lucida* (1980) as the ontological proof of photography—faces a fundamental challenge.

This analysis unfolds around the following question: When a restored photo becomes not a "trace" of the past but a "probabilistic output" of a generative model, does mourning and family memory remain in the temporality of preservation, or do they shift toward the temporality of generation directed toward the future? This question is not merely a critique of technology but is situated at the intersection of the media ontology of photography, the reconstructive nature of memory, and the new temporality created by digital technology.

---

## 1. Barthes's "That-Has-Been" and the Conditions of Indexicality

### 1.1. Barthes's Affirmative Realism

For Barthes, photography is fundamentally "indexical." In Charles Sanders Peirce's semiotic categories, an index is a sign that has a physical, causal connection to its object. Smoke is an index of fire; a footprint is an index of a foot. Analog photography is the result of a causal process where light reflected from the subject chemically alters the silver halide crystals on the film through the lens. Barthes anchors the ontological power of photography in this material chain.

> "What the photograph proves is an immense certainty. In the photograph, I can never deny that the thing was there." (Barthes, 1980, p. 120)

For Barthes, "that-has-been" is not merely a belief but an ontological category. The photograph creates a "weird superimposition" (ibid., p. 23) between "past reality" and "present perception." This is what makes photography a "mad image." As a "messenger" that transmits the light of the past to the present, the photograph gains a special status independent of artistic intention or cultural codes, rooted in its indicative power.

### 1.2. The Material Conditions of Indexicality

However, it is crucial that Barthes's thesis of indexicality depends on **specific material conditions**. In analog photography, indexicality is guaranteed by a triple material chain: (a) the physical reflection of light, (b) the chemical development process, and (c) the physical existence as a print. The transition to digital photography already weakened the first link. Digital image sensors convert light into electrical signals, which are then stored as discrete pixel data. At this point, Barthes's indexicality has already shifted to a "weak indexicality" (Manovich, 2001; Gunning, 2004).

AI restoration technology completely ruptures this weakened indexicality. The restoration process is no longer about "preserving" or "amplifying" the physical traces of the original; it is about **probabilistically generating** information that "did not exist" in the original.

## 2. The Technical Structure of AI Restoration: Restoration as Probabilistic Generation

### 2.1. The Operating Principle of Generative Models

Current AI photo restoration services (Remini, MyHeritage, VanceAI, etc.) are based on GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks) or diffusion models. These models "infer" a "plausible" complete image from a damaged input image based on patterns learned from large datasets. Restoration is therefore **generation, not reconstruction**.

In this process, the model performs the following tasks:
- Assigning color to black-and-white images: Allocating the "most probable" color to each pixel based on the statistical distribution of the training data.
- Restoring damaged areas: "Hallucinating" missing information through relationships with neighboring pixels and learned pattern matching.
- Super-resolution: "Synthesizing" high-frequency details from low-resolution inputs.
- Face restoration: "Mapping" the closest candidate from the learned face distribution onto blurry face regions.

The key point is that all these decisions are **probabilistic**. Different restoration results can be produced from the same original image, meaning the model does not reproduce "what was" but generates "what could have been."

### 2.2. "This might have been": From Indexicality to Possibility

An analysis found in web search results summarizes this shift as follows:

> "Barthes's punctum reappears here as a site of interruption, a point where the formal coherence of the image collapses and the generative process beneath it is revealed. The unintended face in an AI image reveals the limits of the system's own perception. It is the result of the algorithm attempting to 'imagine' what cannot be reproduced from the dataset, a computational echo of what Barthes called 'that-has-been (ça a été)'—now transformed into 'that-might-have-been.'"

The philosophical implication of this transformation is profound. Barthes's indexicality was a matter of the **aspect of being**. The photograph testified to the fact that "the object was there." What the restored AI image presents is not a mode of being but a **mode of possibility**: "It could have been this way"—or more precisely, "This was the most probable way it looked"—a statistical estimation.

This means the ontological axis of photography has shifted from **index → probability**. The photograph is no longer a "trace" of the past but becomes the "most probable narrative" of the present about the past.

## 3. The Reconstructive Nature of Memory and the Complicity of AI Restoration

### 3.1. Halbwachs and Bartlett: The Reconstructive Nature of Memory

This change cannot be viewed solely as a "loss" for photography because memory itself is inherently reconstructive. Maurice Halbwachs's theory of collective memory holds that memory is not fixed in an individual's psychological repository but is reconstructed anew each time through social interaction. In *The Collective Memory* (1950), Halbwachs argues that "memory is not a reproduction of the past but a reconstruction based on the needs of the present."

Frederic Bartlett's *Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social Psychology* (1932) experimentally demonstrated this reconstructive nature. Subjects transformed the content of original stories to fit their own cultural schemas each time they recalled them. For Bartlett, remembering is not reproduction but **reconstructive construction**.

From this perspective, AI restoration is not an entirely new phenomenon but can be seen as the **externalization** and **acceleration** of memory's inherent reconstructive nature through digital technology. When a family looks at an album and says, "Remember Grandma's green dress?" they are already engaging in a reconstructive agreement about the past. AI restoration replaces this reconstructive process with the language of "mechanical probability."

### 3.2. The Difference: Externalization and Delegation

However, there is a decisive difference. Human reconstructive memory operates within **intersubjective verification of the community**. There exists a feedback loop of conversation among family members, negotiation of conflicting memories, and cross-verification with the original photograph. AI restoration short-circuits this process. The moment the "gap" between the original and the restored version disappears, and the generated image replaces the original, the reconstruction process is **delegated** to the model's statistical output.

This is not merely a technical issue but an **epistemological and ontological delegation**. The "authority" over what the family's past was shifts from the interaction of family members to the AI model. The restored image enforces the belief that "this is how it must have been originally"—especially when the original no longer exists or is too damaged to be comfortably viewed.

## 4. Derrida and the Inversion of Temporality: From Preservation to Generation

### 4.1. Archive Fever and the Forgetting of the Future

In *Archive Fever* (Mal d'Archive, 1995), Jacques Derrida insightfully argues that the archive is not merely a device for preserving the past but a **structure of forgetting directed toward the future**. The technical structure of the archive determines what will be remembered, which inevitably means that something will be forgotten. Crucially, Derrida sees this forgetting not as mere loss but as the **setting of conditions**:

> "The question of the archive is not a question of the past. It is a question of the future." (Derrida, 1995)

AI photo restoration is the purest implementation of this Derridean insight. Restoration technology claims to "preserve" the past, but in reality, it determines **what form the past will be remembered in** under current technical conditions. The specific skin tones, color palettes, and textures included in the restoration model's training dataset generate a "normalized" version of family memory. Colors not chosen, textures not generated, expressions not reproduced are forgotten as if they never existed.

### 4.2. Freud's Nachträglichkeit (Deferred Action): Reversible Temporality

Freud's concept of *Nachträglichkeit* (deferred action, retrospective attribution) provides a key insight for understanding the temporality of AI restoration. According to this concept, a past event does not become a "trauma" or a "meaningful event" at the moment it occurs, but only later, when another event retroactively attributes meaning to it. The past is not given; it is "retrospectively" constructed later.

The temporality of AI photo restoration can be read as a technical implementation of *Nachträglichkeit*. The original black-and-white photo is, in itself, merely an incomplete latent potential. The moment of colorization/restoration—i.e., the present technical act—finally bestows a "complete form" upon the past. This demonstrates an inversion of temporality, where the past is retrospectively constructed by the present.

However, a crucial divergence occurs here. In *Nachträglichkeit*, the reconstruction of the past is an **immanent, psychological** process of the subject. In contrast, in AI restoration, the reconstruction is a **mechanical, statistical** process, and the subject remains a recipient. This difference is the decisive point that separates the temporality of preservation from the temporality of generation.

## 5. The Fate of the Punctum: What Happens Where the Index Dies

### 5.1. The Paradox of the Generated Punctum

For Barthes, the *punctum* is a "pricking wound." It arises from the contingency of a detail that breaks the *studium* (general cultural and historical interest) and directly pierces the viewer. Crucially, this *punctum* is inseparable from the indexicality of "that-has-been." The *punctum* pierces us because it is both **accidentally captured** and **actually existed**. The "beautiful details" generated by AI have lost this indexical foundation. They are not "accidents" but the model's statistical outputs.

Nevertheless, paradoxically, AI-restored images generate a new kind of *punctum*. As seen in user experience studies and online cases (e.g., sharing MyHeritage's Deep Nostalgia™ feature), AI-restored photos evoke powerful emotional responses. Especially when the face of a deceased person is vividly reproduced and a vaguely remembered background becomes concrete, users report being "moved to tears" or feeling "as if they have traveled back in time."

This emotional response is structurally similar to Barthes's *punctum* but has a different ontological basis. If Barthes was "pierced" because he knew the details in the photo **actually existed**, the user in AI restoration is "pierced" because the image **matches the reconstruction of old memories**. That is, when the color generated by AI meets the family's reconstructive memory—"Yes, Grandma did wear that kind of dress"—the emotional impact arises not from the proof of indexicality but from the **confirmation** of memory.

### 5.2. The Transition of the Punctum: From Evidence of Existence to Event of Interpretation

This analysis suggests that the ontological status of the *punctum* is changing. As one online analysis points out:

> "The punctum survives not as an index of the real but as an event of interpretation: at the very moment meaning stumbles, when something else asserts itself between the human and the machine."

The *punctum* moves from the realm of indexical fidelity to the realm of **generative affect**. This is why restored photos can still produce emotional effects, and also why the ethical status of that emotion must be questioned.

## 6. Benjamin's Aura and Generative Reproduction: The Difference Between Reproduction and Generation

### 6.1. Benjamin's Framework and Its Difference

In *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1936), Walter Benjamin argued that reproduction technology destroys the aura of the original work—its uniqueness, authenticity, and distance. Photography and film, by producing "copies" that exist without an original, replaced the cult value of art with exhibition value.

However, AI image restoration is fundamentally different from the problematic Benjamin envisioned. Benjamin's reproduction was the **reproduction of an already existing original**. A reproduced photograph conveyed the same content as the original in a different material form. In contrast, AI restoration is the **generation of information that was not in the original**. If reproduction was a matter of "copying," AI restoration is a matter of **generation**.

This difference is not trivial. What Benjamin saw through the destruction of aura was the potential for the politicization of art. What AI restoration raises is an identity crisis of memory itself. Just as the distinction between original and copy once became problematic, now the distinction between **generated memory and experienced past** becomes the issue.

### 6.2. The Emergence of the Generated Original

On a more fundamental level, AI restoration creates a paradoxical situation: the "generation of the original." The restored image is not a "copy" of a scene that actually existed. It is a "visual suggestion probabilistically output by the AI model," and this forms the "original memory" in the user's mind. In other words, the AI restoration becomes the **visual original for the memory of an unexperienced past**.

This recalls what Jacques Rancière called a "change in the regime of representation." For Rancière, a change in the artistic regime is not merely a change in style but a change in the "distribution of the sensible (partage du sensible)" itself—what can be seen and what can be said. AI restoration changes the very conditions of "what can be seen of the past" in the realm of family memory. The past is no longer "discovered" but "generated."

## 7. The Temporality of Mourning: From the Temporality of Preservation to the Temporality of Generation

### 7.1. An Extension of Digital VR Reunion/Memorial Services

AI photo restoration is part of a broader digital memorial ecosystem. VR reunions recreating the face and voice of the deceased (e.g., MBC's documentary "You Met Me"), automatic "memory" slideshows on smartphones (Google Photos, Apple Memories, etc.)—all these technologies reconfigure the temporality of mourning. Traditional mourning involves a dual movement: **acknowledgment of loss** and **internalization of the object in memory**. As Freud defined in *Mourning and Melancholia* (1917), healthy mourning is a process of internalizing the lost object and transferring libido to new objects.

However, AI restoration and digital memorial technologies replace this internalization process with **externalization** and **visualization**. The deceased no longer exists only in memory but is vividly reproduced on screen. This can be a tool that supports the mourning process, but it can also become a device that indefinitely postpones mourning.

### 7.2. The Temporality of Preservation

The temporality of traditional photography is the **temporality of preservation**. The photograph preserves the past, but at the same time, it confirms that the past is past. What Barthes felt in the childhood photo of his mother was a dual sense of time: "She was there—now she is no more." Preservation presupposes loss, and mourning is the process of accepting this loss.

In this temporality of preservation, the past is a **definitive perfect tense**. What happened, how it looked, is already decided, and the photograph testifies to that definitiveness. Of course, as Halbwachs points out, human memory reconstructs this definitiveness, but the original photograph as an "indexical anchor" limits the scope of reconstruction.

### 7.3. The Temporality of Generation

What AI restoration introduces is the **temporality of generation**. The past is no longer a perfect tense but becomes a **future perfect tense**: "This will have been this way." The past created by the generative model is retroactively determined by the present's statistical estimation.

Key features of this temporality of generation are **reversibility** and **plurality**. Multiple different pasts can be generated from the same original. And these generated pasts are selected according to the present emotional needs. This means the process of mourning shifts from the confirmation of loss to a matter of **choice**.

### 7.4. Diagnosis of the Transition: Coexistence of Two Temporalities

However, the dichotomous nature of the question—"Is it the temporality of preservation or the temporality of generation?"—risks oversimplifying the actual situation. What is observed in the practice of AI restoration technology is a **complex coexistence** of the two temporalities. That is:

(a) Users experience the restored image as an "improvement" of the original, still maintaining the illusion of indexicality. The temporality of preservation operates in expressions like "This is the original color."

(b) At the same time, users are aware that multiple versions of the same image can be generated, and they **consciously accept** this generative nature. The temporality of generation is revealed in expressions like "This color is prettier, but I don't know if it's accurate."

(c) The most interesting point is the potential emergence of a **third temporality**. This temporality could be called neither preservation nor generation, but rather the temporality of **co-construction**. In this temporality, the past becomes a complex construct co-created by the traces of the original, the probabilistic generation of AI, and the reconstructive memory of humans.

## 8. Conclusion: Photography and the Ethics of Memory After Indexicality

### 8.1. The Change in the Ontological Status of Photography

AI photo restoration demonstrates that Barthes's "that-has-been" can no longer function as the ontological proof of photography. The ontological axis of photography has shifted from **index → probability → generation**. This change is not the "death" of photography but an **ontological redefinition** of photography.

The photograph is no longer a trace of the past but becomes a probabilistic narrative about the past. This means that while photography is freed from its "causal connection to reality," it simultaneously enters the realm of "generative responsibility."

### 8.2. The Ethics of Memory: How to Handle the Generated Past

The core question raised by this change is the **ethics of memory**. When the past generated by AI replaces the original, or becomes the standard for a "clearer" memory than the original, we face the following practical and ethical challenges:

**Transparency**: It must be made explicit how the restored image differs from the original, what was generated and what was preserved. Current commercial services do not provide this information.

**Plurality**: Instead of a single "optimal" restoration result, interfaces that allow multiple possible pasts to be juxtaposed are needed. Memory must be plural, not singular.

**Preservation of the Original**: Institutional and technical safeguards must be established to prevent the restoration from completely replacing the original. The restoration should serve as an "annotation" of the original, not erase the original itself.

**Critical Literacy**: User education is necessary. A critical media literacy is required to recognize that AI-restored images are not "the past itself" but "the present's estimation of the past."

### 8.3. Final Judgment

The answer to the core question—"Does mourning and family memory remain in the temporality of preservation, or do they shift toward the temporality of generation?"—must be **not a binary choice but a dialectical synthesis**. AI restoration technology does not completely abolish the temporality of preservation. The traces of the original (facial contours, posture, composition) still belong to the temporality of preservation and serve as the "anchor" for the restoration process. At the same time, the colors, textures, and details added by the generative model introduce the temporality of generation.

Therefore, the AI-restored image is a complex construct where **a triple temporality of past-present-future** intersects. The traces of the past (analog remnants), the technology of the present (generative model), and the expectations of the future (the way the reproduced past will be remembered) coexist within a single image. Within this triple temporality, the process of mourning is redefined not as simple "preservation" nor complete "generation," but as a temporality of **co-construction** that negotiates between trace and generation.

This is the sharpest challenge AI restoration technology poses to Barthes's ontology of photography, and at the same time, its most productive extension. "That-has-been (ça a été)" is no longer a valid ontological category, but instead, the ontology of photography is opening new horizons within the tension between **"that-could-have-been (ça aurait pu être)"** and **"that-will-have-been (ça aura été)"**.
