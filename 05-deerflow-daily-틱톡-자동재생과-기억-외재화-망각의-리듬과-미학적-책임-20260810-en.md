# Media Aesthetics in the Age of Technogenesis: TikTok's Auto-Play, the Rhythm of Memory and Forgetting, and the Attribution of Aesthetic Responsibility

## 1. Introduction: The Temporality of Platform Capitalism and the War for Attention

The auto-play and recommendation feed of short-form video platforms like TikTok have fundamentally changed how attention operates in the digital media environment. While a user watches one video, the platform is already buffering the next, predictively arranging future viewing experiences even as the user's gaze lingers on the present. This 'predictive arrangement' goes beyond mere technical convenience; it functions as a device that reorganizes the very temporal structure of human perception, memory, and aesthetic experience.

This paper analyzes how TikTok's auto-play and recommendation feed produce a rhythm of memory and forgetting for users, centering on Bernard Stiegler's concepts of 'exteriorization of memory' and 'industrialization of attention,' as well as Vilém Flusser's concepts of 'technical images' and the 'program' of the 'apparatus.' Furthermore, when reading this phenomenon within Flusser's framework as a 'case of technical images programming human choices,' it conceptually examines to what extent the responsibility for aesthetic experience should be traced between user taste and platform prediction.

In this process, we connect Gilbert Simondon's concept of 'concrétisation' with discussions of synthetic data feedback loops and model collapse in generative AI, comparing an evolutionary interpretation of technical objects with an interpretation of regressive closure loops. Ultimately, by summarizing the implications of this analysis for eco-digital or artificial nature research, we aim to illuminate the fundamental questions raised by media aesthetics in the age of technogenesis.

## 2. Stiegler's Exteriorization of Memory and the Production of a Rhythm of Forgetting

### 2.1. Exteriorization of Memory: From Aid to Displacement

In Volume 1 of *Technics and Time*, Stiegler argues that human memory has always been exteriorized through technical media. According to him, memory is not merely an internal mental representation within the brain but is stored and transmitted in material supports as 'tertiary memory'—writing, tools, images, databases, etc. This tertiary memory enables the preservation of collective memory beyond an individual's lifespan, but simultaneously creates a contradiction: the content and rhythm of memory are determined by the mode of operation of that medium.

The crucial point in Stiegler's argument is that the exteriorization of memory is not simply a 'mnemonic aid' but fundamentally restructures the temporality of the subject. For example, writing fundamentally changed the memory practices of oral cultures, and photography regulated the speed of forgetting by fixing moments of the past. Stiegler sees this process of exteriorization leading to the 'industrialization of attention.' Since the 20th century, the culture industry—especially television and advertising—has commodified public attention and regulated it according to a specific rhythm to promote consumption.

### 2.2. TikTok and the Production of a Rhythm of Forgetting

TikTok's auto-play function radicalizes this trend. While a user watches one video, the platform is already loading the next video's content and immediately selects the next video based on the user's viewing behavior (scroll, like, watch time). What is important here is that even as the user focuses on the current video, the platform is already designed for that video to be 'forgotten.' Auto-play starts the next video immediately upon the end of the current one, allowing the user no 'in-between' (gap). This 'in-between' is typically the temporal interval where lingering appreciation, reflection, or memory consolidation occurs.

From Stiegler's perspective, TikTok's auto-play performs an 'exteriorization of forgetting' beyond the exteriorization of memory. That is, the platform decides for the user what to remember and what to forget. Since the user has no temporal leeway to consciously reflect on the previous video, its content is almost immediately pushed into the realm of forgetting. This is an extreme manifestation of what Stiegler called the 'industrialization of attention.' If television maintained viewers' attention for a set period and regulated rhythm through advertisements, TikTok fragments attention itself into discrete units and forces each unit to forget the previous one.

This rhythm of forgetting is not merely individual. Extending Stiegler's concept of 'tertiary memory,' TikTok's recommendation algorithm accumulates collective viewing data from users, forming an 'external memory store.' However, this store functions not to preserve past experiences but as a 'predictive model' to anticipate and induce future viewing behavior. Therefore, the platform's memory is not oriented toward the past but toward the future, taking on the character of 'preemption.' This preemption is precisely the mechanism that produces the 'rhythm of forgetting.' As the decision over what the user should remember is transferred to the platform's predictive model, the boundary between memory and forgetting is reshaped by the platform's operational logic.

## 3. Flusser's Technical Images and Program: The Programming of Choice

### 3.1. Technical Images and the Program of the Apparatus

In *Into the Universe of Technical Images*, Vilém Flusser argues that modern technical images (photography, film, digital images) are fundamentally different from traditional images (painting, sculpture). Traditional images represent the world through human perception and imagination, but technical images are visualizations of concepts (texts)—that is, 'images of texts.' In particular, digital technical images are generated by computer programs, and these programs function as 'apparatuses' that predefine the possibilities for human choice.

For Flusser, an apparatus is not simply a machine but an 'automated device' operating according to a program. A camera is itself an apparatus; the photographer is compelled to press the shutter button according to the camera's program. The apparatus plays a dual role: it 'enables and simultaneously limits' human choice. Flusser warns here of the risk that human freedom is reduced to 'choice within the apparatus.' That is, humans can only choose from the options the apparatus provides; they cannot change the program of the apparatus itself.

### 3.2. TikTok's Recommendation Feed: A Structure Where Technical Images Program Choice

TikTok's recommendation feed is a vivid example of Flusser's argument. The user appears to choose the next video through a simple gesture—'swipe up'—but in reality, this choice is only possible within the sequence already arranged by the platform's algorithm. With each scroll, the platform predicts the 'next video to watch' based on the user's past behavior data and pre-loads content according to that prediction. In other words, the user's choice is pre-programmed by the platform's prediction.

Here, Flusser's concept of 'technical images' can be applied not to the individual TikTok videos themselves but to the way these videos are arranged—that is, the structure of the 'feed.' Each video is an individual technical image, but more importantly, these images constitute a meta-image: an 'infinitely chained feed.' Within this feed, the user feels as if they are freely choosing, but in reality, they are merely following a flow preset by the platform's program. In Flusser's terms, the user has become a 'functionary' of the apparatus. The functionary participates in the apparatus's operation but does not determine its rules.

In this structure, how is the user's 'taste' formed? In traditional aesthetics, taste was understood as the result of the subject's capacity for judgment—that is, sensory judgment. However, TikTok's recommendation system constructs the user's taste 'retroactively.' When a user likes a video or watches it for a long time, the algorithm interprets that behavior as 'taste' and continues recommending similar content. This creates a circular structure where the user's taste is 'generated' and 'reinforced' by the platform's predictive model. The taste the user 'believes they have chosen' is actually something the platform has suggested.

## 4. Simondon's Concrétisation and the Feedback Loop of Generative AI

### 4.1. Simondon's Concept of Concrétisation and the Evolution of Technical Objects

In *On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects*, Simondon argues that technical objects are not merely tools for use but evolving entities with internal coherence. Technical objects evolve from an 'abstract' stage to a 'concrete' stage, a process where various functions are integrated into a single structure, becoming more efficient and stable. For example, early engines had separate components, but modern engines organically combine parts, reducing functional redundancy and improving performance.

Simondon's concept of concrétisation emphasizes that technical objects undergo a process of 'individuation,' adjusting and developing themselves in relation to their environment, rather than being merely passive tools. In this process, technical objects acquire a unique 'mode of existence' independent of human intention.

### 4.2. Viewing TikTok's Recommendation Algorithm as a Case of Concrétisation

TikTok's recommendation algorithm can be partially understood through Simondon's concept of concrétisation. Early recommendation systems were simple rule-based (e.g., showing popular videos), but current algorithms use deep learning neural networks to integratively process various factors: user behavior patterns, visual features of content, soundtracks, hashtags, etc. This resembles the direction of concrétisation in terms of functional integration.

Furthermore, this algorithm continuously learns and updates through interaction with users. Data from user scrolls, likes, shares, and watch times feeds back to readjust the model. This corresponds to what Simondon called the 'relationship of the technical object with its environment.' That is, TikTok's recommendation system is not merely a fixed program but an 'individuated technical object' evolving through interaction with the user environment.

### 4.3. Synthetic Data Feedback Loops and Model Collapse in Generative AI

However, this evolutionary process carries serious risks. Recent discussions in generative AI research about 'synthetic data feedback loops' and 'model collapse' illustrate this problem well. Model collapse refers to the phenomenon where an AI model, when using its own generated data as training data again, gradually loses diversity and eventually becomes trapped in specific patterns. This occurs because the model fails to reflect the distribution of real data, repeatedly reproducing its own output while accumulating information loss.

A similar feedback loop operates in TikTok's recommendation algorithm. If a user only responds to a certain type of content, the algorithm recommends more of that content, and the user is increasingly exposed to it, biasing their taste. This could be called 'taste model collapse.' The user's taste becomes increasingly narrow, and content is consumed only within a predictable range. This blocks the user's opportunity to discover new things, while simultaneously making the platform more accurate in predicting user behavior.

At this point, Simondon's concept of concrétisation and the feedback loop of generative AI yield contrasting interpretations. One is a positive interpretation: technical objects achieve higher efficiency and integration in their evolutionary process. The other is a critical interpretation: this evolution results in a closed loop that suppresses diversity and creativity.

## 5. Evolution of Technical Objects and Regressive Closure Loops: Comparing Two Interpretations

### 5.1. Evolutionary Interpretation: Algorithmic Advancement as Concrétisation

The first interpretation follows Simondon's concept of concrétisation, viewing TikTok's recommendation system as an 'evolution of technical objects.' From this perspective, the algorithm increasingly integrates user behavior data more sophisticatedly and optimizes itself for better predictions. This is analogous to an engine rearranging its components for more efficient combustion. In this process, the user gains a more personalized experience, and the platform achieves higher user engagement. This interpretation connects to an optimistic discourse that technology makes human life more convenient and enriched.

Moreover, in this interpretation, 'responsibility' between user choice and platform prediction is distributed. The platform merely reflects user preferences, and the user must take responsibility for their own choices. This is based on a traditional liberal concept of the subject.

### 5.2. Regressive Closure Loop Interpretation: The Risk of Programmed Choice

The second interpretation combines Flusser's concept of the apparatus with discussions of model collapse in generative AI, emphasizing that this evolution is actually a 'regressive closure loop.' From this perspective, the advancement of the algorithm proceeds in a direction that increasingly limits the user's possibilities for choice. The more data the user provides to the algorithm, the more accurately the algorithm predicts the user's behavior, and by providing only content that fulfills that prediction, it 'narrows' the user's experience.

This is a typical case of what Flusser warned: 'the program of the apparatus dominates human choice.' It is also a more sophisticated form of Stiegler's 'industrialization of attention,' where not only is the user's attention commodified, but the very direction of that attention is prescribed by the program. In this interpretation, the user does not 'choose' their taste; they merely perform the 'predicted taste' of the platform.

The regressive closure loop interpretation attributes the problem of responsibility to the structural characteristics of the platform's design and algorithm. The problem is the structure itself, which causes the user to unconsciously act according to the platform's program. This suggests that the evolution of technical objects may proceed not simply as an increase in efficiency but in a direction that suppresses human freedom and creativity.

### 5.3. A Synthetic Examination of the Two Interpretations

The two interpretations are not mutually exclusive; in reality, TikTok's recommendation system possesses both aspects. On one hand, the algorithm evolves to more accurately reflect user preferences; on the other hand, that evolution makes the user's experience more predictable and uniform. The important question is not which interpretation is more valid, but how 'aesthetic responsibility' should be defined at the point where these two interpretations coexist.

At this point, we can reference Benjamin's discussion of technical reproduction. In *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction*, Benjamin argued that technical media like photography and film destroyed the 'aura' of traditional art while simultaneously expanding the public's mode of perception and opening new political possibilities. TikTok's auto-play and recommendation feed also have a dual effect. On one hand, they fragment user attention and enforce forgetting; on the other hand, they enable access to diverse content in ways impossible in previous media environments. The problem is that this access is pre-structured by the platform's predictive model.

## 6. Tracing Responsibility for Aesthetic Experience: Taste, Prediction, and the Position of the Subject

### 6.1. Distribution of Responsibility in the Formation of Taste

In aesthetic experience, 'responsibility' generally refers to the ethical and epistemic obligation a subject has regarding their sensory judgments and choices. However, in TikTok's recommendation system, this responsibility is distributed among multiple subject-technical elements. First, the user experiences the illusion of active choice in forming their taste, but in reality, the range of choice is limited by the algorithm's predictions. Second, the platform performs predictions based on user data, but those predictions themselves are not transparent to the user. Third, the algorithm designer designs the system to maximize user engagement, but does not clearly take responsibility for how the user's taste may be distorted as a result.

This distribution of responsibility complicates the question: 'To what extent should the responsibility for aesthetic experience be traced?' If the user simply acted according to the platform's predictions, the user cannot bear full responsibility for their taste. Conversely, if the platform merely reflected user preferences, it is difficult to blame the platform for distorting the user's taste.

### 6.2. Stiegler's 'Industrialization of Attention' and the Attribution of Responsibility

From Stiegler's perspective, this problem connects to the 'destruction of human attentional capacity' brought about by the 'industrialization of attention.' Stiegler argues that modern digital technology reshapes human attention into an extremely short-term and reactive form, causing humans to lose the ability to control and reflect on their own attention. This is not merely an individual problem but a cultural and political one. Stiegler's proposed solution is to build a new form of 'psychic apparatus' through the 're-industrialization of attention.'

In the case of TikTok, this can be interpreted as the need for the user to secure a 'space for play' (espace de jeu) where they can consciously choose their viewing experience without relying on the platform's auto-play and recommendations. However, this space is structurally blocked by the platform's design itself. Auto-play gives the user no time to pause their choices, and recommendations predict the user's choices in advance.

### 6.3. Flusser's 'Apparatus Critique' and the Shift of Responsibility

Flusser offers a more radical solution to this problem. He argues that one must become a 'programmer' who can change the program of the apparatus. That is, one must move beyond simply choosing within the apparatus to a position where one can redesign the apparatus's mode of operation itself. This requires not intervention at the user level but democratic control over the platform's algorithm and interface design.

Applied to the case of TikTok, instead of passively consuming recommendations, the user should have the 'authority' to understand how the recommendation algorithm works and to adjust it as needed. For example, this could involve increasing the transparency of recommendations or providing an interface where users can control how their data is used. However, current platform designs do not offer such possibilities; rather, they keep the user as a 'functionary.'

## 7. Conclusion: Implications for Eco-Digital/Artificial Nature Research

### 7.1. The Tension Between Evolution and Regression of Technical Objects

This paper has analyzed how TikTok's auto-play and recommendation feed go beyond Stiegler's exteriorization of memory to produce a rhythm of forgetting. Viewed through Flusser's concept of technical images, this is a phenomenon where technical images program human choices, raising fundamental questions about the attribution of aesthetic responsibility between user taste and platform prediction.

The analysis reveals a tension between two interpretations: one viewing the evolution of technical objects as concrétisation (increasing integration and efficiency), and the other viewing it as a regressive closure loop (narrowing possibilities and suppressing diversity). This tension is not merely theoretical but has practical implications for how we design and regulate digital platforms.

### 7.2. Toward an Eco-Digital Aesthetics

The findings of this paper suggest that an eco-digital or artificial nature approach to media aesthetics must consider the following: First, the temporal structure of digital platforms—auto-play, infinite scroll, recommendation feeds—is not neutral but actively shapes users' memory, attention, and aesthetic experience. Second, the responsibility for aesthetic experience cannot be fully attributed to either the user or the platform; it is distributed across a network of human and technical actors. Third, addressing the risks of regressive closure loops requires not only user awareness but also structural changes in platform design, including transparency, user control, and democratic governance of algorithms.

Ultimately, the question of aesthetic responsibility in the age of technogenesis is not about finding a single locus of blame but about designing technical objects that support human flourishing, creativity, and critical reflection. This requires moving beyond the current paradigm of attention extraction toward a model of attention cultivation—a re-industrialization of attention that respects the temporal rhythms of memory, forgetting, and aesthetic experience.
