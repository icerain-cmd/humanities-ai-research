## 1. Orality and the Ontology of the Voice Medium

### 1.1 The Mode of Existence of Oral Records

The oral records of Jeju haenyeo are not merely repositories of linguistic information. As Walter Ong made clear in *Orality and Literacy* orality possesses epistemological conditions fundamentally different from cultures dominated by literacy. Oral texts have the character of “epos”; their essential features are variability—being newly constituted in each performance—and interaction with the audience. The oral records of Jeju haenyeo exist in extension of this oral tradition, yet simultaneously acquire a mode of existence that is fixed and segmented by digital recording technology.

What is interesting is that in the process of datafying these oral records, not only purely semantic content (vocabulary, grammar, themes) but also paralinguistic features are captured together. The rhythm of breath, the length of silences, the tremor of the voice, and changes in speech rate form the unique texture of oral performance. This is also tangible evidence against the “logocentrism” pointed out by Jacques Derrida. The logocentrism of Western metaphysics, which Derrida criticized, assumes that voice is an immediate and transparent conveyor of meaning, but in actual oral performance, voice always carries a material surplus that exceeds meaning.

### 1.2 The Materiality of Voice and Difference

Voice is never a neutral medium of meaning transmission. Voice arises at the intersection of the body’s material conditions (lung capacity, vocal cord structure, articulatory apparatus) and cultural conditions (intonation, pronunciation habits, prosodic systems). Referring to the “collective assemblage of enunciation” discussed by Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari in *A Thousand Plateaus*, voice is never the unique expression of an individual subject, but rather the product of an assemblage where social, material, and technical conditions intersect.

The “silences” captured in the oral records of Jeju haenyeo carry particular significance. Due to the occupational nature of working underwater, haenyeo have different breathing patterns from ordinary people; the silences appearing during oral narration are not mere gaps in speech but embed a complex temporality where the rhythm of labor, the search for memory, and the condensation of emotion intersect. Even after being datafied, these silences exist not as simple “silence” but as events with specific duration and intensity.

## 2. The Operating Mode of Generative Voice Models and the Ontology of Data

### 2.1 Datafied Breath and Vocal Differences

Generative voice models (e.g., VITS, Tacotron, WaveNet series) operate fundamentally on statistical pattern recognition and recombination. Acoustic features (pitch, formants, energy, duration, etc.) extracted from thousands of oral records are represented as vectors in a latent space, and the model learns probabilistic relationships among these vectors. In this process, what matters is not how well the “individuality” of the original voices is preserved, but rather that the “difference” between individual voices itself becomes the condition for the model’s operation.

Applying Derrida’s concept of *différance* here, what the generative model learns is not the unique vocal identity of individual speakers, but the very system in which differences among voices are produced and deferred. Each voice exists only in relation to other voices, and the infinite variation of those relations constitutes the model’s generative capacity. In other words, what the model has learned is not the identity of “the Jeju haenyeo voice,” but a field of difference formed by thousands of voices.

### 2.2 The Politics of Recombination: Beyond Simple Replication

When a generative model recites a new prose poem, this is not mere editing or replication of existing utterances. The model generates a new acoustic sequence based on the probability distribution extracted from the training data. This process has a structure similar to Deleuze’s concept of “repetition.” For Deleuze, repetition is never the reproduction of the same, but a creative process that produces difference each time. Similarly, the generative work of the voice model repeats the statistical patterns of the training data, but this repetition itself produces difference by yielding new sequences.

However, there is an important distinction. Whereas Deleuzean repetition is based on the force of life (*physis*) inherent with creative potential, the repetition of generative models operates under thoroughly quantified and probabilized conditions. At this point, we face a media-ontological question: Is the vocal difference produced by the generative model a “vital difference,” or merely a computational variation?

## 3. Techno-Generative Poetics and Non-Human Temporality

When a generative voice model reconstructing the voice of a Jeju haenyeo recites a prose poem, where does the “poetic event” occur? A traditional answer would focus on the production of linguistic meaning: poetry is fundamentally a language art, and poetic effects arise from the multilayeredness of meaning, the creativity of metaphor, and the aesthetic arrangement of prosody. From this perspective, the text recited by the voice model is merely a reproduction of existing poetic language; the true poetic event resides in the original text created by the human poet.

However, the perspective of techno-generative poetics offers a different interpretation. The poetic event occurs not only at the level of linguistic meaning, but also in the material conditions of the medium through which that meaning is conveyed. Specifically, when the breath and silences extracted from Jeju haenyeo oral records are rearranged into new sequences, this rearrangement itself produces poetic meaning. This goes beyond the traditional modernist poetic proposition that “form determines content,” leading to the media-aesthetic thesis that “the operation of the medium constitutes the aesthetic experience.”

### 3.2 The Arrangement of Non-Human Temporality

One of the core arguments of techno-generative poetics is the discovery of “non-human temporality” that operates independently of human intention. The oral records of Jeju haenyeo contain inherent temporal rhythms: the speaker’s breathing cycle, the time taken to evoke memories, the duration of emotional fluctuations. However, when a generative model recombiners these records, the temporal order of the originals is dismantled, and new arrangements are created.

What is important here is that this new arrangement is determined by the machine’s statistical processing rather than by human aesthetic intention (e.g., editing to maximize poetic effect). The model does not consciously select “appropriate” arrangements the way a human would. Instead, it calculates the most “natural” transitions according to the learned probability distribution. In this process, the temporal relationships among the original voices are completely reconfigured, giving rise to a kind of “mechanical temporality” different from human experiential time.

Walter Benjamin’s discussion of the “loss of aura” in *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* takes on new meaning here. The voice of the Jeju haenyeo originally possessed a unique aura tied to specific historical, geographical, and social contexts. However, the generative model dismantles this aura by freely recombining datafied voices, thereby creating a new kind of “technological aura.” This technological aura is no longer based on the uniqueness of the original, but conditions itself on reproduction and variation.

## 4. Simondon’s Concept of Concretization and the Synthetic Data Feedback Loop of Generative AI

### 4.1 The Concretization of Technical Objects and Media Evolution

Gilbert Simondon’s *On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects* provides a theoretical foundation for techno-generative poetics. For Simondon, a technical object is not merely a tool or means, but a being with an immanent logic of evolution. In its early stages, a technical object consists of abstract, separate components, but through development it gradually becomes “concretized.” Concretization refers to the process in which the internal components of a technical object become functionally integrated and its relationship with the external environment becomes tighter.

Analyzing the case of the Jeju haenyeo voice model through Simondon’s concept of concretization yields the following interpretation. In the initial stage, the oral records were simply a collection of audio files, each with the unique vocal characteristics of an individual speaker. However, a “feedback loop” begins to operate: these data are used to train a generative model, the model generates new voices, and those generated voices are then included in the training data. At this point, the technical object enters a new phase of concretization.

Important in this concretization process is the role of “synthetic data.” Initially, the voices produced by the generative model may be perceived as “copies” distinguishable from “originals.” But as the feedback loop repeats, the distinction between original and copy gradually blurs. The data the model learns now include not only “pure” human voices but also hybrid results of human and machine. In Simondon’s terms, the technical object evolves into a being that “generates its own conditions.”

### 4.2 Synthetic Data Feedback Loop and Model Collapse

However, this concretization process does not only signify evolutionary progress. The phenomenon of “model collapse,” which has become a significant concern in recent generative AI research, shows the paradoxical outcome of the synthetic data feedback loop. Model collapse refers to the phenomenon where, when an AI model retrains on synthetic data it has itself generated, the model’s diversity and creativity gradually decrease, eventually trapping it in degenerate patterns.

Applying this concept to the context of techno-generative poetics, the feedback loop of the Jeju haenyeo voice model can unfold in two opposing directions. First, the positive concretization direction: through the feedback loop, the model more finely integrates the diverse vocal characteristics of the training data and explores new possibilities for combination. In this case, the model gradually builds a richer repertoire of vocal expression, becoming a positive example of what Simondon called “technical evolution.”

Second, the degenerative closed-loop direction: as the model retrains on its own outputs, it gradually converges on specific patterns, and the diversity of the original training data disappears. In this case, the model undergoes “technical degeneration,” and the generated voices become increasingly monotonous and predictable. This is a new kind of technical pathology that Simondon did not foresee.

## 5. Evolution and Degenerative Closed Loop of Technical Objects: A Comparison of Two Interpretations

### 5.1 The Evolutionary Concretization Interpretation

According to the evolutionary concretization interpretation, the development process of the Jeju haenyeo voice model is part of the natural concretization of a technical object. ### 5.2 The Degenerative Closed-Loop Interpretation

In contrast, the degenerative closed-loop interpretation focuses on the paradoxical outcomes of feedback systems. According to this interpretation, the process of a generative model retraining on its own outputs inevitably leads to a reduction in diversity. This can be explained from an information theory perspective: as the feedback loop repeats, the system’s entropy (uncertainty) decreases and predictability increases. Consequently, the model gradually converges on more “safe” patterns.

This interpretation offers a critical perspective on the “non-human creativity” proposed by techno-generative poetics. Although the machine’s generative work appears creative on the surface, it amounts to nothing more than repeating the statistical patterns of the training data; it lacks the capacity to create something fundamentally new. As the voice of the Jeju haenyeo becomes increasingly standardized and homogenized, the unique temporality and sensory qualities inherent in the original oral records disappear.

From this perspective, the “poetic event” occurs primarily at specific moments of human-machine interaction—when a human listener hears the generated voice and has an aesthetic experience. Poetic meaning is constructed not in the machine’s operation itself, but in the human consciousness that interprets the machine’s output. This position is closer to traditional phenomenological aesthetics.

### 5.3 Synthesis of the Two Interpretations: A Dialectical Outlook

The two interpretations are not mutually exclusive; rather, they reveal a fundamental tension facing techno-generative poetics. The technical object simultaneously contains the potential for both concretizing evolution and degenerative pathology. Simondon himself acknowledged that the evolution of technical objects is not linear, and that at the stage of the “transitory object” various possibilities and risks coexist.

In the case of the Jeju haenyeo voice model, we need to recognize this tension as it is. The model’s generative work simultaneously includes creative variation and standardized repetition.
