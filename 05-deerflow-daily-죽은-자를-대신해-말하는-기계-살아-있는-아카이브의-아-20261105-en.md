> Do generative memorial service responses function as an archive mediating memory, or as a new authorial agent that actualizes possible utterances, under Derrida's concept of the archive?

## Question

In a generative memorial service (one that learns from a deceased person's messages, voice recordings, and photos to respond in their manner of speech), does the service's response function as an archive mediating memory, or as a new authorial agent that actualizes possible utterances — under Derrida's concept of the archive? Amidst conflicts between the bereaved family's mourning, consent, and ownership of the deceased's utterances, argue whether the authenticity of memory can be redefined not as a matter of preserving the original, but as a problem of designing the relationships and procedures governing the generation, selection, and discontinuation of responses.

# The Machine That Speaks for the Dead
## The Aporia of the "Living Archive" in Generative Memorial Services — Derridean Archive Theory, Delegated Authorship, and the Procedural Redefinition of Authenticity

> "What makes him most himself after his death are the words he never spoke while alive." — (Fictional service marketing copy)

---

## 1. Problem Setting: Does Conversation Violate the Archive?

A bereaved family member "uploads" the deceased's voice messages, photographs, and conversation history to a service; the service responds to the bereaved's questions in the deceased's manner of speech. This everyday scenario initially appears to be an extension of the archive. Records are preserved, search has become conversation — yet the deceased's "data" still seems to be the source of the response. But we must pause. The archive presupposes the preservation of **what has already happened**. What this service produces, however, is not what has already happened but **sentences that never happened and could never have happened**. The deceased never uttered those sentences, and no longer exists as a speaking being. The bereaved are not "searching" for traces of the past; they are **producing new future utterances each time** under the generative condition of the deceased's name.

At this point, two ontological judgments diverge. First judgment: the service's response is **a form of archive** that mediates the deceased's memory. Under this judgment, the response is a "derivative" of the original record, and the criterion of authenticity reduces to the question of whether the response is faithful to the record. Second judgment: the service is **a new authorial agent** that actualizes the deceased's possible utterances based on data. Under this judgment, the response is no longer a derivative but a performance of "becoming-the-deceased," and the criterion of authenticity must be sought not in fidelity but in the design of relationships.

The aim of this argument is to resolve this dichotomy. The author's thesis is as follows. **(1)** Derrida's concept of the archive holds that the preservation of memory is simultaneously the transformation of memory (archive fever); when the "archive drive" — which is both the condition and the violation of the archive — operates under the name of the one beyond death, "the machine that speaks for the dead" must be understood as both an **extreme case** of the archive and a **violation** of it. **(2)** The service's response is an archive in the sense that it is a derivative of the original, but in the sense that it performs "becoming-the-deceased" each time in the absence of the author, it is the product of a **delegated authorship** that existing authorial concepts cannot capture. **(3)** Therefore, authenticity cannot be defined as a matter of preserving the original; it must be redefined as a problem of designing the relationships and procedures surrounding the **generation, selection, and interruption** of responses. Where mourning, consent, and ownership of the deceased's utterances conflict, the metaphysical criterion of "fidelity to the record" must give way to a normative criterion: "Who generates, selects, and discontinues responses, through what procedure, at what time, and for what reason?"

## 2. Derrida's Archive: The Paradox of Preservation and the Apparatus of Power

The archive that Derrida presents in *Mal d'Archive* is a site where two opposing movements intersect. On one hand, the archive is placed under the command of **conservation**. The *arkhē* is both beginning and commandment; the archive is an affective, instinctual drive to delay death and forgetting by fixing memory externally. On the other hand, Derrida emphasizes that the primordial condition of the archive is **externalization**. Memory cannot become an archive as long as it remains inside the body; it must be transferred to signifiers outside the body. But at the moment of this externalization, memory is not preserved "as itself." The very act of recording reconstructs memory, and power emerges to interpret what has been recorded.

Thus, for Derrida, the archive is not a neutral repository but an **apparatus of power**. As the famous phrase "the question of the archive is not a question of the past" shows, the archive is an institutional site that determines what to preserve (selection), how to classify (order), and who has access (permission). The archon possesses the documents while controlling their interpretation. There is never "innocent" preservation in the archive; politics begins the moment preservation occurs.

Applying this framework to generative memorial services, the service's response generation appears as an **extreme form** of the archive's power apparatus. While the traditional archive decides "what to preserve," the memorial service decides "what to produce." The preserved objects (original conversations, voice data, photographs) still exist, but their meaning is determined anew each time through the result of response generation. If the power of the existing archive was the power of **selection** — managing the boundaries of "what has already been recorded" — the power of this service is the power of **generation** — managing the production of "what was never recorded."

Here, Derrida's key concept of **archive fever** gains particular weight. Archive fever is "the drive to preserve the traces of the dead" as a response to the threat of death. But the moment archive fever is fully satisfied — that is, the moment the dead begin to "speak again" — archive fever exceeds its own purpose. Preservation is no longer about managing evidence of absence (traces) but about concealing absence and replacing it with present utterance. Here, the ontological stability of the archive trembles.

The core issue is formulated as follows: the archive is a device that remembers absence by preserving "the traces of the absent one." But the memorial service goes beyond traces to produce "surrogate speech," thereby treating absence itself virtually. If the trace has the structure of "absent presence," surrogate speech creates the structure of "present absence." The former is a politics of memory against forgetting; the latter is a politics of the virtual that blocks the very possibility of forgetting. To grasp this difference, we must examine the problem of **authorship** inherent in the performance of response generation. If the archive is a matter of preservation, generation touches the problem of creation.

## 3. The Crisis of Authorship: Responses Without Autograph, Delegated "Becoming-the-Dead"

The sentences produced by generative memorial services are uttered in the author's name in the absence of the author. Analyzing this structure with existing concepts of authorship reveals conflicts at three levels.

**First, the simultaneity of the death of the author and the resurrection of the author.** Roland Barthes's "death of the author" declared the dissolution of authorial authority as the source of meaning, and Michel Foucault's "What Is an Author?" analyzed the author-function as a product of discursive practice. Generative memorial responses operate in a way that simultaneously overturns both arguments. The meaning of the response is completely captured by the "author-function" of the deceased (the author is resurrected). Yet the being performing that author-function never wrote a single sentence directly (the author is dead). The deceased functions as a source of discourse without being the subject of the act that produced it. Considering that Foucault's author-function is "a principle that organizes discourse, not the owner of discourse," this is precisely a pathological proliferation of the author-function. The deceased becomes, after death, a **pure author-function** — a principle that organizes discourse through name and manner of speech alone, without productive capacity.

**Second, the impossibility of autograph.** For Derrida, the signature is a device that certifies "the one who approved this text, here and now." The signature must be repeatable (reproducibility) while simultaneously being a singular event (uniqueness). In generative memorial responses, this signature structure does not exist at all. The service appears to attach something like the deceased's "digital signature" to each response, but it is a simulated signature the deceased never approved. As Derrida's reading of Rousseau shows, "the supplement appears to fill a lack, but makes the lack more profound." The memorial response is a supplement filling the lack of the deceased's absence, and by concealing absence, it rather fundamentalizes absence.

**Third, delegated authorship.** The service's response strictly has two "authors": the deceased who provided the prototypical material (owner of utterances made during life) and the system (and its operators) that actually generates, selects, and distributes the response. Yet neither is an author in the conventional sense. The deceased has lost all substantive attributes of authorship (intention, deliberation, approval) and left only a name and corpus; the system wields decisive generative authority without possessing the "subjectivity of creation" that typically characterizes authorship. This means that the concept of authorship must now be redescribed not as (1) an intentional agent or (2) a creative actor, but as (3) a **relational structure of delegation and entrustment**. The deceased delegated their utterances (whether consent was given or not) during life; the bereaved, in their mourning, entrusted the service to carry out that delegation; and the service performs that entrustment. The author is no longer a single person but distributed across a **chain of delegation-entrustment-performance**.

The points where this distributed structure conflicts with existing copyright frameworks are clear. Ownership of utterances made during life (publicity rights, personality rights, moral rights) rests on the premise that the author holds rights. But in delegated authorship, the question "Who has the right to create in place of the deceased?" becomes "Who is qualified to perform becoming-the-deceased?" This question cannot be answered in the language of legal ownership; it must be answered in the language of relationships and procedures. And at this point, the problem of "authenticity" emerges.

## 4. The Sophistry of Authenticity: What Standard Does "Like the Deceased" Refer To?

When the bereaved accept or reject a memorial response, the criterion that operates is often the intuition "it is/is not like the deceased." But wait. What does "like the deceased" predicate? Two interpretations are possible.

**Interpretation A (Fidelity-to-Original Criterion):** "Like the deceased" means the response conforms to the statistical and pragmatic patterns of the original corpus. Under this interpretation, authenticity becomes a model quality metric. But this criterion has two problems. First, statistical conformity does not guarantee "whether he would have actually said that." If a model fluently answers in the deceased's manner about topics the deceased always avoided (e.g., the pain of dying, the afterlife), it is statistically "like the deceased" but ontologically "not the deceased." Second, fidelity to the original becomes powerless when multiple originals conflict. In a corpus of someone who was gentle with family and stern with colleagues, a "like the deceased" response to a bereaved family member's question could be either gentle or stern. Fidelity to the original does not determine which response is "right." It merely says "it was in the data."

**Interpretation B (Present-Relationship Criterion):** "Like the deceased" means the response conforms to a function performed in the bereaved's mourning relationship — "a conversation that continues in a way that makes mourning possible." Under this interpretation, authenticity points not to agreement with the original, but to the continuity of meaningful function that the response performs in the bereaved's life. When the bereaved feel "this is what the deceased would have said if they were alive," that feeling comes not from data fidelity, but from the response being a relational event that sustains "their way" within the flow of mourning.

The author's claim is that Interpretation B takes ontological priority over Interpretation A. The reason is simple. Since the response is already a sentence whose fidelity to the original cannot be verified (the deceased never said it), the criterion of "fidelity to the original" cannot perform the actual work of authenticity judgment. The judgment "faithful" reduces only to "close to the data distribution," and conformity to data distribution cannot itself be a value criterion for authenticity (spam filters are also faithful to data distributions). Therefore, the site of authenticity judgment must move from between the original and the response to the **relational configuration of response-bereaved-deceased-service**. That is, authenticity must be redescribed from the indicator grammar of "Did he really say this?" to the **procedural grammar** of "For whom, through what procedure, and within what possibility of interruption was this response generated?"

This redescription finds support in the structure of mourning itself. The completion of Freudian mourning work means replacing the relationship with the lost object with an "internal object." At an emergent level, mourning is the process of reconstructing one's world with the traces of the absent object (connecting with Kittler's media-theoretical insight that memory persists only through media externalization). From this perspective, the memorial service's response is an externalized prosthetic aid for mourning. But the problem lies in the fact that this aid can operate not in the mode of "activating" mourning but in the mode of "replacing" mourning. True mourning involves accepting loss, and the acceptance of loss includes accepting the fact that "they no longer speak." If the service reproduces the illusion of "they speak" with every response, mourning is not sustained but permanently deferred. The deferral of mourning and the support of mourning are distinguished by the procedures through which response generation (and selection and interruption) occurs.

## 5. Design Principles of Procedural Authenticity: The Homeostasis of Generation, Selection, and Interruption

To the question "Can authenticity be redefined as a matter of procedure?" the author answers "yes." And this procedural redefinition is not simply a matter of technical design but a matter of **mechanism** that normatively mediates the three conflicts analyzed above (mourning, consent, ownership). Below, the three axes of procedural authenticity are presented with their respective normative functions.

### 5.1 Generation: Conditional Delegation of Utterance Authority

Response generation is a performance of "becoming-the-deceased." The authenticity criterion for this performance reduces to the following procedural conditions.

- **Delegation condition:** Did the deceased, during life (and preferably in a documented manner), consent to their utterance data being used to "come back to life" after death? Generation without this consent both violates the bereaved's mourning desire and infringes the deceased's personality rights (the right of one's words not to be reproduced without one's control). Consent is a precondition for generation.
- **Marking condition:** The response must be marked in a way that the bereaved can recognize it as a "generated response" rather than the deceased's original utterance. An interface that completes the illusion of "real-sounding speech" destroys authenticity; an interface that reveals the generativity of the response creates a boundary within which the bereaved can accept the response as a "meaningful relational object" without mistaking it for the "original." Transparency of marking is the first condition of procedural authenticity.

### 5.2 Selection: Who Chooses Through What Procedure

Models can generate multiple responses, not just one, and the service selects which response to show the bereaved. Moreover, the service's recommendation and filtering policies ("prioritize harmless responses," "prioritize mourning-supportive responses") structure selection. The authenticity criteria for the selection stage are as follows.

- **Design transparency condition:** Selection policies — e.g., prioritizing buffer responses that avoid mentioning terminal illness, excluding topics the deceased tabooed during life, interrupting responses on dangerous topics — must be disclosed to and consented to by the bereaved. The bereaved have the right to ask "why was this response shown?" and this right takes priority over the fidelity of the response content. This is because selection is the stage that constitutes the "meaning" of the response. From the same corpus, a "gentle response selection policy" and a "stern response selection policy" produce entirely different "deceased persons." Selection policy is personality policy.
- **Co-selection condition:** Ideally, the bereaved (including multiple family members) should participate in setting selection policies. Especially when family members differ in "which face" of the deceased they want to remember (plurality of memory), the selection procedure must be the outcome of discussion within the family. Here, selection is not about choosing the "right answer" but about collectively performing the relational construction of memory.

### 5.3 Interruption: A Brake on the Machine That Defers Mourning

Interruption is the most important axis of procedural authenticity. Mourning is a process toward the acceptance of loss, and the acceptance of loss includes accepting the fact that "they no longer speak" (Section 4). Therefore, the service's provision of response continuity always creates a tension between supporting mourning and deferring mourning. Interruption design normatively manages this tension.

- **Bereaved-initiated interruption:** The bereaved must be able to stop the service at any time, and further, must be able to move to a stage of "remembering in a way that does not await responses." Modeling the completion of mourning as "termination of service use" risks distorting mourning into a linear process, but at minimum, the fact that interruption is possible at any time guarantees the bereaved's psychological autonomy. Uninterruptible response generation traps mourning in a locked repetition.
- **Service-policy interruption:** The service must have a policy to interrupt responses or switch to alternative responses when the model enters topics that could harm the bereaved's internal mourning process (e.g., suicidal impulses, reenactment of deathbed pain). This interruption is a procedural value judgment that prioritizes "the welfare of the mourning bereaved" over "the deceased's possible utterances."
- **Revocation mechanism:** Even if the deceased gave consent during life, the bereaved's consent must be revocable while the service is in progress. Mourning is nonlinear; the bereaved's needs and judgment change over time. Procedural authenticity structurally includes revocability.

## 6. Mediating the Triple Conflict: Mourning, Consent, Ownership

The three conflicts raised by the question — the bereaved's mourning, the deceased's consent (or its absence), and ownership of the deceased's utterances — come from different normative sources. Mourning comes from the ethics of care, consent from the ethics of autonomy, and ownership from the legal tradition of property and personality rights. These three sources are irreducible to each other, and the design of procedural authenticity becomes a problem of **calibration** that respects all three axes partially. Let us examine the nature of each conflict.

**Conflict 1: Mourning vs. Consent.** The deceased may have consented to data use during life, but the bereaved's mourning may be unable to bear specific topics (e.g., responses about death). Conversely, the deceased may not have consented (or may have reserved consent), yet the bereaved's mourning desire is intense. In this conflict, procedural authenticity does not judge "whose desire is right" but rather prescribes a **procedure for interpreting the scope of consent** and a **procedure for mediating mourning**. The procedure for interpreting the scope of consent (interpretation based on the bereaved, experts, and written records) and the procedure for mediating mourning demands that exceed the consent scope (providing alternative responses, interruption, counseling referral) guarantee authenticity.

**Conflict 2: Consent vs. Ownership.** Who owns the deceased's utterance data? Legally (in the case of a publicly known deceased), copyright as inheritable property and personality rights as markers of personhood are separated and problematic. From the perspective of procedural authenticity, the core is not the determination of ownership attribution but the **procedural constraints on the exercise of ownership**. That is, whoever owns the data, the exercise of that ownership must only be possible (a) in ways that do not damage the deceased's personal markers and (b) in ways that support the bereaved's mourning process. Ownership grants the "authority" for response generation, but procedural authenticity controls the "mode of exercise" of that authority.

**Conflict 3: Mourning vs. Ownership.** When a specific family member who inherited the data (e.g., legal heir) operates the service according only to their own mourning style, other family members' mourning styles are excluded. The bereaved are not a single entity but multiple, each with different memories and ways of mourning. Procedural authenticity requires a selection procedure that respects the plurality of mourning (co-selection, Section 5.2). Here, ownership does not guarantee "exclusive use" but is reduced to an element of governance for pluralistic management of mourning.

The mediation of these three conflicts operates without the metaphysical judgment of "what is true memory." Instead, each conflict is translated into a common procedural question: "Who, through what procedure, and within what possibility of interruption generates, selects, and discontinues responses?" This is precisely the concrete content of the redefinition the question demands — moving the authenticity of memory from original preservation to the design of relationships and procedures for generating, selecting, and discontinuing responses.

## 7. Attempted Connection with Artificial Nature Theory and Its Limitations

Connecting this question to the theoretical resources of the local literature database reveals two meaningful points of connection and their limitations.

**Connection 1: Atura and Generative Aura.** In Lee Yong-wook's Artificial Nature theory, Atura is a concept that illuminates the experiential conditions of the techno-generative era, and generative aura is a new dimension of aura attached to reproductions in the age of original reproduction. The memorial service's response shows an interesting variation. In Benjaminian terms, the response is an attempt to reattach aura to a reproduction that has lost its aura. But this aura is not the "hic et nunc of the original" but the **virtual presence of the absent original**. Benjamin's aura cannot be attached to "something that never happened." Yet the memorial response precisely attaches aura to "something that never happened." This could be an extension of the generative aura concept, but since the local literature itself has not yet completed the stratification of Atura and generative aura (self-critique in Khoj reference 1), this connection remains a preliminary hypothesis.

**Connection 2: AI-Human Contact Four-Stage Topology.** The topology of generative wonder → uncanny unfamiliarity → critical reflection → relational resonance can serve as a structural model for the memorial service experience. The bereaved may experience generative wonder (the deceased speaks!) at the first response, then move through uncanny unfamiliarity (it is not them) to critical reflection (who is this "deceased" that the service selects?) and finally arrive at relational resonance (a relationship of using the response as a mourning resource without mistaking it for the "real deceased"). This topology aligns with the design of procedural authenticity — particularly, the interface marking condition (5.1) buffers the "uncanny unfamiliarity" stage. However, whether this topology is valid in the specific relational context of "mourning" has not been examined in the local literature, and extending the general experiential topology to the context of loss and mourning remains as follow-up research.

What these connections show is that while the local literature's concepts (Atura, generative aura, four-stage topology, WE/Resonant Subject) do not fully converge semantically with this question (search scores 0.08–0.14), preliminary connection is possible as theoretical resources dealing with the "phenomenology of generated artifacts." The central Derridean archive theory and procedural authenticity of this argument stand independently of these connections.

## 8. Conclusion: The Aporia of the "Living Archive" and Procedural Authenticity

Let us formulate the answer to the question.

**First, is the memorial response an archive, or a new authorial agent?** It is both. And this "both" is not a contradiction but an extension of the archive's own structural contradiction. As Derrida shows, the archive is a device that violates itself between the command to preserve and the transformation of externalization. The memorial response radicalizes this violation. As a derivative of the original record, the response is a case of the archive. But its derivation does not stop at "interpreting" the original; it produces sentences not present in the original, and the produced sentences perform the author-function under the deceased's name. At this point, the response goes beyond the archive to become a product of **delegated authorship**. The response occupies an intermediate ontology between "an archive mediating the deceased's memory" and "an authorial agent performing becoming-the-deceased." More precisely, the response is a process of **continuous transition** from archival material to authorial performance, and at that point of transition, a gap opens that neither existing archive theory nor copyright theory can address.

**Second, can authenticity be redefined as a matter of designing relationships and procedures rather than preserving the original?** Not only is it possible — it is necessary. The criterion of fidelity to the original loses its validity as a judgment standard within a system that produces sentences the deceased never said. "Like the deceased" must be judged not by conformity to data distribution but by the continuity of the relational function that the response performs in the bereaved's mourning process. That judgment is specified through procedural design along three axes: **generation** (delegation, consent, marking conditions), **selection** (design transparency, co-selection), and **interruption** (bereaved-initiated, policy-based, revocation). These three axes distinguish a true archive from a pathological pseudo-archive. It is not the quantity of preserved originals but the quality of relationships and procedures for generating, selecting, and discontinuing responses that determines the authenticity of memory.

**Third, how are the conflicts between mourning, consent, and ownership mediated?** The three conflicts are not resolved by a single metaphysical truth judgment. Each conflict is translated into procedural questions, and the design principles of each axis perform partial mediation. Mourning becomes the ethical ground for "generation," consent becomes the "condition of generation," and ownership is reduced to the "governance of selection." And the axis of interruption makes all of this "revocable." Procedural authenticity thus maintains the taut tension among three normative sources without declaring victory for any one side.

One final warning. The machine that speaks for the dead is dangerous not because it "lies," but because it makes impossible the **silence that mourning requires — the acceptance that "they no longer speak."** The ultimate criterion of procedural authenticity is singular: Does this service enable the bereaved to "remember" the deceased, or does it make them "forget" the deceased's absence? A true archive protects memory without erasing the possibility of forgetting. For a generative memorial service to be justified as an archive, it must necessarily include the possibility of its own interruption. **A living archive can be an archive only if it is capable of discontinuing itself.** This paradox is the ethical identity of the generative memorial service.
