## 2. Flusser’s Distinction: Traditional Images vs. Technical Images

Vilém Flusser (1920–1991) establishes the following distinction in *Für eine Philosophie der Fotografie* (1983, *Towards a Philosophy of Photography*) and *Ins Universum der technischen Bilder* (1985, *Into the Universe of Technical Images*):

| Distinction | Traditional image | Technical image |
|---|---|---|
| Medium | Painting, sculpture, murals | Photography, film, video, computer graphics |
| Mode of production | “Direct” representation through the human hand, eye, and imagination | “Indirect” computation mediated by an apparatus |
| Referent | Points to an “original” in the phenomenal world | Visualization of texts—scientific or mathematical codes |
| Ontological status | Magical representation of the world | Computational projection of texts |
| Critical problem | Idolatry—mistaking the image for reality | Forgetting the textual basis of the image, in a form of iconoclasm—the opposite of idolatry |

For Flusser, the key feature of the technical image is that **although it appears like a traditional image on the surface, its generative principle lies in the computational transformation of text, or mathematical code**. Because observers cannot see this computational process, they mistake the image for something “like a window.” This is the focus of Flusser’s critique.

## 3. The Ontological Status of Generative AI and the Applicability of Flusser’s Distinction

### 3.1. Points of Application (Validity)

Flusser’s framework applies **powerfully** to generative AI in the following respects:

**(a) Prompt-to-image transformation = computational projection from text to image**

A diffusion model generates an image by conditioning a latent space on a text prompt. This corresponds precisely to the structure described by Flusser, in which “text operates beneath the surface of the technical image.”

> “Beneath the surface of technical images lies a complex network of scientific texts. These texts make the images possible, while at the same time being concealed by them.” (Flusser, 1985, *Into the Universe of Technical Images*)

When a generative AI user enters a prompt, they are not “drawing” an image but **specifying mathematical conditions**. This is analogous to Flusser’s characterization of the photographer as a “functionary.”

**(b) The problem of the loss of a “critical topology”**

What Flusser most strongly warns against is the tendency to forget that a technical image is the “result of a calculation based on texts,” and to consume it as though it were a natural window onto reality. The surreal images produced by generative AI—photorealistic scenes that nevertheless do not exist—represent a **radicalized form** of this problem. Such images are no longer either representations of reality or simple visualizations of texts; they are products of sampling from statistical probability distributions.

**(c) Expansion of the concept of the “apparatus”**

For Flusser, the *Apparat* is not merely a machine but a “black box whose function is determined by a program.” The transformer-plus-diffusion architecture of generative AI is a **perfect realization** of this concept. Users control only the input—the prompt—and the output—the image—without knowing the internal algorithms.

### 3.2. Points That Do Not Apply or Require Expansion (Limitations)

However, Flusser’s framework alone cannot capture the following aspects:

**(a) The indeterminacy of the diffusion process—“generation from noise”**

Flusser’s technical image fundamentally presupposes the **execution of a deterministic program**. A photograph is the result of a mechanical and chemical process carried out by a camera. By contrast, the diffusion process in generative AI takes **stochastic sampling** as its central mechanism. The “indeterminacy” whereby a different image is generated each time even from the same prompt must be introduced as a new element into Flusser’s framework.

**(b) The disappearance of the concept of the “original”**

Flusser still retains the idea of an original in the form of the “object of photography.” Although the technical image distorts or transforms this original, the existence of the original itself remains presupposed. Generative AI, by contrast, **creates entities that do not exist in reality on the basis of statistical corpora alone**. This requires a new ontological status that moves beyond Flusser’s schema of “original and representation.”

**(c) The status of the prompt—“a condition that is neither text nor image”**

In Flusser’s binary distinction between traditional and technical images, text is limited to **scientific codes—mathematics and physics**. Generative AI prompts, however, are “semiotic conditions” written in natural language. A prompt is neither an image, a program, nor a mathematical formula; it has a third ontological status. This constitutes a new domain that cannot be fully captured by Flusser’s binary framework.

**In conclusion**: Flusser’s distinction between “traditional images” and “technical images” remains **a valid starting point** for analyzing the ontology of images and texts generated by AI. In particular, the insight that “text operates beneath the surface of the image” intuitively captures the core of the prompt-to-diffusion process. However, this framework is not sufficient on its own and requires the following theoretical supplements:

1. **Diffusion ontology** — a concept capable of capturing generation from noise, stochastic indeterminacy, and the topology of latent space
2. **The semiotics of the prompt** — an analysis of the transformation through which a natural-language prompt becomes a condition for an image
3. **The concept of the statistical original** — a new paradigm that treats a statistical distribution within a corpus as an “original”
