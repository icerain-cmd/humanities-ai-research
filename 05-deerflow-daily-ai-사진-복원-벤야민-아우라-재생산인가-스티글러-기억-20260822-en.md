## 1. Benjamin's Aura: Disappearance or Transformation?

### 1.1. Logic of the Original Text

For Benjamin, aura is tied to the artwork's **"unique existence" (einmaliges Dasein)** and its **"spatiotemporal uniqueness."** Technical reproduction (photography, film) separates this existence from the realm of tradition (Tradition) and cult value (Kultwert). "Even the most perfect reproduction of a work of art is lacking in one element: its presence in time and space, its unique existence at the place where it happens to be."

The crucial point here is that **Benjamin neither celebrated nor simply lamented the disappearance of the aura.** Rather, he saw reproductive technology as a dialectical moment enabling the political awakening of the masses through exhibition value (Ausstellungswert). The disappearance of the aura is both a loss and a liberation.

### 1.2. New Conditions Posed by Generative AI Restoration

However, generative AI-based photo restoration fundamentally differs from the technical reproduction (photography as a mechanical, photochemical process) that Benjamin posited:

| Category | Technical Reproduction (Photography) | Generative AI Restoration |
|----------|--------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Mode of Operation** | Photochemical, optical transcription (copy) | Probabilistic generation (synthesis) |
| **Relationship to Original** | Partial, selective reproduction of the original | Supplementation of details not in the original |
| **Status of Lack** | The copy lacks the original's 'here and now' | The restored image produces 'what might have been' |
| **Truth Value** | Indexicality — causal connection to the object | Probability — statistical fit to the training distribution |

This difference is decisive. **If technical reproduction makes us experience lack through its difference from the original, generative restoration erases lack itself and produces a complete image.** The restored photo does not 'repair' the damaged points of the original; rather, it fills the empty spaces by generating the **most probable details** according to the statistical distribution of the training data.

## 2. Core Thesis: Not the Disappearance of Aura, but the Substitution of Aura

### 2.1. The Creative Function of 'Hallucination'

The question asks whether the model's hallucination can be seen as **producing a new aura.** I answer **affirmatively, but on the condition of redefinition.**

In the context of photo restoration, the generative model's hallucination (considered an error in natural language processing) produces a new aura in the following ways:

1.  **Destruction of Indexicality**: The original photo carries the index of what Barthes called *ça-a-été* (the 'that-has-been') — the physical evidence that light touched the film. Generative restoration severs this indexical connection and replaces it with statistical probability.

2.  **Construction of Retroactive Presence**: The restored image does not show a scene that 'actually existed' in the past, but rather a scene that the present's statistics judge as 'what the past must have been like.' This is a **construction of the present retrojected onto the past.**

3.  **Condition for a New Aura**: This new aura is not based on the original's unique existence (spatiotemporal uniqueness), but on the **"sensation that a lost past has been recovered"** — an affective presence arising from the very experience of recovery. In other words:

> **Aura of the original photo = 'That was once there'**
> **Aura of the restored image = 'That must have been like this'**

### 2.2. The Dialectic of Aura Substitution

This is not a simple restoration of the aura. Rather, the restored image simultaneously produces two opposing effects:

**Positive Effects:**
- Allows the user to 'again' see the contours of a face they could no longer see
- Reconstructs a damaged page of a family album into a 'complete' scene of memory
- Enables visual contact (albeit synthetic) with a lost person

**Negative Effects:**
- Erases the **material evidence of time** inherent in the original photo's physical damage (stains, cracks, fading)
- Replaces the **active work of imagination** where the user filled in the blur with their own memories
- Suture the boundary between 'what was' and 'what might have been,' leading the user to believe the restored result is equivalent to the original

It is precisely because of this duality that I propose calling this phenomenon **'aura substitution.'** Neither disappearance nor recovery, but an aura with a completely different origin (the original's presence → statistical probability) replacing the previous aura.

## 3. Stiegler's Tertiary Memory: Medium for Mourning or Device for Datafication?

### 3.1. Stiegler's Pharmacological Frame

Stiegler extends Husserl's primary memory (immediate retention of perception) and secondary memory (recollection, representation) to conceptualize **tertiary memory (retention tertiaire)** stored in technical external media. Tertiary memory is not simply a repository of memory, but a **technical environment that transforms the very conditions of memory.** And this technical environment always possesses the duality of the **pharmakon** — simultaneously poison and cure.

Applying this frame to photo restoration apps:

- **The Poison Aspect**: The restored image replaces the incompleteness of the original, **shortening the mourning work** where the user confronts loss. Freud's mourning is a painful process of relinquishing the lost object through reality-testing each memory one by one. The restored, complete image allows bypassing this process.

- **The Cure Aspect**: By allowing the user to 'see again' the face of a family member they can no longer remember clearly, the restored image enables **the transmission and intergenerational sharing of memory.** This could be an instance of what Stiegler calls positive pharmacology (positive addiction).

### 3.2. Specific Criteria Distinguishing the Two Phases

The core of the question lies in the specific criteria for **what makes this restored image a medium for mourning and memory, and what makes it a platform's datafication device.** I propose the following six criteria:

---

#### Criterion 1: Temporal Directionality of the Image

| Medium for Mourning/Memory | Datafication Device |
|----------------------------|---------------------|
| The image is **oriented toward the past** — the user's lived memory verifies and supplements the restoration result | The image is **oriented toward the future** — the restoration result becomes input for predicting user profiles, kinship networks, and consumption patterns |
| The restored image prompts a memory verification question: "Was it like this?" | The restored image generates recommendations for "other users with similar family photos" |
| **Memory verifies the image** | **The image produces data points** |

This criterion focuses not on the ultimate use of the image, but on the **direction of the temporal relationship** the image establishes with the user. The same image can have its temporal direction reversed when stored in a family album versus uploaded to social media.

---

#### Criterion 2: User's Epistemic Stance Toward Hallucination

| Medium for Mourning/Memory | Datafication Device |
|----------------------------|---------------------|
| The user knows the restored details (eye color, wrinkles, floral patterns in the background) are **probabilistic products** but accepts them emotionally — "It might have been different, but it's nice to see it this way" | The user believes the restoration result is the **original that actually existed** — the interface obscures the probabilistic nature of generation through language like "restored" |
| **'As if' attitude** — affection maintaining critical distance | **'Was' attitude** — disappearance of critical distance |
| Similar to Barthes' *punctum* experience — the image pierces me, but I know it is constructed | This is the illusion of image transparency — believing the image shows the past without any mediation |

The decisive factor here is the language of the interface. Expressions like "we improved your photo" or "restored to sharpness" hide the probabilistic nature of generation and reinforce the illusion of technical transparency. If the interface said **"we redrew it"** or **"we probabilistically filled in missing parts,"** the user's epistemic stance would be different.

---

#### Criterion 3: Platform's Data Regime

| Medium for Mourning/Memory | Datafication Device |
|----------------------------|---------------------|
| The uploaded original image is **deleted immediately after processing** or processed only on-device | The uploaded image is **preserved on the server** for model training, personalization, and profiling |
| The terms of service specify the scope of data use and guarantee the **right to deletion** | The terms of service justify data collection with vague phrases like "using images for service improvement" |
| **Facial data from the photo is not absorbed into model parameters** | Faces from the photo **re-enter the training data of the generative model**, influencing restoration results for other users |

This is the most objectively verifiable criterion. Through auditing the terms of service and data retention policies, one can determine which side a specific app falls on.

---

#### Criterion 4: Epistemic Transparency of the Interface

| Medium for Mourning/Memory | Datafication Device |
|----------------------------|---------------------|
| **Simultaneous** before-and-after comparison is possible — the user can always see what is original and what was added | A smooth transition effect **presents only the final restoration result** |
| Restored areas are **visually marked** — e.g., dotted lines, transparency differences, color distinctions for probabilistically generated parts | The **distinction between restored and original areas disappears** — the image is presented as a continuous whole |
| The user is offered **multiple versions of the generated result** — an option to "see other possibilities" | The user is offered only a **single result** — presented as if there is a single correct answer |

The interface is the most direct device shaping the user's epistemic stance. An interface that **visualizes the restoration process** presents the generated result as conditional and probabilistic, while an interface that **makes the restoration process transparent (invisible)** presents the result as absolute and factual.

**※ Haraway's Point of Intervention**: Donna Haraway's "Situated Knowledges" argues that all visual representations are constructed from a specific position, through specific devices, for specific purposes. Whether the restoration app's interface visualizes or obscures the restoration process is directly linked to the issue of **observer responsibility.** A transparent interface allows the user to **recognize that their visual representation is constructed**, realizing Haraway's ethics of 'partial visibility.' An opaque interface reproduces the 'God trick' — the illusion of a universal observer located nowhere.

---

#### Criterion 5: Social Context of Use

| Medium for Mourning/Memory | Datafication Device |
|----------------------------|---------------------|
| The restored photo is used in the **private sphere** — family albums, personal devices, small family chat rooms | The restored photo circulates in the **public, algorithmic sphere** — social media feeds, platform galleries, community sharing |
| The context of use is **commemorative** — tied to specific anniversaries, family events, memorials | The context of use is **consumptive** — a cycle of 'likes,' shares, comments, and algorithmic recommendations |
| **Integrated into memory rituals** | **Functions as a data point in the engagement economy** |

---

#### Criterion 6: Status of the Original

| Medium for Mourning/Memory | Datafication Device |
|----------------------------|---------------------|
| The original blurry photo is **preserved** — the restored image exists as its attachment | The original is **discarded/replaced** — only the restored image remains, and access to the original is lost |
| The blur and damage are valued as **material traces of time** | The blur and damage are treated only as **noise to be removed** |

**※ Why This Criterion is Most Important**: Earlier, I argued that "blur is aura." The photochemical damage, stains, and fading of the original photo are not mere defects, but **material traces of time.** As Barthes said in *Camera Lucida*, the *noeme* (essence) of photography is the ontological proof that 'that-has-been.' This proof of existence is further strengthened through the passage of time — through the photo's fading, cracking, and staining. **A damaged photo shows that time has passed.**

When generative restoration removes this damage, it removes the evidence of time and places in its stead a 'complete' image without temporality (an image outside the flow of time). This is precisely the point closest to Benjamin's 'disappearance of the aura.' But this disappearance is not due to reproduction, but to **substitution** — replacing the original's temporal traces with statistical completeness.

## 4. Synthesis: The Pharmacological Structure of the Restored Image

### 4.1. Hierarchy of the Six Criteria

The six criteria are not equal. They have the following hierarchy:

```
[Most Objective · Structural]
    ① Platform's Data Regime (Criterion 3) — Objectively verifiable through terms of service and data retention policies
    ② Epistemic Transparency of Interface (Criterion 4) — Verifiable through auditing UI design
    ③ Status of the Original (Criterion 6) — Verifiable through the app's policy on preserving/discarding the original
    ④ User's Stance Toward Hallucination (Criterion 2) — Verifiable through in-depth interviews
    ⑤ Social Context of Use (Criterion 5) — Verifiable through user observation
    ⑥ Temporal Directionality of the Image (Criterion 1) — Requires comprehensive analysis of use context and data flow
[Most Subjective · Phenomenological]
```

The key point is that **Criteria 3, 4, and 6 form the structural conditions, while Criteria 2 and 5 determine the user's experience upon those conditions.** In other words, the data regime and interface design are **determined first**, and upon them, the user's epistemic stance and social use are formed.

### 4.2. The Threshold of the Pharmakon

From Stiegler's perspective, the restored image itself is a **pharmacological object** containing the potential for both mourning and datafication. Which side it acts on is a matter of **threshold:**

- **To function as a medium for mourning:** The original must be preserved (Criterion 6+), the interface must transparently show the probabilistic nature of generation (Criterion 4+), data must be deleted (Criterion 3+), and the user must be able to maintain an 'as if' attitude (Criterion 2+). When these conditions are met, the restored image becomes a **positive tertiary memory** for the transmission and intergenerational sharing of memory.

- **To function as a datafication device:** The original is discarded (Criterion 6-), the interface obscures the generation process (Criterion 4-), facial data re-enters model training (Criterion 3-), and the user believes the result is real (Criterion 2-). Under these conditions, the restored image becomes a **device for converting loss and kinship relations into data commodities.**

### 4.3. Decisive Insight: Blur is Aura

The most important insight reached by this analysis is this:

> **The blur, stains, and cracks of the original photo are not mere technical defects, but material evidence of time, and therefore a condition for aura.**

Benjamin's aura is based on the uniqueness of the 'here and now.' The physical damage of the original photo is precisely the evidence that this 'here and now' exists within time. That the photo has faded is evidence that it has existed in an album for 40 years. That it has stains is evidence that someone may have taken it out and cried over it.

When generative AI 'restores' this damage, it is not a simple quality improvement, but an **act of removing the traces of time.** And in its place is placed a 'complete' image filled with statistically most probable details — but details unrelated to the specific history of a specific family.

This is precisely the **'production of datafied loss':** a circular structure that takes the user's specific experience of loss as input, outputs the statistical image that best fits that loss, and in return for that output, acquires the user's facial data and kinship information.

## 5. Conclusion: Summary of the Conceptual Problematization

This analysis arrives at the following questions:

1.  **Production of a new aura, not the disappearance of aura?** → Yes. But this 'new aura' is not based on the original's unique existence (Benjamin), but on a **retroactive presence** based on statistical probability. I call this **'aura substitution.'** The restored image replaces the aura of the original (unique existence in time) and produces the aura of a complete, timeless image (the illusion of a recovered past).

2.  **Medium for mourning or device for datafication?** → Six criteria distinguish this. The most decisive criteria are **the preservation of the original** and **the epistemic transparency of the interface.** Centered on these two criteria, the same technology can become either a positive tertiary memory for the transmission of memory or a negative device for converting loss into data commodities.

3.  **Ontological status of blur**: Blur is material evidence that visualizes the boundary between memory and forgetting. When generative restoration removes this blur, it is not a simple technical improvement, but an ontological act that denies the very possibility of forgetting. A complete memory cannot be memory, and an image from which the traces of loss have been removed can no longer be an object of mourning.
