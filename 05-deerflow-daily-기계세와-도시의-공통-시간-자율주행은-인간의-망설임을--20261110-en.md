## Question

Let us assume a situation in which an autonomous vehicle anticipates a pedestrian’s gaze, gait, and subtle movements in the road, and proactively slows down or changes its route. Does the central transformation of the machine age lie in automating the act of driving, or in restructuring the city’s shared temporality by replacing the brief present in which humans hesitated and exchanged signals with the calculation of a probabilistic future? Drawing together Simondon’s theory of technical objects and Bergson’s concept of duration, argue how sensor latency, prediction errors, and pedestrians’ intentional detours reveal the temporal coordination of humans and machines, and whether autonomy can be reconstructed not as an individual’s capacity for choice but as a problem of a transportation assemblage in which prediction, intervention, and correction are distributed.

## 1. Bergson’s Duration (*la durée*) and the “Brief Present” of Traffic

### 1.1 A Bergsonian Analysis: The Paradox of Prediction

For Bergson, time is not a quantitative sequence spatialized by the intellect—the seconds of a clock or a position on a coordinate grid—but a qualitative, heterogeneous, continuous flow of becoming. In *Creative Evolution*, he describes reality as “that which endures: the past pressing into the present and creating something qualitatively new.” Time as duration fundamentally limits prediction. What can be predicted is only what is already contained in reality—that is, mechanical repetition.

From a Bergsonian perspective, an autonomous vehicle’s act of “predicting” a pedestrian’s gaze, gait, and minute movements contains a fundamental **category error**. A pedestrian’s next step, to borrow the language of *Matter and Memory*, is a process in which “pure memory” is selected within the sensorimotor present, and this is an emergent event that cannot be reduced to a probability distribution. An autonomous-driving system represents a pedestrian’s future position as a probability density function in three-dimensional space, \(P(x,y,z,t)\), but this merely “spatializes” the pedestrian’s duration; it does not capture the pedestrian’s temporal reality.

An interesting tension emerges here. **Do human drivers not also “predict” pedestrians’ movements?** Human drivers also read pedestrians’ gaze and gait and slow down accordingly. Yet human “reading” possesses the temporality of what Bergson calls “sympathy” in *Mind-Energy*: through another person’s gestures, I intuitively grasp their intention, and this grasp is closer to a resonance of durations than to analytical calculation or probability estimation. In other words, the human driver’s prediction includes a “direct apprehension of duration,” whereas the machine’s prediction remains a statistical processing of “spatialized time.”

### 1.2 The Ontology of the “Brief Present”

The question’s central concept—the “brief present in which humans hesitated and exchanged signals”—lies at the intersection of Bergson’s concept of duration and William James’s “specious present.” On the road, humans construct a **shared present** within a temporal window of one to three seconds through exchanged glances, slight deceleration, hand gestures, and nods. This present has:

1. **A qualitative totality** — It is not merely the sum of reaction times but possesses the distinctive emotional and perceptual quality of “this moment at this intersection.”
2. **An open-ended ambiguity** — A pedestrian may suddenly change direction, or a driver may yield. This ambiguity is not “noise” to be eliminated but the very condition of interaction.
3. **Intercorporeality** — To use Merleau-Ponty’s term, subjects on the road read one another’s intentions through their respective body schemas. This reading is not an observation by a third party but a direct exchange between bodies.

When an autonomous vehicle replaces this “brief present” with the “calculation of a probabilistic future,” what disappears is not simply the occupation of the human driver but **shared temporality as a qualitative totality**. Once every interaction is reduced to the output of a predictive model, the present on the road is transformed from a shared, creative duration into a synchronization problem among separately operating prediction algorithms.

## 2. Simondon’s Theory of Technical Objects and the Analysis of the Traffic Assemblage

### 2.1 The Concretization of Technical Objects and the Associated Milieu

The central argument Simondon develops in *On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects* (1958) is that a technical object is not simply a passive tool but possesses **its own internal unity and logic of evolution**. A technical object evolves from an “abstract” state, in which each function is separately assembled, toward a “concrete” state, in which its functions are integrated and its structure becomes increasingly cohesive. In this process, the technical object creates its own **associated milieu (*milieu associé*)**. It forms the conditions necessary for its operation, and this milieu is both the condition of the technical object’s existence and its product.

When autonomous vehicles are analyzed within Simondon’s framework, the central question is: **Is an autonomous vehicle a technical individual, or is it one element within a larger technical ensemble?**

### 2.2 The Ontological Status of the Autonomous Vehicle

The autonomous vehicle presents a typical example of an “abstract” technical object. Its functions—perception, judgment, control, and communication—exist as separate modules, and their coordination remains incomplete. Yet the vehicle is evolving in the direction of what Simondon calls “concretization”: the fusion of cameras, lidar, and radar; end-to-end neural networks; and vehicle-to-vehicle communication all demonstrate the gradual integration of functions.

More important, however, is the vehicle’s associated milieu. An autonomous vehicle does not merely **drive on** existing roads; to operate, it **transforms urban space itself**: high-definition mapping, vehicle-to-infrastructure systems, the digitization of pedestrian signaling systems, and the machine-readable standardization of road signs. This requires an expanded application of Simondon’s concept of the associated milieu. The associated milieu of an autonomous vehicle includes not only physical roads but also a multilayered assemblage of **data infrastructures, communication protocols, legal regulations, and insurance systems**.

### 2.3 Shifting to the Problem of Distributed Autonomy

Within Simondon’s framework, autonomy is not an attribute of an individual subject, whether human or machine. The autonomy of a technical object can be defined as the degree to which it can regulate its interaction with its environment—that is, **the degree to which it can regulate its conditions of operation without external intervention**. This definition directly supports the central argument of the question: **autonomy must be reconstructed as a problem of the traffic assemblage in which prediction, intervention, and correction are distributed.**

The “autonomy” of an autonomous vehicle is distributed as follows:

| Dimension | Distribution | Simondonian Interpretation |
|------|----------|-------------|
| **Prediction** | Modeling pedestrian behavior, predicting vehicle trajectories, estimating road conditions | Informatization of the associated milieu → constructing the environment as a variable flow of information |
| **Intervention** | Authority to decide braking, acceleration, and steering | The degree of operational autonomy of the technical individual |
| **Correction** | Alternative strategies following prediction failure; transferring control to a human driver | The persistence of the abstract stage—a sign of openness |

The key point revealed by this table is that the “autonomy” of an autonomous vehicle cannot be measured as a single level, such as Level 0–5. It is instead a **complex assemblage in which different dimensions exhibit different patterns of distribution**. However precise a vehicle’s predictive capacity may be, if the authority to correct prediction failures remains with a human, that autonomy retains a distributed character.

## 3. The Temporality of Prediction and the Effects of Disruption

### 3.1 Transparency of Friction: Temporal Coordination Revealed by Error

The central argument of Document 3 (“Transparency of Friction”) concerns the conditions under which technological errors, unpredictability, and algorithmic bias shift from being “invisible” to becoming “visible.” This concept makes a decisive contribution to the analysis of autonomous driving’s temporality. **During normal operation, the predictive temporality of an autonomous vehicle is transparent**: pedestrians do not know whether the vehicle is “seeing” them, nor can they experientially access how the vehicle’s predictive model operates. Yet when prediction fails—when a vehicle misjudges a pedestrian’s slow pace and brakes suddenly, for example, or when a pedestrian intentionally departs from the model’s expectations—this temporality finally becomes visible through “friction.”

The moment of friction exposes three temporal phenomena:

1. **The visibility of sensor latency**: Under normal conditions, a processing delay of 100 milliseconds is not experienced. But when the predicted and executed trajectories diverge, this delay is experienced as a “difference.” The pedestrian feels, “That car saw me too late,” and this feeling is the technical object’s internal temporality becoming externalized.

2. **The limits of the prediction model’s temporal horizon**: Most autonomous-driving prediction models have a prediction horizon of five to eight seconds. Within this horizon, everything is processed probabilistically. But events beyond the horizon—such as a pedestrian suddenly changing direction after spotting a friend on the opposite side of the street—appear as unprocessable singularities. This reveals the boundary of the technical object’s “temporal individuality.”

3. **Temporal asymmetry produced by intentional deviation**: When a pedestrian, aware that an autonomous vehicle is making predictions, intentionally performs an unpredictable movement—suddenly stopping or walking in a zigzag, for example—the fundamental asymmetry of human–machine interaction becomes visible. Humans can recognize the machine as a predicting entity and respond accordingly, whereas the machine cannot yet model the human’s very awareness of being predicted. This is the moment when “transparency of friction” turns into “transparency of the subject,” the corresponding concept in Document 3: through friction, the subject—the pedestrian—becomes aware of the temporal relationship they have entered into with the machine.

### 3.2 The Ontological Function of Detours: Recovering Duration

Within a Bergsonian framework, a pedestrian’s intentional detour can be interpreted not simply as an act of “deviation” but as a **struggle to recover duration**. The temporality of probabilistic prediction spatializes and quantizes duration—for example, by updating a predicted position every 0.1 seconds. The pedestrian’s intentional noncooperation in response is a form of resistance to having their own temporal flow replaced by another’s calculation.

This resistance has the following ontological implications:

- Autonomous-driving systems are built on the premise that “everything is predictable.” This premise is a form of the **mechanistic reductionism** Bergson criticized in *Creative Evolution*. The pedestrian’s intentional detour is a performative act that “demonstrates” the impossibility of this reductionism in reality.
- From Simondon’s perspective, by contrast, this detour can be read as a **signal demanding the technical object’s openness (*ouverture*)**. As a technical object becomes more concrete, it must acquire greater flexibility in its interaction with the associated milieu. Pedestrian unpredictability reminds us that a technical object can never become a “completely closed” system—that it must always remain open to the emergence of its environment.

## 4. Replacement or Transformation? A Reinterpretation of the Shift in Temporality

The central question presents a binary choice: “Does the central transformation of the machine age lie in automating the act of driving, or in replacing the brief present in which humans hesitated with the calculation of a probabilistic future?” The simple answer is “the latter.” But a deeper analysis reveals that, from a Simondonian perspective, the distinction itself is difficult to sustain.

### 4.1 The Two Transformations as Different Levels of the Same Process

Simondon’s theory of technical objects explains that the “automation of driving” (the transition from Level 0 to Level 5) simultaneously entails the “reorganization of shared temporality.” This is because the evolution of the technical object occurs **simultaneously at two levels**:

- **The level of the individual**: Driving functions are transferred from the human to the machine. This is a question of which subject performs a particular technical operation—braking, accelerating, or steering.
- **The level of the milieu**: Change at the level of the individual necessarily entails the reconstruction of the associated milieu. Once driving is automated, roads are no longer primarily spaces of human-to-human signaling but information-exchange networks among machines, humans, and infrastructures. This reorganization necessarily includes a reorganization of temporality: the qualitative and open-ended present shared among humans is replaced by the synchronized quantitative time of machines.

Therefore, the two alternatives in the question are not competitive but complementary. Automation is the “mechanism” of temporal reorganization, while temporal reorganization is the “meaning” of automation.

### 4.2 Not Replacement but Superposition

An important modification is nevertheless necessary: the term “replacement” is too simple. What occurs in reality is not a discontinuous replacement but **superposition and stratification**.

Even in cities where autonomous driving has been introduced, human drivers, pedestrians, and cyclists continue to exist, each maintaining its own temporality. The problem is how these heterogeneous temporalities are coordinated within a single traffic assemblage. Here Simondon’s concept becomes important:

> “The more a technical object becomes concrete, the more heterogeneous elements it integrates into a single system.” (*On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects*, Chapter 2)

As an autonomous vehicle becomes more “concrete”—and acquires a higher degree of autonomy—it faces the task of **integrating into a single system** increasingly diverse temporal agents: the qualitative duration of humans, the irregular trajectories of bicycles, the unpredictable movements of dogs, and the emergent changes of weather. This integration does not eliminate heterogeneous temporalities; it is a problem of “coordination” within a single distributed system.

## 5. Reconstructing Autonomy: The Distributed Traffic Assemblage

### 5.1 From Individual Capacity to a Property of the Assemblage

The question asks whether autonomy can be reconstructed “not as an individual’s capacity for choice but as a problem of the traffic assemblage in which prediction, intervention, and correction are distributed.” A synthetic examination of Simondon and Bergson suggests that this reconstruction is **not only possible but necessary**.

Simondon’s theory of technical objects redefines autonomy as a **property not of an individual—human or machine—but of a relation**. The autonomy of a technical object is the degree to which it can regulate its own conditions of operation in interaction with its associated milieu. This definition entails the following:

1. **Autonomy is a matter of degree, not kind**: Human drivers are not completely autonomous—their choices are always constrained by traffic regulations, pedestrian intentions, and road conditions. Nor are autonomous vehicles completely nonautonomous—they make decisions and move independently within a given environment. The important question is not **who is autonomous**, but **how autonomy is distributed across the various points of the assemblage**.

2. **Autonomy can be divided into a threefold structure of prediction, intervention, and correction**:
   - **Prediction**: Who or what estimates future states?
   - **Intervention**: Who or what decides and executes actual actions?
   - **Correction**: Who or what adjusts the situation when prediction is wrong or intervention fails?

   These three functions were integrated in the human under traditional driving conditions, but they become differentiated and distributed within an autonomous-driving assemblage. Prediction is performed by a machine—a deep-learning model—and intervention is also performed by a machine—the actuators—while correction may remain with a human safety driver or remote monitor. This distribution changes as technology evolves.

3. **The criterion for whether an arrangement of autonomy is “good” is adaptability, not efficiency**: For Simondon, a good technical object is not merely efficient; it is **capable of adapting to the emergence of its environment**. Accordingly, the “optimality” of distributed autonomy should not be measured by how smoothly the vehicle travels—an efficiency criterion—but by how flexibly it responds to unpredictable events, such as sudden pedestrian behavior, prediction-model failure, or communication loss—an adaptability criterion.

### 5.2 Symmetry and Asymmetry: The Politics of Distributed Autonomy

Document 5 (“The Invisible Hand—Algorithmic Power”) argues that “perception is shaped not only by biological conditions but also by social conditions.” Applied to the autonomous-driving assemblage, this means that distributed autonomy is not a neutral technical design but a **problem of power and control**.

The distribution of autonomy in an autonomous-driving assemblage entails the following asymmetries:

- **Asymmetry of prediction**: The autonomous vehicle predicts the pedestrian, but the pedestrian cannot know precisely what the vehicle predicts. This informational asymmetry determines the power relations of the interaction. The temporality of one-sided prediction creates, on the other side, a relationship between “the seen” and “the unseen.”

- **Asymmetry of correctability**: When prediction fails, the autonomous vehicle can enter a safe mode or transfer control to a human. A pedestrian can modify their own behavior—through an intentional detour—but there is no systematic pathway through which this modification feeds back into the vehicle’s prediction model. This can be explained through Document 3’s concept of “transparency of the subject”: pedestrians do not know how they are being “read” by the machine, and this opacity becomes a condition of asymmetrical interaction.

- **Asymmetry of ethical decisions**: In situations where a collision is unavoidable, decisions concerning the protection of pedestrians versus passengers are programmed into the algorithm, but the criteria for these decisions are not transparent to the public. This creates a new ethical imbalance that did not exist in the same form in traditional traffic.

## 6. Conclusion: Toward a Shared Temporality in the Age of Technogenesis

### 6.1 Three Levels of Temporality

Taken together, the analysis shows that the ways autonomous driving reorganizes the city’s shared temporality can be divided into three levels:

| Level | Existing Temporality | Temporality After Autonomous Driving | Meaning of the Shift |
|------|-----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Micro level** (intersection, 1–3 seconds) | A qualitative, empathic present among humans | Probabilistic prediction and real-time synchronization | Spatialization of duration—the trade-off between the loss of qualitative totality and the acquisition of predictability |
| **Intermediate level** (urban block, minutes to hours) | Individual route choices and self-organization of traffic flow | Centralized/decentralized route optimization and predictive control of traffic flows | Hierarchization of autonomy—individual freedom of choice replaced by systemic efficiency |
| **Macro level** (entire city, days to years) | Human-centered standards of urban design and transportation policy | Data-driven urban planning and intelligent transportation infrastructure | Expansion of the associated milieu—the city itself is redefined as part of the technical object |

### 6.2 From the Perspective of Technogenesis

Document 1 (“From Technological Editing to Technogenesis”) and Documents 2 and 4 (“Structuring Knowledge in the Age of Technogenesis”) place this analysis within a broader framework. Lee Yong-wook’s concept of “technogenesis”—a paradigm shift in which technology goes beyond being a mere instrumental extension and redefines the very concept of human creativity—requires us to understand the temporal transformation of autonomous driving not simply as “automation” but as the **generation of temporality itself**.

The new shared temporality created by the autonomous-driving assemblage is not merely a replacement of “human beings’ slow reactions with machines’ rapid calculations.” It is a temporal arrangement in which **the past—the patterns contained in training data, the present—real-time sensor inputs, and the future—probabilistic predictions** become entangled in a new way. Within this arrangement, the “present” is no longer the empathic resonance among humans but the synchronization point of multiple prediction models. The “past” is no longer experienced as memory but constitutes the prediction model as training data. The “future” is no longer an open field of possibilities but a space pre-calculated as a probability distribution.

### 6.3 Remaining Questions

At the end of the analysis, it is important to clarify both the limitations of the search results and the unresolved issues within the question itself.

**Limitations of the search results**: The Khoj references provided do not include the direct primary works of Simondon or Bergson, nor do they address the specific topic of the temporality of traffic. Document 3 (“Transparency of Friction”) partially intersects with this discussion through its analysis of the visibility of errors, but it does not analyze the concrete mechanisms of sensor latency or prediction models. Accordingly, the Bergson–Simondon axis of this analysis depends on direct readings of those thinkers’ primary works, while the Khoj literature serves a supporting role, particularly in extending the discussion toward a technogenetic framework.

**Unresolved issues in the question itself**:

1. **Can Bergson’s concept of duration be applied to traffic interaction?** Bergson’s duration is fundamentally a concept concerning the temporality of consciousness, and caution is required when applying it to unconscious or semiautomatic bodily movements. Most driving activity is semiautomatic for a skilled driver. Driving lies at the boundary between what Bergson calls “pure memory” and the “sensorimotor present” in *Matter and Memory*, and its temporality cannot be reduced either to pure duration or to purely spatialized time.

2. **Can Simondon’s theory of technical objects encompass “intelligent” technologies—learning algorithms?** When Simondon discusses the evolution of technical objects, he has primarily mechanical and electrical systems in mind: steam engines, vacuum-tube amplifiers, and electric motors. Whether digital algorithms with self-modifying and learning characteristics—such as deep-learning prediction models—can be fully incorporated into his framework, or whether the ontology of the “technical object” must be expanded, remains an open question.

3. **What are the normative criteria for “distributed autonomy”?** Although this analysis has argued that autonomy is a matter of distribution, it has not answered the normative question of how autonomy should be distributed. This is a practical question located at the intersection of political philosophy, law, and urban engineering, extending beyond the field of philosophy of technology and requiring further research.
