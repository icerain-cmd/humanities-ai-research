# AI-Generated Images and the Dialectic of the Aura: From Benjamin to Simondon

## Introduction: Setting the Problem

Walter Benjamin's *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction*, written between 1935 and 1939, is a classic analysis of how new media like photography and film fundamentally alter the ontological status of art. Benjamin's core thesis is that technical reproduction destroys the 'aura' of the artwork — its unique here-and-now (*hic et nunc*), its sense of distance, and the uniqueness of perception. However, today we face new technical conditions that Benjamin could not have anticipated. Images mass-produced by generative AI models, especially the so-called 'AI slop' flooding social media, eliminate even the indexical trace that photography possessed. Does this signify not merely the disappearance of the aura, but a new phase where the very concept of the 'original' loses its ontological meaning? This paper analyzes this question through Benjamin's concept of the aura, Baudrillard's theory of the simulacrum, and Simondon's concept of the concretization of technical objects, further examining whether the synthetic data feedback loop and model collapse of generative AI form a regressive closed loop of technical evolution.

## 1. Benjamin's Aura and the Indexicality of Photography: The Ontology of Reproduction

For Benjamin, the aura is not merely an 'atmosphere' or 'mystery.' It is based on the material existence of the work, its 'here-and-now.' The original is created at a specific time and place, and its physical existence carries the authority of historical testimony. When Benjamin defines the aura in *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* as "a unique phenomenon of a distance, however close it may be," this implies a spatial-temporal structure of perception. Traditional artworks maintained a sense of distance within a ritualistic context (religious worship, courtly display), and this distance was the core of the aura.

The advent of photography fundamentally changes this structure. When Benjamin says in *A Small History of Photography* that "photography freed the human hand for the first time," he sees reproduction not as mere imitation but as an ontological shift. Photography captures the original 'indexically.' That is, light reflected from the subject causes a chemical change on the film, establishing an indexical relationship. In Charles Sanders Peirce's semiotics, an index is a sign that has a physical causal relationship with its object; photography, like smoke or a shadow, directly 'points to' the object. This indexicality grants the photographic image an authority of 'truthfulness.' Even as Benjamin diagnoses the disappearance of the aura in photography, he sees this disappearance not as resolving the issue of 'authenticity' but rather as endowing it with political meaning. By making art accessible to the masses through reproduction, art shifts its function from ritual to politics.

However, the indexicality of photography still presupposes the existence of an 'original.' A photograph is the trace of a real object. Therefore, Benjamin's disappearance of the aura remains at the stage where 'reproduction destroys the aura of the original.' The original still exists, but its uniqueness is diluted by reproduction. This is clearly evident in Benjamin's thesis: "The technical reproducibility of the artwork destroys its aura." The original still exists, and the reproduction holds the status of a 'copy' of that original.

## 2. The Ontological Shift of AI-Generated Images: Loss of Indexicality and the Simulacrum

AI-generated images fundamentally subvert this structure. Generative image models (e.g., Stable Diffusion, DALL-E, Midjourney) learn the statistical distribution of training data to generate 'plausible' images. This process involves no physical causality. That is, an AI-generated image does not capture light from a specific object but is a reconstruction of probabilistic patterns extracted from billions of images. Therefore, in Peirce's semiotics, an AI image is neither an index nor an icon based on similarity to an object; rather, it approaches the phase of a symbol, where signs generate signs. However, this differs from traditional symbols because AI images are based on statistical correlations, not linguistic conventions.

At this point, Jean Baudrillard's theory of the simulacrum offers crucial insight. In *Simulacra and Simulation*, Baudrillard analyzes the third order of simulacra, where there is no reality. This is a stage where only 'generation' by the model exists, with neither original nor copy. For Baudrillard, Disneyland is not a miniature of real America but an example of the 'hyperreal' where everything is simulated. AI-generated images are even more radical. While Disneyland still occupies a physical space, AI images are pure products of data, with no room for an 'original' to intervene in their generation process. There is training data, but there is no 'original' for a specific image. An AI-generated image of a 'cat' is not a photograph of a particular cat but a visualization of the probabilistic distribution of 'cat.'

This ontological shift adds a new dimension to Benjamin's concept of the aura. If the disappearance of the aura in photography was a stage where 'reproduction replaces the original,' then in AI images, we transition to a stage where 'the very concept of the original disappears.' This is not merely an advancement in reproduction technology but a change in the ontological order. Benjamin's diagnosed 'disappearance of the aura' still signified a state where the aura of the original was absent, but in the world of AI images, the conceptual framework of the 'aura' itself becomes meaningless. This is because the aura presupposes the dichotomy of original and copy. Without an original, the disappearance of the aura cannot occur. This creates an irony where the phenomenon Benjamin called the 'collapse of the aura' deconstructs the very conditions of that collapse.

## 3. Simondon's Concretization and the Feedback Loop of AI-Generated Images

However, this analysis remains static. To understand the ontology of AI images more dynamically, we need to introduce Gilbert Simondon's concept of the concretization of technical objects. In *On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects*, Simondon views technical objects not as mere tools but as organic entities that evolve according to internal principles. Early technical objects remain at an abstract stage, with each part functioning independently, but through evolution, parts become coordinated and functions integrated, transitioning to a concrete stage. For example, early car engines had parts operating separately, but modern engines integrate cooling and combustion systems to maximize efficiency.

Viewing generative AI models from Simondon's perspective, this technology can be seen as currently undergoing a process of 'concretization.' Early GAN (Generative Adversarial Network) models were at an abstract stage with separate generator and discriminator, while current diffusion models, through a noise removal process, are closer to a concrete stage where generation and discrimination are integrated. However, AI image generation simultaneously forms a unique feedback loop. This loop reveals a new form of 'technical evolution' that Simondon did not anticipate.

The problem is the 'synthetic data feedback loop,' where generative AI creates its own training data. In reality, many AI models are trained on images collected from the internet, but as the internet itself becomes increasingly filled with AI-generated images, a situation arises where the model learns from its own outputs. AI researchers call this phenomenon 'model collapse.' That is, when a model repeatedly learns from its own generated data, the diversity of the original data distribution is lost, and it gradually produces only monotonous and distorted outputs. This resembles an infinite reproduction of copies of copies where the original has disappeared.

At this point, the relationship between Simondon's concept of concretization and model collapse can be divided into two interpretations.

**First Interpretation: The Perspective of Technical Evolution.** Model collapse can be seen as a 'temporary pathology' occurring during the concretization process of a technical object. According to Simondon, technical objects evolve through coordination with their environment. As generative AI changes the internet image ecosystem and then adapts to this changed environment, temporary instability may occur. This is a natural stage of technical evolution, which can be overcome through higher levels of concretization (e.g., integrating human feedback, multimodal learning). In this interpretation, AI images are still in a state of 'abstract technology' that has not yet fully concretized and will evolve into a more stable form.

**Second Interpretation: The Perspective of a Regressive Closed Loop.** Conversely, the synthetic data feedback loop could represent a regressive phase of technical evolution. Simondon saw that technical objects evolve when they maintain 'openness.' That is, technology acquires new functions through interaction with the external environment. However, when an AI image generation model repeatedly learns only from its own outputs, this is a phenomenon where the technical object blocks contact with the external world and becomes trapped in a self-closed loop. This corresponds to what Simondon called the 'regression of technical objects.' Just as an organism loses genetic diversity through inbreeding, the AI model loses data diversity and gradually transitions to 'model collapse.'
