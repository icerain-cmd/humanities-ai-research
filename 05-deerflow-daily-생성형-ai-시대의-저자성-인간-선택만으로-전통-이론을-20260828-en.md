# Generative AI and the Reconstruction of Authorship: Distributed Creative Conditions of Data–Model–Platform–Human

## 1. Introduction — The Structure of the Problem

Your question requires a theoretical choice between two opposing positions:

> **Position A (Maintain Traditional Authorship Concept):** Can we maintain the traditional concept of authorship solely because human choice intervened in the final form of the work?
> 
> **Position B (Reconstruct Authorship Theory):** Must we reconstruct authorship theory to account for the distributed creative conditions formed by data collection, model design, platform interfaces, and reader responses?

Below, I first examine legal and industry cases from 2024–2025, then propose a direction for reconstructing authorship theory using the ‘AI Player’ concept presented in your *Human Contents* paper as a mediating link.

## 2. Case Analysis — Tensions Revealed by Law and Industry

### 2.1. U.S. Courts: Maintenance and Fractures of the ‘Human Authorship’ Principle

The U.S. copyright framework from 2024–2025 appears to adhere to the traditional authorship concept on the surface, but the very manner of this adherence reveals fractures in the concept.

| Key Decision | Content | Implications |
|-----------|------|------|
| **Zarya of the Dawn** (2023, Copyright Office) | AI-generated images themselves not eligible for copyright; human text + selection/arrangement protected | Applied the traditional criterion of ‘selection and arrangement’ to the AI era, but whether this criterion is suitable for AI media remains unverified |
| **Thaler v. Perlmutter** (DC Circuit, 2025) | Both constitutional copyright clause and current law confirm the premise of human authorship | But judgment on human+AI collaboration cases (not Thaler's claim that “AI is the author”) was deferred |
| **Tremblay v. OpenAI** (ND Cal, ongoing) | Direct infringement claim allowed; fair use defense scheduled for trial in 2026 | Core issue of training data copyright remains unresolved |
| **Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence** (Delaware, 2025.2) | Plaintiff prevails — replication in dataset construction deemed infringement | First substantive ruling that training data collection itself can be copyright infringement |

What these cases collectively reveal is a paradox: **courts maintain the traditional criterion of human authorship, but they fail to recognize that the object to which this criterion applies (the creative process) has fundamentally changed.** In other words, legal tools remain fixed on an 18th-century concept of authorship while attempting to judge 21st-century creative conditions.

### 2.2. Domestic Webtoon Platforms: The Routinization of Distributed Creation

The introduction of AI-assisted tools (especially for coloring and background generation) in the domestic webtoon industry has routinized creative conditions that make it difficult for the traditional authorship concept to function:

1. **Dimension of Training Data:** Naver Webtoon’s adoption of a method that “learns only from a specific artist’s drawings” [citation: 2024 Webtoon Industry Generative AI Adoption](https://v.daum.net/v/1IzlieBE7Q) is a strategy for copyright avoidance, but it also acknowledges that training data has a **direct qualitative impact** on the creative process. That is, which data is used for training determines the style and quality of the output.

2. **Dimension of the Platform:** The format of webtoons (number of panels per episode, scrolling method, optimized screen ratio) is structured by the platform’s UI/UX design. The artist’s ‘intention’ can only be expressed within these formal conditions.

3. **Dimension of Reader Response:** Comments, view counts, and recommendation algorithms provide feedback on whether a series continues and the direction of the story. Readers function not as consumers but as **distributed participants in the creative process**.

This distribution across three dimensions shows that webtoon creation is not the intentional expression of a single human author, but is formed through a **distributed network of data–model–platform–readers**.

## 3. Position and Limitations of the ‘AI Player’ Concept

### 3.1. The Answer Provided by Your Paper

Your paper in *Human Contents* Vol. 78 defines the AI Player as an agent who is “not a mere user of AI tools or a passive participant of the platform, but one who skillfully operates technology and tools to maximize their own creativity, capable of designing, producing, and publishing valuable content.” This definition is a compromise that **places humans at the center of action** while acknowledging collaboration with technology.

The four behavioral categories (questioning, editing, curating, publishing) all presuppose human metacognitive control, positioning AI as a ‘modifier’ – a tool-like partner of the human subject.

### 3.2. Tensions in the Concept — Does the AI Player Sufficiently Explain Distributed Creation?

However, the core of your question is this: **Does the AI Player concept adequately capture the distributed agency of data–model–platform?**

In your paper, AI still occupies the status of a ‘tool.’ AI’s agency is assumed to be controllable by humans. But:

1. **Layer of Training Data:** Which data the AI model is trained on lies outside the human user’s control. The output style of Midjourney or Stable Diffusion is determined by billions of training images, which imprint a specific ‘media bias’ onto the generated product.

2. **Layer of Model Architecture:** The transformer structure, attention mechanism, number of latent space dimensions, etc., are shaped by engineers’ design decisions, which **structurally constrain** the entire space of possible outputs.

3. **Layer of Platform Interface:** Which prompt templates are provided by default, which guidelines are applied, which styles are recommended – these are shaped by the platform’s commercial and ethical decisions.

These three layers operate before the moment of human ‘choice’ and constitute the very universe of selectable options. **Human ‘choice’ is a choice within an already structured space, and the structuring of this space is carried out by agents unrelated to the human user (data collectors, model designers, platform operators).**

## 4. Direction for Reconstructing Authorship Theory

Based on the above analysis, my conclusion leans toward **the need for a reconstruction of authorship theory.** However, it is not a “complete abandonment of the traditional authorship concept” but rather an **expansion of the authorship concept from an individual creator to a complex attribution model of a distributed network.**

### 4.1. Four Layers of Distributed Authorship

| Layer | Agent | Content of Creative Contribution | Relationship to Traditional Authorship Concept |
|--------|------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| **Data Layer** | Original authors (training data providers) | Determines output style, genre, discursive range | Recognized only as violation; not recognized as creative contribution |
| **Model Layer** | Engineers, researchers | Designs structural constraints and possibilities of the generation space | Classified as ‘tool makers’; not recognized as co-authors |
| **Interface Layer** | Platform designers, planners | Induces, restricts, and frames user behavior | Classified as ‘service providers’ |
| **Human User Layer** | AI Player | Final selection, editing, arrangement, publishing | The only layer recognized as author |

### 4.2. Necessary Theoretical Shifts

**① Reexamination of the ‘Selection and Arrangement’ Test**

U.S. copyright law, following the Feist standard (minimal creativity), uses human selection and arrangement as the criterion for authorship. However, this criterion does not ask how the **objects** (options) of selection were constituted. In the context of AI-generated content, ‘selection’:

- Does not recognize the **agency of data, model, and platform** that contribute to generating the objects of selection,
- And relies solely on the final-stage human action as the basis for authorship.

This commits an error similar to **ignoring the architect’s blueprint and the quality of building materials, and calling only the person who laid the last brick the ‘architect.’**

**② Kittlerian Shift: Autonomous Agency of Media**

Kittler’s media theory deconstructs the traditional model of aligning human intention with expression. Media systems are not mere tools, but **a priori structures that condition human perception, cognition, and communication.** In the case of generative AI:

- The entire process from data collection → model training → output generation → user selection
- Depends more on the **operating logic of the media system** than on human intentional expression.

This directly denies the core premise of the traditional authorship concept — **that intention precedes expression and expression faithfully reflects intention.**

**③ Proposal for a Distributed Authorship Model**

Synthesizing the above analysis, the following distributed authorship model is needed:

> **Distributed Authorship:** In the generative AI media environment, the author is not a single human individual, but a **networked attribution relationship** among data providers, model designers, platform designers, and human users. In this network, ‘creation’ is not a single point of intentional action, but an **event** where multiple points of agency converge.

This model requires distinguishing between the legal level and the theoretical level:
- **Legal Level:** Maintain the human authorship requirement and liability attribution system of current copyright law, but specify the ‘creative control’ standard for AI-assisted works.
- **Theoretical Level:** Authorship theory explaining the creative phenomenon should be reconstructed toward recognizing distributed agency. This requires a theoretical effort to expand your ‘AI Player’ concept **from AI as a modifier to AI as a subject dimension.**

### 4.3. Expansion of the AI Player Concept — From Modifier to Distributed Subject

Your paper defines the AI Player as ‘a human who utilizes AI.’ However, the above analysis suggests:

**The AI Player concept should encompass a dual meaning:**
1. **AI Player as a human–AI hybrid subject:** Humans perform prompting, editing, curating, but the conditions and possibilities of those actions are structured by the AI model, data, and platform.
2. **Human as a point in a networked attribution:** The final point of legal responsibility and creative decision remains human, but that human’s ‘creativity’ is redefined not as original invention in the traditional sense, but as **selection, arrangement, and recontextualization within an already structured space of possibilities.**

This is a further theorization of the “metacognitive collaboration” concept from your paper. Metacognitive control should be expanded from simply the ability to skillfully use AI tools into **the ability to recognize that one’s own creative actions occur within a distributed network, and to critically reflect on the influence of each layer of that network (data–model–platform).**

## 5. Conclusion: Authorship Theory Must Be Reconstructed — But as Expansion, Not Rupture

A direct answer to your question:

> **“Can we maintain the traditional concept of authorship solely on the grounds that human choice intervened in the final form of the work?”**
> 
> → **No, we cannot.** Human choice always occurs within an already structured space of possibilities, and the structuring of this space is critically contributed to by the agency of data collectors, model designers, and platform designers. Making ‘choice’ a sufficient condition for authorship renders most of the creative process invisible.

> **“Must we reconstruct authorship theory to account for the distributed creative conditions formed by data collection, model design, platform interfaces, and reader responses?”**
> 
> → **Yes, we must.** However, this reconstruction should not be a complete abandonment of the traditional authorship concept, but a **repositioning of the authorship concept on a spectrum from individual author to network of distributed agency.**

Your ‘AI Player’ concept provides a starting point for this reconstruction. Viewing the player as a hybrid human+AI subject captures one axis of distributed creation. However, for this concept to become more refined, it requires:

1. Redefining AI’s agency not as a ‘tool’ but as ‘media conditions,’
2. Theoretical tools to analyze how each of the three layers (data, model, platform) intervenes in the creative process, and
3. A normative model for how to systematize legal responsibility and attribution while acknowledging distributed agency.

These three tasks can advance your AI Player theory into a **complete theory of distributed authorship** encompassing data, model, platform, and human.
