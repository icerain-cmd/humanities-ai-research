# Predictive Work-Allocation Systems and the Preemptive Organization of Temporality — At the Intersection of Bergson and Foucault

## 1. Formulating the Problem: Automation or the Preemption of Temporality?

A predictive work-allocation system in an Amazon warehouse goes beyond the simple automation of labor. By analyzing workers’ past movements, rest periods, and error records and using them to specify the pace and routes of work during the next shift in real time, the system’s fundamental transformation does not consist in replacing human labor with machines. Rather, **it consists in calculating future possibilities for action before workers experience their own fatigue and judgment, thereby organizing the temporality of the body in advance.** This is a concrete instance of how the Mechanosphere fundamentally reconstructs the conditions of human experience.

---

## 2. Bergson’s Duration (*durée*) — How Prediction Destroys Time

### 2.1. The Opposition Between Qualitative Duration and Quantitative Time

For Bergson, duration is a qualitative, heterogeneous, and creative flow that cannot be divided according to a homogeneous metric. In *Matter and Memory*, he argues that the past is condensed within the present, and that duration is a movement in which past and present are interwoven through memory. The Amazon system, which reduces 10,000 past movements to statistical patterns and calculates future routes, **replaces precisely this qualitative duration with a quantitative time series.**

**The operation of the Mechanosphere:** The worker’s movements captured by sensors are no longer the qualitative accumulation of fatigue as the worker experiences it, but discrete data points displayed on a dashboard. Based on this data, the system issues an instruction such as, “You must reach shelf 47-B in three minutes.” Before the worker feels their own fatigue, their body has already been placed on a predicted trajectory.

### 2.2. Three Levels at Which Prediction Destroys Duration

1. **The reduction of the past:** The worker’s past is flattened into statistical correlations rather than creative memory—recollection and image-memory. A slow movement on a Tuesday three weeks earlier—perhaps caused by a personal circumstance, such as knee pain that day—is replaced by the label “inefficient route.”
2. **The neutralization of the present:** Because the system continually compares present performance with future predictions, workers can no longer experience their movements as present in themselves. Each movement acquires meaning only within a field of difference, such as “two seconds behind the assigned prediction.”
3. **The occupation of the future:** The future is no longer a field of open possibilities but is closed into an already calculated probability distribution. The phrase “94% prediction accuracy” signifies that the future has been statistically occupied.

The result produced by the combination of these three levels is not simply an intensification of labor. It is **the destruction of duration—that is, the dismantling of the very conditions under which time can be experienced qualitatively.**

## 3. Foucault’s Disciplinary Power — How Prediction Intensifies Discipline

### 3.1. The Classical Apparatuses of Disciplinary Power

In *Discipline and Punish*, Foucault identifies the following as core mechanisms of disciplinary power: (1) surveillance, or the panopticon; (2) normalized training, or *dressage*; (3) normalizing judgment; and (4) examination. All of these are **apparatuses that organize the body temporally.** Military drills, school timetables, and factory workflows standardize the body according to a temporal sequence.

### 3.2. How the Predictive System Transforms Discipline

The Amazon system transforms classical discipline:

- **The temporal extension of surveillance:** Whereas the panopticon involved spatial surveillance—one could always be seen without knowing exactly when one was being watched—the predictive system performs **temporal surveillance**. Sensors store the worker’s past, track the present, and predict the future. Workers know that the entirety of their time lies within the system’s field of vision.

- **The individualization of training:** Whereas classical discipline imposed the same movement standards on every body, the predictive system uses **individualized predictive models** to calculate routes and speeds optimized for each worker. This is an intensification of training and, at the same time, a more refined form of capture.

- **The preemption of normalization:** Classical discipline operated by returning deviations to the norm after they occurred. The predictive system intervenes **before deviation occurs**: “Your fatigue index is predicted to exceed the threshold. Proceed to the rest area for 15 minutes.” In this command, rest is not a worker’s right but an instruction aimed at maintaining the optimal condition predicted by the system.

- **The continuous examination:** Whereas the classical examination was an evaluation conducted at a specific moment, the dashboard performs a **real-time, continuous examination**. Every movement is immediately scored, and that score is fed back into the predictive model for the next movement.

### 3.3. The Triple Apparatus of Database, Algorithm, and Dashboard

What matters here is that this system operates not merely as a technical tool but as an **ontological apparatus**. This is also the point at which the concepts of the Mechanosphere and Artificial Nature converge (see Document 1, the discussion of the age of technogenesis). The database preserves the past, the algorithm calculates the future, and the dashboard reconstructs the present as a tension between the two. The worker’s body is situated within a threefold temporality composed of **past patterns stored in the database, future predictions generated by the algorithm, and present commands displayed on the dashboard.**

## 4. The Intersection of the Two Discussions — The Temporal Apparatus of the Mechanosphere

### 4.1. The Point of Convergence Between Bergson and Foucault

Bergson analyzes how the qualitative experience of time is destroyed, while Foucault analyzes how time is organized as an apparatus of power. Their intersection enables the following question:

**Does the predictive system use the destruction of duration as a mechanism of power?**

The answer is affirmative. It is not the worker’s experienced time—fatigue, boredom, concentration, or emergent judgment—but the system’s time of prediction—the optimal sequence calculated by the algorithm—that becomes the actual time of the workplace. Workers no longer possess their own time. Their bodies function merely as media for actualizing a predicted future.

### 4.2. Why “Preemptive Organization” Is More Fundamental Than “Automation”

If automation means replacing human labor with machines, what this system does is **subordinate human labor to the temporality of the machine.** Humans continue to labor, but the temporal conditions of their labor are fundamentally reconstructed.

The logic of automation: “The machine works instead of the human.”

The logic of preemptive organization: “The human works within the predictions calculated by the machine.”

The former replaces the subject of labor, whereas the latter **occupies the temporal conditions of labor.** This occupation is more fundamental because it demonstrates that the conditions of laboring experience can be completely transformed even without replacing the laboring subject.

## 5. Conditions of Disruption — How Detours, Delays, and Refusals Break the Temporal Coupling

### 5.1. Invisible Disruptions: Qualitative Leakage from the Data

For the predictive system to operate completely, every action must be rendered as data. Yet not every action by a worker can be fully captured. The following disruptions may occur:

- **Intentional inefficiency:** When a worker picks up an item 0.5 seconds later than the system predicts, the act is recorded in the data as an “error,” but is experienced by the worker as “the recovery of my own time.” This slight delay reduces the accuracy of the predictive model while preserving, for the worker, a minimal unit of temporal agency.

- **Emergent modification of routes:** Even when the system specifies an optimal route, a worker may alter it in response to the need for cooperation with another worker, sensory judgment—“This box looks heavy”—or simple habit. Such modifications fall outside the system’s predictive range.

- **Collective detours:** Through tacit cooperation among workers, temporary breaks, adjustments in work pace, and role exchanges may occur that the system does not predict. These generate a more powerful disruption than individual resistance.

### 5.2. The Ontological Significance of Disruption

These disruptions are not merely system errors. They **demonstrate that duration has not been completely extinguished.** Workers’ bodies continue to generate qualitative time, and this generated time possesses an emergent quality that the system cannot predict. Disruption is a sign that human temporality has not been completely subsumed under machine temporality.

### 5.3. The Double Character of Disruption

However, disruption itself is also at risk of being captured by the system. The system may feed the remaining 6% of error in its “94% prediction accuracy” back into the training data and update the model. In other words, **disruption becomes training data for the next version of the system.** Unless disruption completely disables the system, it is converted into material for the system’s evolution.

## 6. Why Human Autonomy Must Be Reframed as a Problem of Redesign

### 6.1. The Limits of the Concept of Inner Will

Traditionally, autonomy has been understood as grounded in the individual’s inner will—free will, conscious choice, and moral judgment. Yet in the face of a predictive system, the claim “I choose freely” becomes powerless. Under conditions in which the system calculates a worker’s fatigue before the worker feels it and specifies their route before their own judgment does, inner will can operate only within the system’s predictive range.

Even if a worker decides to rest “on their own,” if the system has already predicted “fatigue index: 87%; rest recommended,” their decision may amount merely to an act of approving the system’s prediction. The crisis of autonomy here is not simply the loss of freedom of choice, but the condition of being unable to distinguish **which choices are truly “one’s own.”**

### 6.2. The Problem of Redesigning the Human–Machine Assemblage

The path to recovering autonomy does not lie in strengthening individual will or consciousness. Rather, it lies in **redesigning the arrangement of humans and machines itself.** This leads to the following practical questions:

- **The arrangement of transparency:** Is it possible to create an arrangement in which workers can know how their data are collected and what predictions which models produce? This is not merely a matter of providing information, but of establishing **critical access rights** to the triple apparatus of database, algorithm, and dashboard.

- **The arrangement of refusal rights:** Can a mechanism be designed through which workers can refuse the system’s predictions and work according to their own judgment? A “reject prediction” button would be more than a simple function; it would institutionalize the **minimal condition of temporal autonomy.**

- **The arrangement of joint decision-making:** The system may predict a worker’s fatigue, but can a bidirectional arrangement be created in which workers enter their experienced fatigue into the system and thereby modify the predictive model? This would signify not only the **democratization of data** but also the **sharing of temporal agency.**

### 6.3. The Transition to WE / Co-Resonant Subjectivity

The stages of “relational resonance” mentioned in Document 2 suggest the possibility that human–machine contact may move beyond opposition—an unpleasant uncanniness—and critique, or critical reflection, toward **relational resonance**, in which both parties transform one another. In the context of a predictive system, this would mean a condition in which:

- workers neither passively follow the system’s predictions,
- nor simply reject the system,
- but both parties create a new temporality of work through mutual adjustment.

Achieving this requires that workers’ temporal autonomy be considered from the design stage of the system itself. This is not merely a technical issue but an ontological and ethical one.

## 7. Conclusion: Beyond Automation, the Politics of Temporality

The predictive work-allocation system in an Amazon warehouse raises a more fundamental problem than automation: **the politics of temporality.** The system’s central transformation consists in the following:

1. **It does not replace human labor;** rather, it calculates, organizes, and occupies workers’ time before they experience it as their own.
2. **Bergson’s concept of duration** reveals what the system destroys—the conditions of qualitative temporal experience—while **Foucault’s theory of disciplinary power** enables us to analyze how the system operates through temporal surveillance, individualized training, preemptive normalization, and continuous examination.
3. **Workers’ detours, delays, and refusals** disrupt the system’s complete operation, but these disruptions are themselves at risk of being fed back into the system as training data.
4. **The problem of autonomy** must be reframed not as a problem of the individual’s inner will but as a problem of redesigning the human–machine assemblage. This is not simply a matter of resistance or adaptation, but of seeking the conditions under which both parties can jointly create a new temporality.

It is hoped that this discussion will not only illuminate the concrete operations of the Mechanosphere but also contribute to establishing a theoretical point of grafting with the ecology of Artificial Nature and the theory of WE / co-resonant subjectivity.
