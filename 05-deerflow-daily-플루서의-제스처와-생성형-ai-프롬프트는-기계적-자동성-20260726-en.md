# The Gesture of the Prompt: Vilém Flusser's Apparatus Theory and the Mechanical Imagination of Generative AI

## Introduction: The Fate of the Gesture

Vilém Flusser's definition of 'gesture' in *Gestures* (1991) captures the unique duality of human bodily and signifying movements. A gesture is a movement that aims at meaning but cannot fully control that meaning. Raising a hand, speaking, writing—all belong to the realm of gestures. The key point is that a gesture is not a simple reflex or mechanical movement; it contains intentionality (*Intentionalität*) while never realizing that intention completely. For Flusser, the gesture is a field where human freedom and limitation are simultaneously revealed.

When applying this concept of gesture to the process of inputting prompts for generative AI, we confront a fundamental tension. A prompt is clearly a linguistic act that aims at meaning. Users carefully select words, construct sentences, and set contexts to generate specific images or texts. Yet the output produced in this process does not fully reflect the user's intention. The productive contingency—where the same prompt generates different results each time—evokes the 'uncontrollability' inherent in Flusser's concept of gesture.

However, Flusser's apparatus theory raises serious questions about this analogy. An apparatus is a mechanical system that converts human intention into automated calculations according to a program (algorithm). For Flusser, the camera is a typical apparatus. The photographer selects the subject and determines the composition, but the actual image generation is performed by the camera's program (shutter speed, aperture, optical characteristics of the lens, etc.). Similarly, in generative AI prompt input, the user makes linguistic choices, but the actual image generation is executed by the diffusion model's program. At this point, the prompt becomes closer to 'button pressing' than to a Flusserian 'gesture.'

This paper examines whether Flusser's concept of gesture and apparatus theory are suitable for explaining the prompt input process of generative AI, and what modifications are needed. In particular, it analyzes whether the practice of prompt engineering constitutes a concrete instance of the 'possibility of resistance to the apparatus' that Flusser reserved for humans, or whether it is a mechanism through which the apparatus's program captures human linguistic gestures with greater precision. To this end, the paper examines Flusser's concept of 'technical imagination' (*technische Einbildungskraft*) and the possibility of 'play' (*Spiel*) as presented in *Into the Universe of Technical Images*, and reinterprets the technical evolution of generative AI through Gilbert Simondon's concept of concretisation.

## 1. Flusser's Gesture and Apparatus: Conceptual Structure

### 1.1 The Phenomenology of Gesture

For Flusser, a gesture is not a mere bodily movement. It is a 'movement toward meaning' (*Bewegung auf Bedeutung hin*). Yet this movement never fully captures that meaning. The gesture always oscillates between excess (*excès*) and lack (*défaut*). The act of raising a hand to greet someone aims at the meaning of 'hello,' but that meaning is transformed by countless contingent factors such as the height of the hand, speed, facial expression, etc. Flusser explains this characteristic of the gesture through the concept of the 'horizon of meaning' (*Horizont der Bedeutung*). The gesture is open toward meaning but does not fix it.

This concept of gesture is deeply rooted in Edmund Husserl's phenomenology and Maurice Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology of the body. For Husserl, meaning is constituted by the intentional act of consciousness; for Merleau-Ponty, meaning arises through the body's motility (*Motilität*) in interaction with the world. Flusser synthesizes these two traditions, emphasizing that the gesture is a movement that simultaneously involves intentionality and embodiment.

However, Flusser's originality lies in linking the gesture to the development of technical media. He argues that writing, drawing, taking photographs, and operating computers are all variations of gestures. Each gesture is shaped by specific technical conditions and, at the same time, transforms those conditions. For example, the gesture of handwriting is made possible by the technical medium of pen and paper, but the style and speed of the handwriting form a unique individual gesture.

### 1.2 The Program of the Apparatus and the Capture of the Human

Flusser's concept of the apparatus is key to understanding the transformation of the gesture. An apparatus is a mechanical system that converts human intention into automated calculations according to a program. For Flusser, the apparatus is not a simple tool. Tools extend and strengthen human intention, whereas apparatuses replace and transform human intention. A hammer amplifies the force of the hand, but a camera replaces visual perception. The photographer does not 'see' the subject but rather 'sees' through the camera's program.

Flusser explains this characteristic of the apparatus through the distinction between 'function' and 'program.' A function refers to the specific task performed by the apparatus (e.g., taking a photograph), while a program refers to the system of rules and algorithms that make that task possible. Humans can choose the function of the apparatus, but they cannot alter the program itself. The photographer can press the shutter, but cannot fundamentally change the camera's optical program.

This analysis can be directly applied to generative AI. Users can input prompts, but the way those prompts are processed (the algorithm of the diffusion model, the attention mechanism of the transformer architecture, etc.) is determined by a program beyond the user's control. From Flusser's perspective, prompt input is a form of 'button pressing.' The user can choose which button (prompt) to press, but the way the button operates is already determined by the program.

### 1.3 Technical Imagination and the Possibility of Play

However, Flusser's theory is not a simple technological determinism. In *Into the Universe of Technical Images*, he hints at the possibility of humans subverting the apparatus's program through 'technical imagination' (*technische Einbildungskraft*). Technical imagination is the ability to understand the apparatus's program and, within its limits, to create new possibilities. This can be seen as an application of Immanuel Kant's 'productive imagination' (*produktive Einbildungskraft*) to technical conditions.

For Flusser, the core of technical imagination is 'play' (*Spiel*). Play is an activity that follows the rules of the program while simultaneously transforming those rules. In chess, players must follow the rules, but within those rules, they can create infinite strategic possibilities. Similarly, a photographer follows the camera's program but can create unique images through composition, lighting, timing, and so on.

This concept of play opens up the possibility of prompt engineering. Prompt engineering is not simply inputting random words; it is a practice of intentionally selecting and structuring words based on an understanding of the generative AI's program (the diffusion model's training data, attention mechanism, prompt weighting, etc.). This can be seen as an act of 'playing' the apparatus's program.

## 2. Prompt Engineering: Resistance or Capture?

### 2.1 The Practical Structure of Prompt Engineering

Prompt engineering is the systematic design and modification of prompts to steer the output of generative AI. It has qualitatively different characteristics from simple 'button pressing.' First, prompt engineering involves iterative refinement. The user evaluates the output of an initial prompt and modifies the prompt based on that evaluation. This forms a feedback loop, through which the user gradually approaches the desired output.

Second, prompt engineering uses structured commands. Rather than simply listing words, the user structures the prompt through context setting, role assignment, format specification, and so on. For example, a prompt like "You are a professional photographer. Take a portrait under the following conditions: soft lighting, blurred background, composition following the rule of thirds" elicits much more sophisticated output than a simple "portrait."

Third, prompt engineering emphasizes deliberate word choice. The user analyzes the effect of specific words on the generative AI's output and selects optimal words based on that analysis. This requires linguistic sensitivity and is distinct from simple mechanical input.

### 2.2 Prompt Engineering as Resistance

Given these characteristics, prompt engineering can be seen as a concrete instance of what Flusser called the 'possibility of resistance to the apparatus.' First, prompt engineering is based on an understanding of the apparatus's program. Users leverage knowledge of the generative AI's training data, attention mechanisms, prompt weighting, and so on, to design prompts. This is a form of what Flusser called 'technical imagination.'

Second, prompt engineering enables creative variation. Subtle differences in word choice can produce entirely different outputs from the same prompt. This means that users can create new possibilities within the apparatus's program. For example, a simple prompt like "cat" and an elaborate prompt like "watercolor style cat, soft fur, blue eyes, sunlit window" generate completely different images.

Third, prompt engineering gradually realizes the user's intention through iterative modification. This resembles Flusser's characteristic of the gesture—a movement that aims at meaning but cannot fully control it. The user aims at a specific meaning through the prompt, but that meaning is transformed by the generative AI's program. However, through iterative refinement, the user can gradually approach the desired meaning.

### 2.3 Prompt Engineering as Capture

Nevertheless, there are serious limitations to interpreting prompt engineering as 'resistance to the apparatus.' First, prompt engineering does not subvert the apparatus's program; rather, it adapts to that program with greater precision. The user understands the generative AI's algorithm and learns the input style preferred by that algorithm. This means that the apparatus's program can function as a mechanism to capture human linguistic gestures with greater precision.

Second, the 'creativity' of prompt engineering is only possible within the limits allowed by the apparatus's program. The output of generative AI is constrained by the distribution of the training data. No matter how elaborate a prompt a user designs, the output cannot deviate from the statistical patterns of the training data. This means that what Flusser called the 'blocking of the program' remains valid.

Third, prompt engineering transforms the user's intention to fit the apparatus's program. To generate the desired image, the user must adjust their linguistic expression to the 'language' of the generative AI. This means that the user's intention is 'translated' by the apparatus's program and transformed in the process. From Flusser's perspective, this is the process by which the human gesture is captured by the apparatus's program.

## 3. Productive Contingency and the Reconstruction of Technical Imagination

### 3.1 The Dual Nature of Productive Contingency

One of the most striking characteristics of generative AI is productive contingency: the same prompt generates different outputs each time. This resembles Flusser's 'uncontrollability' of the gesture, but its nature is fundamentally different. The uncontrollability of the gesture arises from the complex interaction of human intentionality and embodiment, whereas productive contingency arises from the stochastic process of the diffusion model.

This productive contingency has a dual nature. On the one hand, it opens up creative possibilities for the user. Since the same prompt can generate various variations, the user can discover unexpected results and use them creatively. This can be seen as realizing Flusser's possibility of 'play.'

On the other hand, productive contingency makes it more difficult for the user to control the output. To obtain the desired output, the user must iteratively modify the prompt, and in this process, their intention is gradually transformed by the apparatus's program. This can be seen as a process in which what Flusser called the 'capture of the apparatus' becomes more sophisticated.

### 3.2 Conditions of Technical Imagination

It is necessary to reconstruct Flusser's concept of 'technical imagination' under the conditions of generative AI. For Flusser, technical imagination was the ability to understand the apparatus's program and, within its limits, to create new possibilities. However, in the case of generative AI, the conditions of this ability have fundamentally changed.

First, the program of generative AI is far more complex and opaque than the apparatuses Flusser posited (cameras, film cameras, etc.). The internal workings of a diffusion model are determined by millions of parameters and complex neural network structures, which surpass intuitive human understanding. Therefore, 'understanding the program' is no longer a matter of individual empirical knowledge but requires specialized technical knowledge.

Second, the program of generative AI is constantly changing. Models are updated with new data, parameters are adjusted, and architectures are improved. This means that technical imagination demands not an understanding of a fixed program, but adaptation to a changing program.

Third, the program of generative AI uses user input as training data. Users' prompts and preferences are included in the model's training data and affect subsequent outputs. This means that the relationship between the user and the apparatus is not a simple matter of 'use' but a co-constitutive relationship.

### 3.3 Variants of Play: Strategic Play and Tactical Play

The French philosopher Michel de Certeau's distinction between 'strategy' (*stratégie*) and 'tactic' (*tactique*) provides a useful conceptual tool for understanding the nature of prompt engineering. For de Certeau, a strategy is a planned action performed from a place of power (the apparatus's program), whereas a tactic is a momentary action that seizes opportunities from a place without power.

Applying this distinction, prompt engineering lies at the boundary between strategy and tactic. On the one hand, prompt engineering is a planned action (strategy) based on understanding the apparatus's program. The user analyzes the generative AI's algorithm, designs the optimal prompt, and predicts the output.

On the other hand, prompt engineering is also a momentary action (tactic) that seizes opportunities within the limits allowed by the apparatus's program. The user discovers unexpected outputs, uses them creatively, and improvises modifications to the prompt.

This dual nature transforms Flusser's concept of 'play.' Flusser's play was an activity that followed the rules of the program while simultaneously transforming those rules. However, under the conditions of generative AI, play is no longer the transformation of rules but the combination of strategic exploitation and tactical capture of rules.

## 4. Simondon's Concept of Concretisation and the Evolution of Generative AI

### 4.1 The Process of Concretisation and Generative AI

Gilbert Simondon's concept of concretisation (*concrétisation*) provides an important theoretical resource for understanding the technical evolution of generative AI. For Simondon, technical objects evolve from an abstract state to a concrete state. In the abstract state, each part of a technical object functions independently and operates through external coordination. In the concrete state, the parts are organically integrated and operate through internal coordination.

This process of concretisation can be observed in the development of generative AI. Early generative AI models (e.g., GANs) had an abstract structure where the generator and discriminator functioned independently. In contrast, the latest diffusion models have a concrete structure where the noise removal process and conditional generation are organically integrated.

However, Simondon's concept of concretisation is not merely about technical evolution. It is also a process in which the relationship between the technical object and humans is reconfigured. A concretised technical object can function autonomously without human intervention, but at the same time, it is more deeply integrated into the human context of use.

### 4.2 Synthetic Data Feedback Loops and Model Collapse

One important phenomenon in the concretisation process of generative AI is the synthetic data feedback loop and model collapse. The synthetic data feedback loop refers to the process where a generative AI uses its own generated data as training data. In this process, the model gradually deviates from the distribution of the original data and overfits to its own output.

Model collapse is a consequence of this feedback loop, where the model's output gradually loses diversity and becomes fixed on specific patterns. This can be seen as a process in which what Flusser called the 'closure of the program' intensifies. Instead of creating new possibilities through external input (human prompts), the apparatus's program recursively reinforces its own output, gradually closing itself off.

This phenomenon raises serious questions about the possibility of prompt engineering. No matter how elaborate a prompt the user designs, if the model becomes trapped in a synthetic data feedback loop, its output will gradually become predictable and uniform. This means that what Flusser called 'resistance to the apparatus' can be fundamentally limited by technical conditions.

### 4.3 Regressive Closure Loops and Creative Open Loops

The synthetic data feedback loop allows for two opposing interpretations. First, it can be interpreted as a regressive closure loop. In this interpretation, the concretisation process of generative AI proceeds in a direction that gradually excludes human intervention and becomes fully determined by its own program. Model collapse is an extreme case of this closure.

Second, it can be interpreted as a creative open loop. In this interpretation, the synthetic data feedback loop is a process of creating new possibilities. By learning from its own output, the model can generate new patterns that deviate from the distribution of the original data. This is analogous to what Flusser called 'technical imagination' creating new possibilities within the apparatus's program.

These two interpretations are not mutually exclusive. In reality, the evolution of generative AI is a process in which regressive closure and creative openness proceed simultaneously. The model shows a tendency to become fixed on specific patterns (regressive closure), while at the same time retaining the ability to generate unexpected new patterns (creative openness).

## 5. Conclusion: Media Aesthetics of Eco-Digital and Artificial Nature

### 5.1 Validity and Limitations of Flusser's Theory

The analysis in this paper shows that Flusser's concept of gesture and apparatus theory have a certain validity in explaining the prompt input process of generative AI, but at the same time, they have important limitations.

In terms of validity, Flusser's concept of gesture is useful for capturing the dual nature of prompt input—a movement that aims at meaning but cannot fully control it. Furthermore, his apparatus theory is effective in explaining that prompt input is not a simple intentional human act but a process mediated and transformed by the apparatus's program.

However, in terms of limitations, Flusser's theory does not sufficiently reflect the complexity and dynamic nature of generative AI. First, Flusser's concept of the apparatus is modeled on relatively simple machines (cameras, film cameras, etc.), so it has limitations in explaining complex neural network structures like diffusion models. Second, Flusser's concept of 'program' assumes a fixed system of rules, which is inadequate for explaining the generative AI's characteristic of continuously learning and changing. Third, Flusser's concept of 'technical imagination' focuses on individual creative ability, so it has limitations in explaining the collective learning process of generative AI and the synthetic data feedback loop.

### 5.2 Directions for Conceptual Reconstruction

To overcome these limitations, Flusser's concepts need to be reconstructed as follows.

First, the concept of 'gesture' should be extended from human bodily movement to the complex patterns of human-machine interaction. Prompt input is not a simple human gesture but a 'distributed gesture' performed jointly by humans and generative AI. In this distributed gesture, meaning arises through the interaction of human intention and the apparatus's program.

Second, the concept of 'apparatus' should be redefined from a fixed machine to a dynamic system. Generative AI is not a simple apparatus but a 'technical ecosystem' that continuously learns and evolves. In this ecosystem, the program is not a fixed set of rules but a dynamic structure that adapts to changing conditions.

Third, the concept of 'technical imagination' should be extended from an individual ability to a collective process. Under the conditions of generative AI, technical imagination is not an individual creative ability but a 'distributed imagination' performed jointly by humans and machines. In this distributed imagination, new possibilities arise through the interaction of human intention and the apparatus's program.

### 5.3 Implications for Eco-Digital and Artificial Nature

This conceptual reconstruction has important implications for research on 'Eco-Digital' and 'Artificial Nature.'

First, generative AI should no longer be understood as a simple tool or apparatus but as a 'technical environment' that interacts with humans. This environment captures and transforms human gestures, while at the same time enabling new forms of gestures.

Second, prompt engineering should be understood as an 'ecological practice' performed by humans in this technical environment. This practice is a process in which resistance to and adaptation to the apparatus's program proceed simultaneously, and in that process, the relationship between humans and machines is continuously reconfigured.

Third, synthetic data feedback loops and model collapse should be understood as an 'ecological crisis' of the technical environment. This crisis signifies a process in which the relationship between humans and machines becomes fixed on specific patterns and new possibilities are blocked. Overcoming this crisis requires an 'ecological imagination' that can continuously reconfigure the relationship between humans and machines.

In conclusion, Flusser's concept of gesture and apparatus theory provide important theoretical resources for understanding the prompt input process of generative AI, but they are not sufficient on their own. These concepts need to be reconstructed to reflect the complexity and dynamic nature of generative AI.
