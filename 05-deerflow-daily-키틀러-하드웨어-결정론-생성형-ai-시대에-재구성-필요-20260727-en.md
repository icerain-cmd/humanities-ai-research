# Media Aesthetics in the Age of Technogenesis: Kittler's Hardware Determinism and the Reconfiguration of Generative AI

## 1. Introduction: The Dialectics of Media Determinism

Friedrich Kittler's media theory radicalizes McLuhan's proposition that "the medium determines the message," arguing that the material hardware of media defines the very conditions for storing, processing, and transmitting information. As presented in his *Gramophone, Film, Typewriter*, the analog media of the late 19th and early 20th centuries each drew dividing lines in the perceivable world according to their unique material foundations (mechanical recording of sound, optical imaging, textual input). For Kittler, media are not mere transmission tools but a priori conditions of human perception and cognition. This logic was further reinforced with the advent of digital computers in the late 20th century, leading to a 'digital convergence' where all media converge into a single universal hardware (Turing machine).

However, the rapid development of generative artificial intelligence since the 2020s poses new challenges to Kittler's determinism. Models represented by GPT-4, Stable Diffusion, and Sora are no longer determined directly by hardware; instead, output is determined by abstract conditions at the software level: probabilistic distributions, model weights, and prompt embeddings. At this point, can 'the medium' still be reduced to hardware (GPU, TPU, memory bandwidth), or does it acquire a new ontological status as a software-hardware complex? To answer this question, this paper contrasts Kittler's determinism with the technical conditions of generative AI and reconfigures it by connecting to Gilbert Simondon's concept of 'concretisation,' synthetic data feedback loops, and the phenomenon of model collapse. It also explores the implications of this analysis for 'eco-digital' or 'artificial nature' research.

## 2. Kittler's Hardware Determinism: The Primacy of Materiality

Kittler's media theory adopts a unique position combining Heidegger's critique of technology with Foucault's discourse analysis. In *Aufschreibesysteme 1800·1900* (Discourse Networks 1800/1900), he argued that literary discourse is not merely the circulation of ideas but is shaped by material conditions such as writing tools, printing technology, and educational institutions. Here, the 'discourse network' begins with the textual fixity of the alphabet and transitions through the late 19th-century phonograph (storage of sound), film (storage of movement), and typewriter (mechanization of writing), fundamentally transforming the paradigms of human senses and cognition.

For Kittler, the decisive medium is 'hardware.' Software is merely a superficial description of how hardware operates; true determination occurs in the physical states of electronic circuits, transistors, and memory cells. He famously declared that "there is no software," meaning that all software operations can ultimately be reduced to electrical signals in microprocessors—physical state changes in hardware. From this perspective, the digital computer is a universal medium performing all symbol processing on a single material foundation (silicon chips), constituting a 'hardware a priori' in itself.

However, examining the operating principles of generative AI reveals difficulties faced by this hardware reductionism. The output of a generative AI model is not simply the execution of CPU instructions but is generated through a sampling process over a probabilistic distribution composed of billions of parameters (weights). Here, the determining factors are less the hardware's clock speed or memory capacity and more the model architecture (transformers, diffusion models), training data distribution, loss function design, and the embedding vectors of prompts—software conditions. Of course, this software runs on hardware, but the fact that changes in model weights or subtle differences in prompts on the same hardware produce entirely different outputs indicates that the locus of 'determination' has shifted from hardware to a complex network of software, data, and algorithms.

## 3. Conditions of Generative AI: Model Weights, Data Distribution, Prompt Embeddings

The conditions determining the output of generative AI can be analyzed at three levels. First, **model weights** are billions of real-valued matrices learned during training, a kind of 'technical unconscious' that compresses patterns in input data. While Kittler's hardware determinism emphasized the material structure of media, model weights represent materiality abstracted into numerical form. Second, **data distribution** refers to the statistical characteristics of the training corpus, reflecting collective patterns in large-scale human-generated content such as internet text, images, and audio. This data distribution is not mere 'information' but an accumulation of signs produced under specific cultural, historical, and social conditions, functioning similarly to Foucault's 'discourse formation.' Third, **prompt embeddings** transform user input into a vector space, acting as a 'trigger' that induces conditional probabilities for model output.

These three conditions are closely interconnected. Prompt embeddings activate attention mechanisms through dot products with model weights, generating output based on patterns learned from data distribution. Thus, Kittler's 'determination by the medium' is now mediated not by a single material layer of 'hardware' but by a triple software condition of 'weights-data-prompts.' This does not completely negate Kittler's determinism but suggests that the locus and mode of determination must be expanded. Hardware remains a necessary condition but is no longer sufficient. In generative AI, the materiality of the medium exists in the form of 'abstract materiality.'

## 4. Simondon's Concept of Concretisation and the Ontology of Generative AI

Gilbert Simondon's ontology of technical objects provides a useful conceptual framework to complement Kittler's determinism. In *Du mode d'existence des objets techniques* (On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects, 1958), Simondon argued that technical objects evolve from an abstract stage to a concrete stage, forming interdependent functional networks among internal components. For example, early steam engines had parts functioning separately, while later internal combustion engines tightly integrate components, each presupposing the operating conditions of others. This concretisation process proceeds as technical objects adapt to their environment and achieve higher efficiency and stability through internal resonance.

Generative AI models exemplify Simondon's concept of concretisation in a new way. Early neural network models (e.g., simple perceptrons) remained at an abstract stage, but the Transformer architecture integrates attention mechanisms, layer normalization, and residual connections into an organic system. During training, model weights are precisely adjusted to 'resonate' with the statistical characteristics of data distribution. This can be seen as a digital version of what Simondon called the 'individuation of technical objects.'

However, the concretisation of generative AI is 'synthetic' unlike natural objects, and its environment consists of human-designed data distributions and hardware infrastructure. Importantly, Simondon's concretisation is not mere progress but includes the process by which technical objects actively reconfigure their environment. In generative AI, a 'synthetic data feedback loop' can occur where model-generated output is reused as training data. This connects to Simondon's concept of the 'technical milieu.'

## 5. Synthetic Data Feedback Loops and Model Collapse: The Risk of Degenerative Closed Loops

Synthetic data feedback loops are a significant phenomenon in generative AI. When text or images generated by a model are included in the training corpus for subsequent models, a self-referential cycle forms. Theoretically, this implies a 'recursive' structure where a technical object uses its own output as input, which could be interpreted as Simondon's concretisation progressing toward higher integration. That is, the model could expand its environment through its own creations and learn more sophisticated patterns.

However, research suggests that repeated use of synthetic data can lead to **model collapse**. Model collapse refers to the phenomenon where models trained on synthetic data gradually lose diversity in the original data distribution, converging on specific patterns or repeating meaningless outputs. This is akin to 'informational diversity loss,' similar to how invasive species destroy native biodiversity in ecosystems. This phenomenon shows that Simondon's concretisation is not always evolutionary. Rather, technical objects can fall into a positive feedback loop of degeneration that degrades their own operating conditions.

Connecting this to Kittler's determinism, synthetic data feedback loops suggest a new form of determinism: 'the medium determines its own conditions.' That is, the medium of generative AI generates its own future conditions (data distribution) through its output, opening the possibility of 'software self-determination' beyond hardware determinism. However, this self-determination simultaneously carries the risk of leading to a degenerative closed loop, reaffirming Simondon's insight that technical objects must maintain open interaction with their environment.

## 6. Comparing the Evolution and Degenerative Closed Loops of Technical Objects

Here, we can contrast two interpretive paths. First, from the **perspective of technical evolution**, synthetic data feedback loops in generative AI can be interpreted as progressing toward higher concretisation. The model expanding data distribution through its output, generating new patterns, and acquiring richer expressiveness aligns with Simondon's model of technical objects evolving through interaction with their environment. In this case, model collapse is considered a temporary anomaly, overcome by appropriate data mixing strategies (e.g., balancing human-generated and synthetic data).

Second, from the **perspective of degenerative closed loops**, synthetic data feedback loops are interpreted as a dangerous process leading to self-enclosure and diversity loss in technical objects. This can be seen as extending Kittler's hardware determinism's 'closure' to the software layer. That is, when a medium determines its own conditions, that determination reduces the entropy of probabilistic distributions through mere repetition, potentially leading to 'informational death.' This connects to Vilém Flusser's critique of the 'crisis of technical imagination,' similar to how technical images (tecnimagems) lose contact with reality while reproducing themselves.

These two interpretations are not mutually exclusive. Rather, the current state of generative AI is a transitional phase where both tendencies coexist. Importantly, the evolution of technical objects is not automatically guaranteed; external intervention (human curation, ethical standards, maintaining data diversity) is needed to prevent degenerative closed loops. This reminds us that Simondon's concept of concretisation is not mere technological determinism but an open process involving the relationship between technical objects and humans.

## 7. Implications for Eco-Digital and Artificial Nature Research

The above discussion provides important implications for 'eco-digital' or 'artificial nature' research. Eco-digital refers to hybrid environments of digital technology and natural ecosystems, emphasizing that technical objects act not as mere tools but as active elements constituting the environment. Generative AI is emerging as a key component of data ecosystems in such hybrid environments.

First, synthetic data feedback loops in generative AI alter the 'information cycle' of digital ecosystems. Just as matter and energy cycle in natural ecosystems, data cycles in digital ecosystems. However, synthetic data is like 'artificial nutrients'; excessive use can destroy ecosystem diversity. This suggests the need to establish criteria for evaluating 'data ecosystem health' in eco-digital research.

Second, by reconfiguring Kittler's determinism, we can newly understand the 'material conditions' of artificial nature. Artificial nature is not simply nature made by humans but an 'autopoietic' system where technical objects generate and maintain their own conditions. Generative AI demonstrates the most advanced form of this autopoiesis, while also warning of the risk that its closed loops could lead to system-wide degeneration.

Third, eco-digital research provides an analytical framework through Simondon's concept of concretisation that can simultaneously capture the evolution and degeneration of generative AI. This recognizes that technical objects do not simply progress or regress but can proceed in both directions under specific conditions. Therefore, the design and operation of generative AI should aim not merely at maximizing efficiency but at maintaining 'openness' and 'diversity.'

## 8. Conclusion: Beyond Kittler's Determinism

Is Kittler's hardware determinism still valid in the era of generative AI? The answer to this question is 'partially valid, but reconfiguration is needed.' Hardware remains a necessary condition for generative AI; the computational power of GPU clusters, memory bandwidth, and communication latency determine the scale and speed of models. However, hardware is no longer a sufficient condition. The output of generative AI is more directly determined by software conditions—model weights, data distribution, and prompt embeddings—which form a layer of 'abstract materiality' built upon the materiality of hardware.

Thus, Kittler's determinism must be reconfigured from the proposition that 'the hardware conditions of the medium define the conditions of possibility for the message' to 'the software-hardware complex of the medium is an autopoietic system that simultaneously generates its own conditions and messages.' This is supported by Simondon's concept of concretisation and the analysis of synthetic data feedback loops. Generative AI embodies both the potential for technical object evolution and the risk of degenerative closed loops, providing key insights for understanding the conditions of artificial nature in eco-digital research.

In conclusion, Kittler's hardware determinism has not completely lost its validity in the era of generative AI but must be expanded to more complex layers. The medium still determines the message. However, the locus of that determination has shifted from a single hardware to the interior of a network where weights, data, and prompts are intertwined. This network has the capacity to reproduce and evolve itself, but simultaneously faces the risk of self-closure. Therefore, media aesthetics in the age of technogenesis must recognize this dual potential and move toward exploring conditions that can maintain openness and diversity.
