## 1. Precise Location of the Problem: Tension Between Relational Ontology and Normative Asymmetry

The Whole-Emergent WE is a relational field co-produced by humans and technical actors. This concept was born from your own synthesis, traversing Simondon's **transindividuation** and Kittler's **medial conditionality**. At the ontological level, WE is a relational entity that mutually constitutes humans and technology, irreducible to either side.

However, a crucial problem arises here.

> "Just because relations are entangled, is responsibility equally divided?"

This question carries the following implication: **Ontological relationality does not imply normative symmetry.** The fact that something occurs within a relational field is a separate issue from whether the actors within that field possess the same level of power, autonomy, information, and visibility. The error of equating these two is found in both techno-solutionism and human exceptionalism.

## 2. Asymmetry of Design Power — Three Levels

### 2.1. Epistemic Asymmetry: The Problem of Visibility

WE is a relational field, but access to the constitutive conditions of this field is not equal between humans and technical actors.

Technical actors (algorithms, recommendation systems, generative AI models) are characterized by **operational opacity**. This may be intended closure (trade secrets, algorithmic black boxes) or a consequence of structural complexity (non-interpretability of deep learning). In either case, human users are structurally influenced by the system's outputs without knowing its internal workings.

This is the first power asymmetry you identified in the July 28 conversation: **epistemic asymmetry**. Even though WE is a relational field, knowledge of the conditions of this field is hidden from humans and visible only to technical actors (or the designers and corporations behind them).

### 2.2. Structural Asymmetry: Pre-determination of Options

The second level is **decision-making authority asymmetry**.

Kittler's media ontology shows that technical systems are not merely tools but prescribe the very conditions of human action. The UI of software, the accessibility of data infrastructure, platform rules, algorithm weights — all of these are **design decisions**, and these decisions pre-structure the range of choices for human users.

Humans can only act within these structured options. Technical actors have the power to constitute these options. Here, the asymmetry of responsibility arises: **Those who constitute the options and those who choose within those options cannot bear the same responsibility.**

This is not merely an instrumental relationship. From the perspective of WE, both subjects are co-produced within a relationship. However, the **condition-setting** of that production process lies with the technical actor (and its institutional sponsors), not with the human. This condition-setting power is the axis of asymmetry in responsibility distribution.

### 2.3. Ontological Asymmetry: The Problem of Agency and Attribution

The third level is the **ontological asymmetry** you identified.

Technical actors possess agency. In Simondon's terms, they have a 'mode of existence.' However, the nature of this agency is fundamentally different from human agency. Technical actors **lack intentionality**. The attribution of legal and ethical responsibility has traditionally been linked to intentionality.

But the crucial point here is not whether technical actors have intentions, but **how agency without intentionality intervenes in the field of responsibility**. Technical actors act, but the meaning and consequences of those actions only arise within the relational field of human-technology. If we assign responsibility for outcomes arising in this relational field, can we attribute 'responsibility' to an actor without intentionality?

Your approach handles this problem elegantly: **Responsibility is attributed solely to humans, but the scope and conditions of that responsibility are constrained by technical conditionality.** This is a third term between human exceptionalism (only humans are subjects of responsibility) and relational egalitarianism (all actors are equally subjects of responsibility).

## 3. Structure of the Argument: 'Relationally Arises but Asymmetrically Distributed'

Synthesizing the above discussion, the following argument can be constructed.

**Premise 1:** The Whole-Emergent WE is a relational field co-produced by humans and technical actors. (Ontological relationality)

**Premise 2:** The conditions that constitute this relational field (design power, operating conditions, information accessibility) are asymmetrically distributed between humans and technical actors. (Asymmetry of power)

**Premise 3:** Responsibility should be distributed in proportion not only to the attribution of actions but also to the control over the conditions of action. (Principle of correspondence between control and responsibility)

**Conclusion:** Responsibility in WE arises relationally but is distributed asymmetrically. Human users bear responsibility for the outcomes of the relationship, but technical actors bear responsibility for the power that constitutes the conditions of the relationship — and the nature and scope of these two responsibilities are qualitatively different.

## 4. Esther and Response-ability: Supplementary Implications

The Estherian concept of 'responsibility' in Document 4 — i.e., a specific individual's 'response' to the 'call' of the general public — offers supplementary implications here. For Esther, responsibility is not a subject's voluntary choice but is constituted by the call of the Other. I do not choose responsibility; I am **constituted** as a responsible subject by the call of the Other.

Applying this to your discussion: The technical system can be seen as 'calling' humans in a specific way. The platform's UI calls me as a 'consumer,' a 'data provider,' a 'content producer.' By responding to this call, I become a specific subject of responsibility. However, this call itself is not chosen by me but is pre-conditioned by technical design.

Here, a point of convergence between Esther and your analysis emerges: the power to design the conditions of the call is the true axis of responsibility. And this power is asymmetrically distributed within WE.

## 5. Cautions Against Three Misunderstandings

To avoid misunderstanding this argument, three points must be clarified.

**First, this argument is not human exceptionalism.**
Attributing responsibility only to humans does not deny the agency of technology. On the contrary, only by accurately recognizing the agency of technology can we analyze how that agency conditions the scope of human responsibility.

**Second, this argument is not technological determinism.**
Emphasizing the asymmetry of design power does not mean that humans are passively determined by technology. WE is a relational field, and technical conditionality does not completely extinguish human agency. Rather, human agency becomes possible only when it recognizes this conditionality and secures a critical distance from it.

**Third, this argument does not complete the distribution of responsibility.**
The specific distribution of responsibility must be determined according to the conditions of each case. What this argument provides is not 'how' to distribute responsibility, but the structural reason 'why' equal distribution is impossible.

## 6. Connection to Your Previous Analysis: Ethical Implications of the Four Levels of Asymmetry

In the July 28 conversation, you already identified four layers of asymmetry: epistemic, decision-making, ontological, and institutional. These four layers can directly serve as analytical tools for the problem of responsibility distribution.

| Asymmetry Layer | Implication for Responsibility Distribution |
|---|---|
| Epistemic asymmetry | Those with knowledge (designers/corporations) bear greater responsibility for the predictability of outcomes |
| Decision-making asymmetry | Those who set the conditions bear primary responsibility for the consequences of those conditions |
| Ontological asymmetry | Intentionality is attributed to humans, but agency also exists in technology → Responsibility lies with humans, conditions are shaped by the agency of both sides |
| Institutional asymmetry | Legal and economic incentive structures distort responsibility → Requires critical responsibility distribution for the structure itself |

Each of these four layers requires separate analysis, but together they show that the distribution of responsibility in WE must be a distribution according to an 'asymmetric chain of conditions and consequences,' not an 'equal division.'
