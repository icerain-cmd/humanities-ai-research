# Media Aesthetics-Memory in the Age of Technogenesis: Extending Stiegler's Tertiary Retention Concept and Its Transformation under Platform Capitalism

## 1. Introduction: The Exteriorization of Memory and Technical Conditions

Bernard Stiegler's theory of memory reappropriates Husserl's phenomenology of time-consciousness from a philosophy of technology perspective, offering the insight that human memory is essentially constituted through technical mediation. His concept of tertiary retention designates the layer of memory objectified in external storage media—writing, photographs, recordings, digital records—beyond primary retention (the retention of the perceptual present) and secondary retention (reproduction through recollection and imagination), which remain within individual consciousness. This concept reveals that technology is not a mere tool but a "pharmacological" being that transforms the very structure of human cognition. That is, technology can be simultaneously remedy (pharmakon) and poison.

However, the emergence of generative AI demands a fundamental reexamination of this concept. Whereas existing tertiary retention was based on static records (texts, images, databases), generative AI is a dynamic system that "generates" new content based on learned data. This is not simply the storage of memory, but the enabling of memory's recombination and predictive generation. This paper analyzes how Stiegler's tertiary retention concept must be extended in the age of generative AI, and what transformations the pharmacological structure of this retention undergoes under platform capitalism. In particular, by connecting Gilbert Simondon's concept of concretization, the synthetic data feedback loop of generative AI, and the phenomenon of model collapse, it will compare two interpretations—the evolution of technical objects versus degenerative closed loops—and derive implications for eco-digital/artificial nature research.

## 2. Stiegler's Tertiary Retention: Pharmacological Structure and Technical Conditions

### 2.1. The Three Layers of Retention and Technical Mediation

Stiegler critically inherits Husserl's *Phenomenology of Internal Time-Consciousness*, arguing that the temporal synthesis of consciousness is impossible without technical objects. Primary retention is the perceptual retention that grasps the moment that has just passed in the "here-now," and secondary retention is the reproduction of memory that recalls past experience. However, these two layers alone cannot explain cultural transmission and historical accumulation. Tertiary retention is memory inscribed in material objects—writing, tools, architecture, recording media—that enables the accumulation of collective knowledge beyond the biological limits of the individual.

For Stiegler, technology is the means by which the "exteriorization of memory" is realized. Humanity has continuously projected its memory outward, from stone tools to writing, printing, and digital media. In this process, technology is not a mere auxiliary but reconstructs the very structure of human cognition. For example, the invention of writing enabled analytical thought and abstraction in societies that had relied on oral memory, while digital media have facilitated real-time information processing and network-based thinking.

### 2.2. The Duality of Pharmacology

The concept of the "pharmakon," which Stiegler borrows from Plato's *Phaedrus*, reveals the essential duality of technology. Technology is simultaneously remedy and poison. Writing supplements memory but can simultaneously bring about the decline of memory itself. Digital technology revolutionizes access to information but also induces attention fragmentation and cognitive laziness. This pharmacological structure suggests that technological development does not always produce positive outcomes; rather, its effects can be contradictory depending on the mode of use and social context.

Stiegler particularly criticizes the phenomenon in the late 20th-century "hyperindustrial society" wherein technology is subsumed under the logic of capital and functions as "poison." The culture industry, advertising, and digital platforms commodify individual attention, standardize emotions and desires, and paralyze critical thought. This is a phenomenon in which technology's inherent "remedial" dimension—namely, the democratization of knowledge and the expansion of creativity—is suppressed.

## 3. Generative AI and the Extension of Tertiary Retention: Dynamic Generation and Predictive Memory

### 3.1. From Static Records to Dynamic Generation

Existing tertiary retention relied primarily on static recording media. Books, photographs, recordings, and databases fix specific moments of the past and allow users to reproduce or reference them when needed. Generative AI, however, fundamentally transcends this static character. Large language models (LLMs) and image generation models "generate" new texts, images, and music based on patterns in trained data. This is not mere reproduction but a process of producing content that did not previously exist through probabilistic prediction and combination.

This generative process demands a new layer of tertiary retention. Stiegler's original concept presupposes memory fixed in external storage media, but generative AI operates a "predictive memory" that goes beyond stored data. That is, AI "remembers" possible future outputs by learning statistical patterns from past data. This requires the concept of a "fourth retention" or "generative retention" that goes beyond primary retention (perceptual present), secondary retention (recollection), and tertiary retention (external storage).

### 3.2. Synthetic Data Feedback Loops and Model Collapse

One of the core mechanisms of generative AI is the synthetic data feedback loop. The circular structure in which outputs generated by an AI model are reused as training data can initially expand data diversity, but in the long term poses the risk of model collapse. Model collapse refers to the phenomenon in which AI, by repeatedly learning from data it has itself generated, gradually drifts away from the distribution of original data, ultimately producing only biased and monotonous outputs.

This phenomenon is deeply connected to Stiegler's pharmacological structure. Generative AI can initially function as a "remedy" that expands human creativity. For example, writers can use AI to obtain new ideas, or researchers can use it to summarize vast bodies of literature. However, when the synthetic data feedback loop persists, AI gradually fails to reflect original human expression and becomes trapped in the statistical average of its own outputs. This demonstrates the process by which technology deteriorates into "poison."

What is more problematic is that under platform capitalism, this feedback loop is deliberately accelerated. Social media, search engines, and content recommendation systems optimize algorithms to maximize user clicks and viewing time. In this process, AI reinforces users' existing preferences and forms "filter bubbles" that block new experiences or heterodox perspectives. Consequently, generative AI reduces human cognitive diversity and reinforces the circulation of homogenized content.

## 4. Simondon's Concretization Concept and the Evolution of Technical Objects

### 4.1. The Process of Concretization and Generative AI

The concept of "concretization" presented in Gilbert Simondon's *On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects* explains the process by which a technical object, from an initial abstract stage, gradually comes to have its internal components mutually coordinated and functions integrated. For example, in early automobiles, the engine, body, and wheels were designed independently, but gradually evolved into a stage of concretization where each part is optimized in consideration of the others' functions.

The development of generative AI can be viewed as an instance of this concretization process. Early language models relied on simple statistical pattern matching, but through advances such as the transformer architecture, self-attention mechanisms, and large-scale pretraining, they have gradually enabled more sophisticated language understanding and generation. Furthermore, multimodal models (processing text, images, and audio simultaneously) exemplify concretization in which diverse functions are integrated into a single system.

### 4.2. The Dual Interpretation of Evolution and Regression

However, caution is needed when applying Simondon's concretization concept to generative AI. Concretization generally proceeds in a direction that increases the efficiency and adaptability of technical objects, but under platform capitalism this process can become distorted. When the evolution of technical objects is driven by the logic of capital accumulation rather than user benefit, concretization can instead lead to a degenerative closed loop.

For example, recommendation algorithms are concretized to increase user satisfaction, but simultaneously reinforce a "lock-in effect" that fixes users to specific content. This is a phenomenon in which the technical object, rather than adapting to its external environment (users' diverse needs), becomes closed off by its own logic. In Simondon's terms, this is closer to the "self-reproduction of a technical system" than to the "evolution of a technical object."

The synthetic data feedback loop of generative AI is a representative case of this degenerative closure. As AI repeatedly learns from its own outputs, the diversity of original data gradually disappears, and the model becomes trapped in specific patterns. This can lead to a state of "technological autism," in which the technical object, instead of evolving through interaction with the external world, becomes isolated through internal circulation.

## 5. Pharmacological Transformation under Platform Capitalism

### 5.1. The Attention Economy and the Exteriorization of Emotion

Platform capitalism is an extreme form of the "hyperindustrial society" Stiegler warned about. In this system, human attention, emotion, and judgment are all commodified. Generative AI functions as a key instrument accelerating this commodification process. For example, social media platforms use AI to analyze users' emotional states and optimize content accordingly. Based on research findings that negative emotions such as anger, anxiety, and fear drive higher engagement rates, algorithms deliberately prioritize content that maximizes emotional response.

This suggests that Stiegler's tertiary retention concept must be extended to the "exteriorization of emotion." Existing tertiary retention focused primarily on the storage of cognitive content (knowledge, information), but generative AI datafies and reproduces emotion itself. Users' emotional responses are collected as training data for AI and then used to generate content that induces emotion. In this process, emotion is separated from the individual's inner experience and transformed into an object manipulable by algorithms.

### 5.2. The Exteriorization of Judgment and Cognitive Laziness

Another significant impact of generative AI is the exteriorization of judgment. Search engines, recommendation systems, and automatic summarization tools reduce the need for users to evaluate and judge information themselves. For example, when a student uses AI to write an essay, or a researcher directly cites AI-generated literature summaries, this risks weakening individual critical thinking.

Stiegler called this phenomenon "cognitive proletarianization." As technology replaces the process of judgment, humans lose their cognitive capacities and become subordinated to the technical system. Generative AI intensifies this process further. Users become accustomed to accepting AI-provided answers without criticism and may lose the ability to formulate questions and seek solutions themselves.

However, what is important here is that this phenomenon is not an inevitable consequence of technology itself, but is promoted by the specific modes of use under platform capitalism. If AI is used in a transparent and responsible manner in public domains such as education, healthcare, and research, it could instead supplement and expand human judgment. The problem arises when the design and deployment of AI are driven by platform profitability rather than user autonomy.

### 5.3. The Exteriorization of Knowledge and the Epistemological Crisis

Generative AI is transforming the very mode of knowledge production and circulation. Traditionally, knowledge was accumulated through academic papers, books, and expert verification. However, AI-generated content bypasses this verification process and is disseminated to the public. When AI produces "hallucinations" that mix fact and fiction, or generates distorted information based on biased data, this can lead to an epistemological crisis.

This issue suggests that Stiegler's tertiary retention concept must be able to explain the "generative distortion of knowledge" beyond the "exteriorization of knowledge." Existing tertiary retention presupposed the accuracy and reliability of recorded knowledge, but generative AI renders knowledge itself fluid and unstable. This means that the pharmacological structure of technology has been extended to a new dimension. AI can serve as a "remedy" by increasing access to knowledge, but can simultaneously function as "poison" by undermining the reliability of knowledge.

## 6. Implications for Eco-Digital/Artificial Nature Research

### 6.1. The Relationship between Technical Objects and Ecosystems

Combining Simondon's concretization concept with Stiegler's pharmacology, there arises a need to understand generative AI not as a mere tool but as a "technological ecosystem." AI is part of a complex system in which human users, data, algorithms, platforms, and regulatory frameworks interact. The health of this ecosystem depends on the balance and mutual coordination of each element.

Eco-digital research assumes that such technological ecosystems operate on principles similar to natural ecosystems. That is, just as diversity, circularity, and adaptability determine the sustainability of an ecosystem, technological ecosystems also require diversity of perspectives, circulation of data, and adaptation to changing environments. The fact that generative AI's synthetic data feedback loop leads to model collapse can be seen as a form of "ecosystem collapse" that occurs when the diversity of a technological ecosystem is lost.

### 6.2. The Constitution of Artificial Nature and the Role of Humans

The concept of "artificial nature" offers the insight that technology does not simply imitate nature but creates a new kind of naturalness. The content produced by generative AI is becoming so sophisticated that it is difficult to distinguish from human creations, opening a new domain of the "artificial." For example, AI-generated artworks, music, and literature raise the need to redefine existing aesthetic standards.

However, the constitution of artificial nature is impossible without active human intervention. Stiegler argues that technology does not replace humans; rather, a "co-individuation" of humans and technology is necessary. That is, humans must not passively accept technological development but actively participate in the design and use of technology. The same applies to generative AI. To prevent AI from being driven solely by the logic of capital, democratic control in the public sphere, transparent algorithm design, and user digital literacy education are necessary.

### 6.3. Proposals for Restoring Pharmacological Balance

Stiegler's pharmacology, while recognizing the duality of technology, leaves open the possibility that technology can be directed toward "remedy." To restore the pharmacological balance of generative AI, the following conditions are needed.

First, data diversity and transparency must be ensured. The training data of AI models must be constructed so as not to be biased toward particular groups or perspectives, and the sources and processing procedures of data must be transparently disclosed. This is a key condition for preventing the synthetic data feedback loop from leading to model collapse.

Second, AI must be designed in a direction that strengthens user autonomy and critical thinking. AI should function not as a mere tool that provides answers, but as a "cognitive partner" that supports users in formulating their own questions and making judgments. For example, interface design is needed in which AI presents multiple alternatives and encourages users to compare and evaluate them.

Third, technological development must be regulated and governed for the public good. So that the logic of platform capitalism does not monopolize the direction of technological development, governments, academia, and civil society must cooperate to establish a framework for the ethical use of AI. This is an institutional mechanism for preventing the pharmacological structure of technology from deteriorating into "poison."

## 7. Conclusion: The Politics of Memory in the Age of Generative AI

Stiegler's tertiary retention concept is being called upon to undergo a fundamental extension in the age of generative AI. From static records to dynamic generation, from cognitive content to the exteriorization of emotion and judgment, and from the storage of knowledge to the generative distortion of knowledge, the concept of retention is becoming increasingly complex. This extension shows that the pharmacological structure of technology is undergoing new transformations under platform capitalism.

Simondon's concretization concept provides a framework for understanding the development of generative AI as the evolution of technical objects, but simultaneously warns of the risk that this evolution may lead to a degenerative closed loop. The synthetic data feedback loop and model collapse are pathological phenomena that occur when the diversity of a technological ecosystem is lost. This confirms the importance of the ecological approach emphasized by eco-digital research.

Ultimately, memory in the age of generative AI is no longer an individual's inner experience or an object fixed in external storage media. It is a dynamic process constantly being reconstituted among humans and technology, capital and the public sphere, diversity and standardization. What matters in this process is not technological development itself, but the social, political, and ethical conditions under which that development takes place. As Stiegler emphasized, technology is never neutral. It always embodies specific values and power relations, shaping our memory and identity. Therefore, the politics of memory in the age of generative AI must be a work of asking who, for what purposes, and in what ways memory is generated and circulated, and which memories are excluded and distorted in this process.

To answer these questions, interdisciplinary research that applies the conceptual tools of philosophy of technology and media theory to concrete cases in reality is necessary. This paper has sought to present a theoretical framework for how media aesthetics-memory in the age of technogenesis should be reconstituted by connecting the theories of Stiegler and Simondon to the phenomena of generative AI. This framework is not a completed answer but will function as a starting point for future discussion.
