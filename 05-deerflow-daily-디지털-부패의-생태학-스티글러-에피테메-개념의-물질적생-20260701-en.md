> Bernard Stiegler's concept of epiphylogenesis — does the ecology of digital decay demand a material-ecological reconfiguration of tertiary retention?

## Question

Bernard Stiegler, in *Technics and Time (La Technique et le Temps)*, diagnosed epiphylogenesis — the accumulation of memory through technical exteriorization — as humanity's unique evolutionary condition. Yet the replacement cycle of smartphones, the discontinuation of SaaS platforms, and the obsolescence of file formats — i.e., the planned obsolescence of digital objects — obstruct the accumulation of epiphylogenesis that Stiegler positively evaluated. Unlike physical artifacts (stone tools, books), digital objects experience not 'sedimentation of knowledge' but 'digital decay' due to storage medium degradation, platform dependency, and format evolution. Does the ecology of digital decay — the organic dissipation of information — demand a material-ecological reconfiguration of Stiegler's concept of tertiary retention? In other words, should epiphylogenesis be thought not only as a condition of accumulation but also as a condition of dissipation?

## Key Findings

# The Ecology of Digital Decay: Toward a Material-Ecological Reconfiguration of Stiegler's Epiphylogenesis

## 1. Problem Setting: The Hidden Presuppositions of Stieglerian Epiphylogenesis

Stiegler's concept of epiphylogenesis is grounded in the insight that humanity has accumulated transgenerational memory through technological exteriorization. In Volume 1 of *Technics and Time*, he sought to capture a uniquely human mechanism of cultural evolution that could not be explained by organic evolution (epigenesis) alone. The conditions Stiegler implicitly assumes for epiphylogenesis to function are as follows:

**(a) Physical durability:** Technical objects must be able to physically endure beyond the era of their creation. A hand axe lasts tens of thousands of years, a parchment manuscript survives centuries, and a printed book, under proper conditions, endures for hundreds of years.

**(b) Carrier-content inseparability:** In pre-digital technical objects, content and carrier are inseparable. The use-wear on a stone tool is etched into that stone; the letters of a manuscript are inscribed on that parchment. This inseparability guarantees the object's 'authenticity' and 'originality.'

**(c) Environment independence:** Physical artifacts can be read or function in and of themselves, independent of the environment in which they are found. The Rosetta Stone, when discovered in 1799, already contained its information within itself; only additional tools (philological knowledge) were needed for its decipherment.

**(d) Gradual sedimentation:** Epiphylogenesis accumulates as layers of time are deposited onto material objects. Subsequent generations maintain cultural continuity by excavating and reinterpreting this sedimented memory.

These four conditions were not explicitly thematized in Stiegler's discussion but rather constituted the ontological characteristics of physical artifacts he naturally presupposed in his case analyses. The starting point of this analysis is how these presuppositions are dismantled in the face of digital objects.

## 2. The Ontological Singularity of Digital Objects

Digital objects violate all four of the above conditions. The nature of this violation must be understood not as mere 'difference' but as an 'ontological rupture.'

### 2.1 Carrier-Content Separation and the Loss of Originality

The most fundamental ontological characteristic of digital objects is the **separation of the bit-string from its physical carrier**. The same PDF file can simultaneously exist on an SSD, HDD, cloud server, and USB memory, and among these copies, the distinction between 'original' and 'copy' does not exist. This arises from the digital object's ontological property of **zero marginal replication cost**.

This separation yields the following consequences:
- **The disappearance of originality:** Walter Benjamin's diagnosis of the loss of aura in the age of mechanical reproduction becomes a structural necessity for digital objects.
- **The loss of indexicality:** Borrowing from Charles Sanders Peirce's semiotics, pre-digital artifacts bear indexical traces of their time and place of origin. The specific use-wear of a particular hand axe, the ink bleeding of a particular manuscript — these testify to the object's unique historicity. Digital objects, allowing only identical copies of zeros and ones, possess no such indexical traces.

### 2.2 Execution Environment Dependency and Virtualized Existence

The most radical characteristic of digital objects is their **total dependence on an execution environment**. A .doc file stored on a floppy disk requires a compatible operating system, application, and hardware to be 'actualized' as meaningful information. While a physical book can be read directly by the eye in ambient light, digital objects have a **separation between virtual existence and actual existence**.

This dependency operates on three layers:

1. **Hardware layer:** Evolution of physical interfaces (IDE → SATA → NVMe), drive mechanisms (magnetic tape → HDD → SSD → flash)
2. **Software layer:** Changes in operating systems (MS-DOS → Windows 95 → Windows 11), file formats (.doc → .docx → .docm), encoding schemes (EUC-KR → UTF-8)
3. **Platform layer:** Cloud service lifecycles (Google Reader → discontinued), social media (MySpace → Facebook → TikTok), SaaS tool lifecycles

This triple dependency fundamentally reframes the ontological question of what a digital object 'is.' A digital object is a relational being that 'exists' only when the entire hardware-software-platform network is operational.

### 2.3 The Structural Internalization of Planned Obsolescence

The 2-3 year replacement cycle of smartphones, the discontinuation of SaaS platforms, and the obsolescence of file formats are not merely technical evolution but the **operating mode of capitalist value realization**. The critical point is that this planned obsolescence is internalized into the very 'essence' of the digital object.

The obsolescence of physical artifacts is **accidental**. A book can get wet, burned, eaten by insects, or chemically degraded by acidic paper — but these are consequences of external conditions, not of the book's 'mode of being.' In contrast, the obsolescence of digital objects is **structural**. File format obsolescence arises from the evolutionary logic of the format itself; SaaS service termination is the consequence of platform business models. In other words, the digital object carries the seeds of its own dissolution within its very conditions of existence.

## 3. The Four Layers of Digital Decay

To reconfigure Stiegler's concept of tertiary retention under digital conditions, I decompose 'digital decay' into four layers. This division is for analytical clarity; in actual phenomena, these layers operate in conjunction.

### 3.1 Layer 1: Carrier Decay

The carriers of digital objects exist physically and therefore undergo physical degradation: HDD platter wear, SSD cell lifespan limits (PE cycles), CD-R dye deterioration (5-10 year lifespan), flash memory charge leakage, etc.

**Characteristic:** This layer superficially resembles the physical decay of pre-digital artifacts, but there is a decisive difference. Physical artifacts (stone tools, pottery) preserve most of their information even with partial damage (e.g., the Rosetta Stone's cracks notwithstanding, most of its inscription remains legible). In contrast, damage to digital storage media is **critical** — a single bit error can render an entire file unreadable. This is due to the **discreteness** of digital information.

### 3.2 Layer 2: Platform Decay

The termination of SaaS platforms, social networks, and cloud services represents the most emblematic form of digital object dissolution. Google Reader (2013), MySpace (massive music data loss, 2019), Yahoo! GeoCities (2009), Vine (2016) — countless platforms have terminated their services, resulting in the mass annihilation of user data.

**Characteristic:** At this layer, digital objects are physically preserved (the data remains on server hard disks), but the access infrastructure disappears, leaving them in a state of 'existing but inaccessible.' This is the digital instantiation of what Paul Virilio called the 'desertification of the real' — the ontological ambiguity of that which can no longer be perceived. The data still exists but no longer **presents** itself.

**Case analysis — Google Reader:**
- Launched 2005, terminated July 1, 2013
- Approximately 300 million RSS feed subscriptions were annihilated
- Part of user data could be exported via Google Takeout, but the network relationships between feeds (the social layer) became unrecoverable
- This case demonstrates that digital objects are built not merely upon data but upon **relationality**. Individual data may survive, but the meaning of the object dissolves along with the relational network.

### 3.3 Layer 3: Format Obsolescence

File format evolution is the most insidious form of digital decay. Formats do not explicitly 'die,' but gradually become unreadable as current software ceases to support them. This layer is particularly noteworthy among forms of digital decay because the data appears to be 'preserved.'

**Historical examples of format obsolescence:**
- WordStar .wsd (1978): effectively obsolete since the early 1990s
- Lotus 1-2-3 .wk1 (1983): unreadable after the 2000s
- Corel WordPerfect .wpd: support discontinued in current Word
- Flash .swf (1996-2020): official Adobe support ended December 31, 2020
- QuickTime .mov (legacy codecs): unplayable on current macOS

**Characteristic:** Format obsolescence takes the form of 'slow violence' (Rob Nixon's concept). It is not a catastrophic event but a gradual progression over time, reaching an unrecoverable state beyond a certain threshold. This resembles the natural decay of physical artifacts (acidic paper decomposition, photographic fading), but the decisive difference is that format obsolescence is the result of **techno-social decisions**. Acidic paper decomposition is a natural process; the obsolescence of .docx format is the result of Microsoft's strategic decisions.

### 3.4 Layer 4: Semantic Context Decay

This layer is the most subtle yet the most severe form of digital decay. Even when data is technically fully preserved, the format remains readable, and the platform continues to operate, the cultural and cognitive context necessary for producing and interpreting that data can disappear.

**Examples:**
- The HTML/CSS code of personal homepages from the 1990s is fully preserved, but without context of the era's web aesthetics, networking culture (webrings, guestbook culture), and information retrieval practices (Yahoo directory-based search), it is impossible to understand why these pages were structured in particular ways.
- Early digital art works (.exe format, interactive) have their executable files preserved, but without understanding their relationship to the era's hardware speed (CPU, RAM), monitor resolution (640×480 CRT), and input devices (keyboard-centric rather than mouse), the intended experience cannot be reproduced.

**Theoretical implications:** This layer signifies the collapse of the cognitive circulation that Stiegler described through the relationship between tertiary retention, primary retention (perceptual experience), and secondary retention (memory and the unconscious). The technical data of the digital object (tertiary retention) remains, but the conditions of primary and secondary retention — through which it could be perceptually experienced and imbued with meaning — have disappeared.

## 4. Ecological Reconfiguration of Stieglerian Tertiary Retention

The foregoing analysis suggests that Stiegler's concept of tertiary retention requires a fundamental reconfiguration under digital conditions. I propose three directions for this reconfiguration.

### 4.1 Accumulation-Erasure Duality

Stiegler's epiphylogenesis is essentially a concept of **accumulation**. Technical exteriorization functions as a mechanism enabling humanity to accumulate knowledge beyond individual lifespans. However, under digital conditions, this accumulation always entails simultaneous dissipation.

This **accumulation-erasure duality** can be formulated as follows: For physical artifacts, accumulation approximates a monotonic increasing function of time, and loss is an exceptional event. For digital objects, accumulation and dissipation proceed simultaneously, and dissipation is a structural condition.

**Example:** As of 2025, approximately 180 zettabytes of digital data are produced globally per year. Yet simultaneously, hundreds of terabytes of user-generated content are deleted or become inaccessible daily on the servers of Facebook, YouTube, and Instagram. Accumulation and dissipation are concurrent phenomena in the digital ecosystem.

This dual structure was not predicted by Stiegler's original model. In his later works on digital technology (Taking Care of Youth and the Generations, 2010; The Neganthropocene, 2018), Stiegler critiqued digital 'systematic stupidity' and 'symbolic misery,' but he did not advance to this ontological reconfiguration of the material conditions of epiphylogenesis itself.

### 4.2 Virtuality-Actuality Structure of Tertiary Retention

The execution environment dependency of digital objects demands a reconceptualization of tertiary retention in terms of the distinction between **virtuality** and **actuality**.

- **Virtual tertiary retention:** Existence as a bit-string encoded on a storage medium. A 'virtual memory' that can only be actualized when the execution environment is in place.
- **Actual tertiary retention:** Memory that is actually read and experienced in an appropriate hardware-software-platform environment.

The importance of this distinction is as follows: For physical artifacts, virtuality and actuality are effectively identical (a book is read the moment it is physically opened). For digital objects, the two are separated, and the degree of this separation determines the severity of digital decay. Even if the file remains (virtuality preserved), if there is no way to read it (actuality lost), the tertiary retention is effectively annihilated.

This invokes Aristotle's **entelecheia** — the process by which potentiality becomes actuality — into the domain of digital ontology. The 'actualization' of a digital object is not a simple physical contact (opening a book) but a complex process requiring the operation of an entire technological stack.

### 4.3 Network Ontology of Tertiary Retention

The relational mode of being of digital objects demands a shift from a **single-object model** to a **network model** of tertiary retention.

**Pre-digital model:** Individual artifacts each independently bear tertiary retention. Just as a single Rosetta Stone provided the key to deciphering Egyptian hieroglyphs, a single object functions as an independent memory repository.

**Digital model:** Tertiary retention is an **emergent property** of the entire network. A photograph uploaded to Facebook does not exist merely as a JPEG file; it acquires meaning within the total network of the file, tags, comments, shares, metadata, and the platform's UI-based mode of display. When one node of this network (e.g., the comments database) dissolves, the status of the photograph as tertiary retention is partially compromised.

This network model forms a point of contact with Bruno Latour's **Actor-Network Theory (ANT)**. Tertiary retention should no longer be understood as a property attributable to individual objects, but as a non-local effect of the entire network of humans, technologies, platforms, and formats.

## 5. Conceptual Resources: Han, Virilio, and Crary

### 5.1 Byung-Chul Han: The 'Non-Reality' of Digital Ontology and Decay

Byung-Chul Han, in *Verschwinden (Vanishing)* (2020), captures a key tension in digital ontology. Arguing that digital images "do not replace reality; rather, they make reality disappear," Han emphasizes the **non-reality** of digital existence.

According to Han's frame, the 'vanishing' of a digital object can be understood not as physical destruction but as a **return to non-reality**. When a physical artifact is burned or shattered, it is real destruction; when a digital object becomes unreadable, it is a process of losing reality itself. Digital decay is thus a form of **annihilation of reality**, ontologically distinct from conventional physical destruction.

However, Han's analysis also has limitations. He diagnoses the non-reality of digital existence primarily from its negative aspect, but fails to account for the **resilience** unique to digital objects — the fact that identical data can exist in countless copies. Unlike physical destruction, digital decay partially retains the possibility of reversibility (migration, emulation, format conversion).

### 5.2 Paul Virilio: The Ecology of Speed and Accelerated Obsolescence

Virilio's thesis in *Speed and Politics (1977)* and *The Aesthetics of Disappearance (1980)* — that the acceleration of technological development fundamentally transforms the conditions of human perception and memory — provides direct clues for understanding the accelerated nature of digital decay.

Virilio's 'dromology' (the ecology of speed) diagnoses that when the speed of technological development exceeds a threshold, human cognitive and perceptual frames can no longer keep pace. Applied to digital decay:

- **Format evolution speed vs. human memory duration:** The average lifespan of a file format (10-20 years) is significantly shorter than the human biological memory lifespan (70-80 years). An individual's personal memory accumulated in digital formats thus experiences format obsolescence even during their own lifetime.
- **The accelerated cycle of platforms:** The average lifespan of SaaS platforms is even shorter (5-10 years). This can be understood as the 'hyper-acceleration' Stiegler critiqued destabilizing the very material conditions of memory.

Virilio would have argued that this is not merely a technical problem but an **ontological problem**: the acceleration of speed dismantles the very concept of 'place (topos)' where memory can reside. Digital objects that have lost temporal stability can no longer function as 'place,' making Heideggerian 'dwelling' impossible.

### 5.3 Jonathan Crary: 24/7 Temporality and the Unsustainability of Memory

Crary's analysis of the temporality of digital capitalism in *24/7: Late Capitalism and the Ends of Sleep* (2013) — uninterrupted operation, the end of sleep, continuous wakefulness — illuminates yet another layer of digital decay.

According to Crary, the 24/7 operation of digital networks compresses past and future into the present. This **persistent present** eliminates the temporal interval necessary for memory to sediment. For memory to accumulate, an interval is needed in which content can 'settle' within the flow of time; the 24/7 regime blocks this interval.

From this perspective, the cause of digital decay lies not merely in technical conditions (format evolution, platform termination) but in **capitalist temporality itself**. Digital objects are designed to be made 'obsolete' through constant updates, new versions, and innovative replacements, destroying the temporal conditions in which memory can stably reside.

## 6. The Dissipative Turn of Epiphylogenesis — From Condition of Accumulation to Condition of Dissipation

Now we return to the essential question: **Should epiphylogenesis be thought not only as a condition of accumulation but also as a condition of dissipation?**

### 6.1 Thesis: Stieglerian Epiphylogenesis — The Technics of Accumulation

Stiegler's epiphylogenesis explains humanity's evolution through the sedimentation of temporal layers onto material objects via technology. In this model, technical objects perform the function of **preservation**, ensuring the continuity of cultural memory. Epiphylogenetic accumulation functions as the **memory repository of the species**.

### 6.2 Antithesis: The Dissipative Dialectic of Digital Conditions

Due to their ontological singularity (carrier-content separation, execution environment dependency, network ontology), digital objects exist in a paradoxical condition where the mechanism of accumulation simultaneously operates as a mechanism of dissipation. The same structures (platform dependency, format evolution, technological acceleration) that enable accumulation also necessitate dissipation.

### 6.3 Synthesis: Accumulation-Erasure Duality

Under digital conditions, epiphylogenesis must be reconceptualized as an **accumulation-erasure duality**. This does not merely mean 'failed accumulation' or 'loss of memory.' Rather, digital epiphylogenesis operates as **accumulation always premised on dissipation** — accumulation that includes dissipation within itself.

Concrete expressions of this dual structure:

1. **The preservation-dissolution paradox:** The infinite replicability of digital objects (maximization of accumulation) produces, for precisely the same reason, the loss of originality (a form of dissipation). What can be infinitely replicated does not 'truly' exist anywhere.

2. **The acceleration-sedimentation paradox:** The production speed of digital information is unprecedented in human history, but this acceleration eliminates the temporal interval needed for information to 'sediment' into cultural memory. We produce more information than ever before, yet most of this information 'dissipates' (loss of visibility, accessibility, context) immediately after production.

3. **The storage-loss paradox:** The plummeting cost of storage allows us to preserve more data than ever before, yet the lifespan of the infrastructure (software, hardware, platforms) needed to access this data is growing shorter. Storage increases while accessibility decreases.

### 6.4 Conclusive Proposition

**Epiphylogenesis is, under digital conditions, simultaneously a condition of accumulation and a condition of dissipation.** This is not a simple refutation of Stiegler's model but a productive extension of his concept. The epiphylogenetic condition that Stiegler diagnosed in *Technics and Time* must no longer be understood as a condition of 'pure accumulation' but as a **complex ecology where the possibility of accumulation and the necessity of dissipation coexist**.

This understanding carries the following practical and theoretical implications:

**First,** the preservation of digital memory can no longer be approached through the techno-optimist frame of 'once stored, preserved forever.' Digital epiphylogenesis is instead the object of **dynamic maintenance**, requiring continuous **migration**, **emulation**, and **format conversion**.

**Second,** responding to digital decay demands a new frame beyond simple technical solutions (cloud backup, format standardization): **ecological stewardship** of digital existence. This means redefining digital preservation not as a technical problem but as a political and cultural task encompassing temporality, materiality, and structures of power.

**Third,** Stiegler's later concept of **pharmacology** — the claim that technology is simultaneously poison (pharmakon) and cure — acquires an additional dimension when applied to the problem of digital decay. The dissolution of digital objects (poison) simultaneously opens new possibilities for accumulation (cure) — historical reenactment through emulation, recontextualization of information through format conversion. The key is the recognition of this duality and responsible response to it.

## 7. Conclusion: Toward an Ecology of Digital Decay

This analysis has sought to identify the structural transformation of Stiegler's concept of epiphylogenesis under digital conditions. In conclusion, the ecology of digital decay demands the **material-ecological reconfiguration** of Stiegler's concept of tertiary retention. This reconfiguration should proceed in the following directions:

1. **From accumulation to circulation:** Instead of viewing epiphylogenesis as mere accumulation, reconceptualize it as a circulatory process of accumulation and dissipation. Digital memory is not 'static data stored in a repository' but a 'living dynamic process maintained through continuous preservation and transformation.'

2. **From individual object to network:** Redefine tertiary retention not as a property of individual digital objects but as a non-local effect of the entire network of humans, technologies, platforms, and formats.

3. **From physical durability to ecological resilience:** Shift the goal of digital preservation from physical durability (permanent preservation) to ecological resilience (the ability to maintain the meaning of information across changing environments).

Digital decay is not merely a technical problem or a problem of information loss. It is a philosophical challenge demanding that the way humanity has entrusted its memory to material objects through technical exteriorization — i.e., the way we have been 'human' — must be fundamentally reconfigured under digital conditions. As Stiegler wrote in the preface to *Technics and Time*, "the human invents itself through technics." The 'human' of the digital age must once again invent itself within the dual condition of digital decay and accumulation.
