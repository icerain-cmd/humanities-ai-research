# Analysis: Predictive Optimization, the Environmentalization of Mobility Rights, and the Redesign of Ecological Publicness

## 1. The Structure of the Problem: The Dual Environment Constructed by Prediction

The optimization of service intervals through a public transportation demand-prediction app is not simply a matter of efficiency. It is a form of **time-axis manipulation**. Borrowing Kittler’s concept, the predictive algorithm spatializes the future demand of passengers into currently computable coordinates, and then feeds the resulting predictions back into real-time vehicle dispatching, thereby **materially constructing** future mobility patterns in the present. This is not a technology that “represents” the future; it is an operation of techno-generation that “generates” the future.

A central tension emerges here. The same algorithm performs the following two operations simultaneously:

- **For average users:** it provides an “optimized environment” characterized by reduced waiting times, lower fuel consumption, and reduced carbon emissions.
- **For users who deviate from the statistical average:** it assigns a “silent environment” in which their mobility patterns are classified as “unpredictable noise” and disappear from the system’s field of visibility.

According to the concept of **friction transparency** presented in Khoj Document 3, algorithmic errors or biases are not merely “technical defects.” Rather, they are conditions through which the very mode of operation of the system becomes visible to the subject. The problem is that, in the case of people with disabilities, irregular workers, and those performing care work, this “friction transparency” does not occur. Their mobility may not even be captured in the “training data” of the predictive model, or it may be treated as an outlier and intentionally excluded from the algorithm’s adjustment of weights. **Before friction can emerge, visibility itself is deprived.**

## 2. The Mechanisms of Environmentalizing Exclusion

Applying the **4+4 environmentalization mechanisms** formalized in your v2 paper draft, *AI_environment_full_draft.md*, to this case reveals the following structure:

### AI as Environment — Four Levels

| Level | Mode of operation | Example in service-interval optimization |
|---|---|---|
| **Infrastructure level** | Data centers, GPS sensors, and communication networks convert physical mobility into digital signals. | Stop sensors and bus GPS data generate a real-time demand map. |
| **Algorithmic level** | Time-series prediction models calculate future demand on the basis of past patterns. | ARIMA/deep-learning models generate demand forecasts by time period and route. |
| **Interface level** | Forecast results are visualized in an operations-dispatch system, structuring decision-making. | A recommendation for service intervals appears on the operations manager’s dashboard. |
| **Ontological level** | The three levels above combine to naturalize the standard of “normal mobility.” | “A route with low real-time demand should receive reduced service” becomes an unquestioned premise. |

### Consequences of Environmentalization — Four Effects

| Effect | Description |
|---|---|
| **Transparency** | The demand of average users becomes visible, while atypical mobility is placed in a state of statistical invisibility. |
| **Trust** | The output of the predictive model is trusted as “objective demand,” providing the basis for legitimizing dispatch decisions. |
| **Naturalization** | “Data-driven optimization” is accepted as a self-evident value, while exclusion is justified as the natural cost of efficiency. |
| **Governmentality** | Indirect regulation of conduct through algorithms: service intervals discipline citizens’ travel times and route choices. |

The implication of this structure is clear. Exclusion by the predictive model is **not a bug but a feature**. Statistical prediction essentially models distributions around an average, and mobility patterns located in the tail of the distribution are systematically pushed down the priority list for system optimization on the grounds that they are “unpredictable.”

## 3. The Time of Care: The Ontology of Statistical Invisibility

The most profound problem emerges here. Why are the mobility of care workers and the commuting patterns of irregular workers systematically excluded from predictive models?

By combining insights from your Eco-Digital framework and Khoj Document 4, “Digital Waste and Sensory Surplus,” the following analysis becomes possible.

The mobility of people engaged in care work—accompanying a disabled family member to the hospital, taking children to and from school, or caring for elderly parents—has the following characteristics:

- **Multi-destination chaining:** Rather than a simple origin-destination pair, these journeys involve multiple intermediate stops and nonlinear routes.
- **Temporal irregularity:** Instead of occurring during fixed commuting hours, they fluctuate according to care needs.
- **Low frequency and high variability:** Demand on a particular route at a particular time may be low, but without this mobility, the chain of care collapses.

These patterns are **classified as noise or outliers in the training data of predictive models**. This is because time-series forecasting algorithms such as ARIMA and LSTM are designed to learn from demand patterns with regular periodicity. As a result:

> Because the time of care cannot be quantified as **predictable demand**, it is treated as **mobility that does not exist**. This, in turn, leads to reduced service on the relevant routes, materially shrinking the mobility rights of those who perform care work.

This is the cyclical structure of what might be called, in Kittlerian terms, **“ontological disposal.”** The problem of digital waste and sensory surplus identified in Khoj Document 4 is not merely a problem of data storage. It extends to the **invisibilization of mobility whose existence is denied because it cannot be quantified**.

The answer these principles offer to your question is clear: **Digital sustainability cannot be evaluated solely by the amount of energy saved, and the evaluation criteria themselves must not become mechanisms that produce inequalities in mobility rights.**

## 5. Criteria for Ecological Publicness: A Four-Dimensional Evaluation Framework

By integrating your Eco-Digital framework with care ethics and data ecofeminism, I propose the following **Ecological Publicness Evaluation Framework (4E: Ecological Publicness Quadrant):**

### Dimension 1: Eco-Efficiency

- **Existing criteria retained:** Carbon emissions, fuel consumption, and reductions in air pollution.
- **Additional indicators:** Computational energy consumption of the predictive model, data-center water use, and the full life-cycle carbon footprint of hardware manufacturing and disposal.
- **Unit of measurement:** CO₂eq per user-kilometer.

### Dimension 2: Temporal Justice

- **Core question:** Whose time is optimized, and whose time is sacrificed?
- **Indicators:**
  - Ratio of maximum waiting time to average waiting time, tracking not only the average but also variance and maximum values.
  - Rate of change in service intervals on routes used by minority users or characterized by atypical demand.
  - Service-maintenance rate during care hours, including particular routes during non-peak periods.
- **Theoretical reference:** Stiegler’s critique of grammatization—who is captured and who is omitted when mobility patterns are discretized?

### Dimension 3: Care Visibility

- **Core question:** Can the predictive model detect the temporal characteristics of care work?
- **Indicators:**
  - Whether demand mapping includes routes with multiple intermediate stops rather than only simple origin-destination pairs.
  - Tracking changes in service accessibility for users with temporally irregular mobility patterns.
  - Collection of qualitative data on “why mobility cannot be quantified as demand.”
- **Operational strategy:** Use predictive uncertainty itself as an input for service design. The lower the reliability of demand forecasting on a route, the more strongly a minimum service interval should be guaranteed through inverse design.

### Dimension 4: Friction Transparency

- **Core question:** Are the exclusion mechanisms of the algorithm made visible to citizens?
- **Indicators:**
  - Whether a public API exists showing what data the predictive model uses and what data it excludes.
  - Whether citizens have a procedure for contesting the grounds of dispatch decisions.
  - Khoj Document 3’s “transparency of the subject”: an interface through which users can understand how their mobility is processed by the system.

### Integrated Evaluation Matrix

```text
Ecological Publicness Index (EPI) =
    α × Eco-Efficiency Score +
    β × Temporal Justice Score +
    γ × Care Visibility Score +
    δ × Friction Transparency Score
```

The weights (α, β, γ, δ) should be determined through democratic deliberation. By establishing the rule that **no weight may exceed 0.5**, the framework prevents any single dimension from overwhelming the others.

## 6. Theoretical Limitations and Further Questions

Although the Khoj references provide an important foundation for this analysis, the following limitations should be made explicit:

1. **The concept of the machine tax has not been applied:** The concepts of the age of techno-generation and the machine tax from Khoj Documents 1, 2, and 5 were used in this analysis only as an ontological background and were not applied to a concrete analysis of the dispatch algorithm. Further analysis is needed to determine how the informational order imposed by the machine tax naturalizes the stratification of mobility.

2. **Normative tensions remain unresolved:** The proposed 4E framework is designed to pursue carbon reduction and the protection of mobility rights “simultaneously.” However, the criteria for determining priorities when trade-offs between the two are unavoidable in practice have not yet been formalized. For example, how can the additional fuel consumption required to maintain care-related routes be justified as “sustainable”?

3. **Further development of Khoj Document 4, “Digital Waste,” is needed:** The extension of the concept of digital waste to the “invisibilization of mobility” is presented here only as a preliminary proposition. To conceptualize “invisible mobility” as a form of digital waste, further theoretical work is needed to extend existing discussions of visual and affective waste into the domains of spatial and temporal waste.

---

**Conclusion:** Digital sustainability cannot be evaluated solely by energy savings, and the evaluation criteria themselves must be subject to democratic deliberation. Ecological publicness, which calculates together the mobility excluded from platform optimization and the care time that makes such mobility possible, requires a paradoxical design principle that resists the statistical averaging of predictive algorithms and incorporates **unpredictability as a legitimate input into service design**. The paradox that the most difficult mobility to predict may be the mobility that most needs protection should itself become the first criterion of ecological publicness beyond the binary opposition between efficiency and equity.
