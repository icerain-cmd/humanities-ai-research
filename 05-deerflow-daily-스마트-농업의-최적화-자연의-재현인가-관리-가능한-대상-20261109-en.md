## Question

Suppose a smart agriculture platform combines soil-moisture sensors, satellite imagery, and weather forecasts to automatically determine when to irrigate and how much water to supply. If, in turning farmland into a set of variables legible to sensors, this system marginalizes farmers’ seasonal experience and crops’ unpredictable growth rhythms, does the algorithmically constructed “optimal growing condition” reproduce nature more accurately, or does it reorganize nature anew as a manageable object? In particular, analyze the moments when gaps in sensor data, farmers’ intuition, and model errors come into conflict, and demonstrate the claim that knowledge of nature is not established through data accuracy alone but is formed through the iterative practice of observation, judgment, and correction.

## Analysis Results

# Analyzing the Case of Smart-Agriculture Irrigation Automation — Does AI Reproduce Nature or Reorganize It?

---

## 1. Framing the Problem: The Ontological Status of Optimal Conditions

The moment a smart-agriculture platform combines soil-moisture sensors, satellite imagery, and weather forecasts to determine the timing and amount of irrigation, the system moves beyond simple measurement and makes a normative declaration about **“what farmland ought to be.”** The algorithmic judgment operating under the name of “optimal growing conditions” allows for two opposing interpretations:

1. **The representation thesis**: By capturing nature’s causal relations more precisely, the algorithm replaces with objective data what farmers have long been able to gauge only through sensation and experience. From this perspective, “optimal” is merely the result of probabilistically predicting how nature itself would operate.

2. **The reorganization thesis**: The algorithm reconstructs nature as a **set of variables legible to sensors**, thereby **backgrounding** seasonal rhythms, the physiological characteristics of crops, and the contingency of unpredictable growth that exist beyond those variables. From this perspective, “optimal” is a construct optimized for data efficiency and managerial effectiveness.

These two theses are not merely a matter of academic preference. Depending on which thesis is supported by the **moments of conflict** that arise between farmers and the system—gaps in sensor data, model errors, and interventions based on farmers’ intuition—we must decide whether to understand AI’s epistemological status as belonging more to the environment or to the object.

---

## 2. What Does “Optimal Growing Condition” Optimize?

### 2.1. Reduction to a Set of Variables and Its Costs

A smart-irrigation system decomposes farmland into **measurable variables**, such as soil-moisture percentage, evapotranspiration, the probability of precipitation, and solar radiation. This process clearly generates informational benefits. Continuous 24-hour monitoring, spatial resolution at the scale of several centimeters, and comparisons with ten years of historical climate data make visible phenomena beyond the reach of human perception.

Yet this visibility is achieved at the cost of **a particular form of invisibilization**. For example:

- **Soil microbial activity** is a factor that is difficult to turn into a variable. When a moisture sensor reads 30%, the amount of water actually available for crop uptake may differ depending on the structure of the soil’s microbial community, even at the same numerical level. The system, however, simplifies this as “30% = irrigation required.”

- **Crop growth rhythms** cannot be captured as a linear input-output function. There are periods when drought stress may actually promote root development, and supplying “optimal moisture” at such a point may have adverse effects on long-term growth. The system, however, has merely learned from historical data that maintaining “30%” is optimal.

- **Seasonal experiential knowledge** is not quantified. A farmer’s judgment that “spring has arrived unusually early this year” integrates the El Niño pattern, local microclimates, and even the circumstances of how water gates are being adjusted in surrounding rice paddies. The system has no access to this information.

At this point, the concept of Artura in **Document 1** provides an important theoretical resource:

> “Unless the distinction between Artura and generative aura is refined more clearly at the level of their respective strata, the two concepts may be read as mutually overlapping rather than mutually complementary.”

The “optimal condition” generated by the smart-agriculture system can be read as a type of Artura. In other words, it is not **a copy of nature**, but rather constitutes **a condition of experiencing nature as manageable**. The system does not reproduce nature “as it is.” It enables nature to be experienced anew as a media construct composed of **sensors, data, models, and judgments**.

## 3. Three Moments of Conflict: The Limits of Representation and the Necessity of Practice

### 3.1. Conflict 1: Sensor Gaps and the “Unmeasured Nature” They Reveal

A soil-moisture sensor measures a point. A single sensor installed at a depth of 10 cm may be assumed to represent the moisture conditions of a 1,000-square-meter rice paddy, but actual soil moisture is spatially heterogeneous. Areas near drainage channels, shaded edges, and heavily compacted soil respond differently even when subjected to the same irrigation.

The gap in sensor coverage becomes most dangerous when **the model is confident**. When the system displays a clear output such as “Current soil moisture: 28%; recommended irrigation in two hours: 15 mm,” the clarity of this output conceals the actual complexity of the farmland. A farmer may walk across the soil and make the sensory judgment that “this corner is different from that one,” while the system’s visualization displays only a single field filled with a uniform color.

The tension that emerges here is not simply a matter of “insufficient data,” but a **mismatch between two systems of knowledge about nature**. The farmer’s intuition asks, “Why is only this corner drying out so unusually?” The system can respond only, “Because the overall average is X%.” The issue is not the precision of the information but **the difference in its type and quality**.

### 3.2. Conflict 2: The Ontological Status of Farmers’ Intuition

“Judging from the direction of the furrows, it seems that this year’s wind patterns will differ from last year’s.” This is a farmer’s judgment that cannot be reduced to data. Yet when an empirically tested farmer’s judgment conflicts with the system’s prediction, it should not simply be dismissed as “subjective bias.”

Stages 2 and 3—“Stage 2: Uncanny Strangeness” and “Stage 3: Critical Reflection”—among the four stages of AI-human contact discussed in **Document 2** shed light on this point:

> “The four stages—generative wonder, uncanny strangeness, critical reflection, and relational resonance—are not simply a list of experiences. They form a **topological structure**.”

The moment when a farmer looks at the system’s irrigation command and feels that “something is wrong” belongs to the stage of “uncanny strangeness.” If this discomfort is simply ignored and the system’s output is always given priority, the relationship becomes fixed as an **asymmetrical authority structure**. If, however, this discomfort prompts the farmer to reconsider their own experience and further question the system’s assumptions, it transitions into the stage of “critical reflection.”

For example, suppose the system orders irrigation at 2 p.m., while the farmer’s experience leads to the judgment that “irrigating at 2 p.m. will result in high water loss because evapotranspiration is high, so irrigation in the morning would be more efficient.” This involves the following **ontological tension**:

- The system’s logic: “Soil moisture 28% < threshold 30% → irrigate immediately”
- The farmer’s logic: “Although soil moisture is 28%, the actual absorption rate is low given the evapotranspiration rate at 2 p.m. → morning irrigation would be more efficient”

Here, the criteria for “more efficient” differ. The system aims at **maintaining soil moisture**, while the farmer aims at **the crop’s actual water uptake**. The system organizes the world around the soil; the farmer organizes it around the crop.

### 3.3. Conflict 3: The Paradox Revealed by Model Error

The moment when a model makes an error ironically becomes decisive evidence that **the representation thesis refutes itself**.

Consider a hypothetical scenario: The system predicts a 70% chance of rain tomorrow afternoon and cancels irrigation. However, no rain actually falls, and the crops experience water stress.

This error is not merely an instance of a probabilistic forecast being wrong. At a deeper level, it reveals an **ontological inversion** at the moment when probabilistic prediction is converted into a decision:

- Prediction stage: “There is a 70% probability of rain” — an honest representation of uncertainty
- Decision stage: “Therefore, irrigation will be canceled” — a performative conversion that turns 70% into fact

In other words, the model acknowledges its own uncertainty through its probabilistic output while simultaneously converting that uncertainty into **the certainty of a decision**. The problem here is not the accuracy of the prediction but the very structure through which **probabilistic knowledge is translated into decisive action**.

When the model makes an error, the farmer’s corrective procedure—“manually turning on irrigation”—is not merely a temporary measure. This moment serves as a **window through which the social and practical construction of knowledge becomes visible**. The system’s knowledge is incomplete and can be completed as knowledge applicable to the field only through the farmer’s intervention. This demonstrates that the **cycle of observation, judgment, and correction** cannot be replaced by the quantity of data alone.

## 4. The Practical Construction of Knowledge: The Cycle of Observation, Judgment, and Correction

### 4.1. The Structure of the Three-Stage Cycle

The three conflicts analyzed above commonly reveal the necessity of the following three-stage cycle:

| Stage | Content | Role of the System | Role of Humans |
|------|------|--------------|-------------|
| **Observation** | Collection of sensor inputs, satellite imagery, and weather data | Automated (high frequency, broad coverage) | Qualitative judgment and detection of abnormal signs |
| **Judgment** | Determination of irrigation timing and amount | Algorithmic inference (probability-based) | Contextual interpretation and resetting of criteria |
| **Correction** | Compensation for errors and invalidation of decisions | Recording error logs | Manual intervention and provision of signals for model retraining |

What is decisive in this cycle is that the **correction stage** is not merely “bug fixing.” It is a **meta-epistemic practice that recognizes the limits of knowledge and makes judgments within those limits**.

When the system makes an error, the farmer:

1. Detects the error by recognizing a discrepancy between the system’s output and the field’s actual condition.
2. Infers the cause of the error: a sensor defect, a forecasting error, or a missing variable.
3. Replaces the system’s judgment with their own by manually irrigating.
4. Integrates the experience into their experiential knowledge: “This system often gets things wrong in situations like this.”

The system cannot completely replace any of these four steps. In particular, Step 2—inferring the cause—can be replaced only if the system is capable of diagnosing the causes of its own errors. Current irrigation systems, however, have difficulty even obtaining the label that their own prediction was wrong. **The fact of an error is registered only when the farmer manually intervenes.**

### 4.2. The Dual Structure of Knowledge

This suggests that knowledge of nature is constituted on two levels:

**Level 1: Data Knowledge**
- Sensor readings, statistical models, and probabilistic forecasts
- Strengths: high frequency, broad coverage, and an objective format
- Limitations: includes only what has been converted into variables and is insensitive to context

**Level 2: Practical Knowledge**
- Accumulated seasonal experience, sensory judgment, and experience in correcting errors
- Strengths: contextual sensitivity, qualitative integration, and adaptability
- Limitations: difficult to transmit comprehensively and resistant to formalization

These two levels are not hierarchical. The reason we cannot say that data knowledge is “more accurate” than practical knowledge is that **the accuracy of data knowledge depends on the error feedback and correction signals provided by practical knowledge**. Unless the system can recognize its own errors, the system’s “accuracy” is merely the result obtained after human correction.

The insight from **Document 3** clearly connects to this point:

> “PLM can function as the epistemic infrastructure of a WE resonant subject emerging from human-model interaction, but this possibility presupposes a verifiable memory structure.”

The smart-agriculture system is likewise a kind of “epistemic infrastructure.” However, if the system lacks a memory structure capable of accommodating farmers’ experiential knowledge, each moment of conflict is repeated as if it were happening for the first time. The system cannot learn from its errors, and farmers must repeatedly confront the same conflicts. This is the inefficiency produced by the absence of a genuine **WE (We-Eco) resonant subject**.

## 5. Conclusion: Does AI Reproduce Nature or Reorganize It?

### 5.1. Refuting the Representation Thesis

The three moments of conflict analyzed above demonstrate why the thesis that “the algorithm reproduces nature more accurately” is difficult to sustain:

1. **The error of reduction**: The system reduces farmland to a set of variables, but factors that have not been converted into variables—microbial activity, growth rhythms, and microclimates—possess actual causal efficacy.

2. **The circularity of self-verification**: The system’s “accuracy” holds only within the variables and thresholds defined by the system itself. When measured according to criteria external to the system, the error rate changes.

3. **The asymmetry of error**: The system’s errors are discovered and corrected only through human intervention. Thus, the system’s knowledge is not “independently true,” but dependent knowledge that becomes valid only after human correction.

### 5.2. Justifying the Reorganization Thesis

The alternative interpretation is as follows: The “optimal growing conditions” constructed by the algorithm are not a reproduction of nature but a **mediated construction that reorganizes nature anew as a manageable object**.

This claim can be justified along three axes:

**First, the structure of environmentalization.** The system reorganizes the environmental conditions surrounding farmland—soil, climate, and time—into a single **field of computability**. Nature is no longer an object existing independently outside the system; it is replaced by a network of variables, models, and judgments constructed within the system. The “nature” encountered by farmers is no longer soil, wind, and growth themselves, but the numerical values, graphs, and alerts displayed on a dashboard.

**Second, the transformation of the conditions of experience.** The concern raised in **Document 1**—“how technology transforms experience”—applies precisely here. The smart-agriculture system transforms the very conditions under which farmers experience nature. The tactile experience of stepping on soil is replaced by the visual experience of reading sensor values, while the seasonal perception that “spring has arrived early this year” is replaced by the numerical judgment that “the cumulative GDD (Growing Degree Days) is 1,200.” More precisely, what is being replaced is not eliminated but **duplicated**. Farmers come to live between two systems of experience, and whenever those systems conflict, ontological tension arises.

**Third, the asymmetrical structure of responsibility.** The system does not bear responsibility for its own decisions. When the system makes an error, the crops and farmers bear the consequences. The system’s error is reduced to a statistical property of the algorithm, while a farmer’s failure to correct the error is attributed to insufficient individual judgment. This asymmetry is a deep mechanism through which AI acquires “objective” authority. By positioning the system’s output as always “data-based” and human judgment as always “subjective,” the asymmetry of technological authority is reinforced.

### 5.3. Why AI Should Be Understood as an Environment

Ultimately, this analysis supports the thesis that AI systems such as smart-agriculture platforms do not function as tools for more accurately reproducing nature as an **object**. Rather, they intervene between humans and nature as a new **environment**.

AI as an environment:

- is not a neutral medium but a medium that actively constructs conditions;
- is not a window showing “nature as it is,” but a lens showing nature after a particular transformation;
- is not a tool that replaces human judgment, but a framework that resets the conditions under which human judgment takes place;
- cannot demonstrate its own accuracy independently, and has significance only within interaction with humans—that is, within the cycle of observation, judgment, and correction.

Whenever farmers clash with the system—“This sensor seems to be malfunctioning,” “The model got today’s rain forecast wrong,” or “I need to make the judgment myself in this exceptional area”—that moment confirms that AI exists not as an object but as an environment. We live **with** AI’s outputs, and we construct knowledge of nature through practices that modify, supplement, and sometimes disregard those outputs.

The accuracy of knowledge depends not on the quantity of data but on the **quality of this practice**: how quickly errors are detected, how flexibly judgments are updated, and how effectively humans and systems resonate with one another. This is the core of the claim that knowledge of nature is “formed through the iterative practice of observation, judgment, and correction.”
