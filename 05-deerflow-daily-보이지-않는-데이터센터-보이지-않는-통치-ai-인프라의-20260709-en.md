> Can Foucault's concept of governmentality be extended to analyze the ecological conditions of AI data centers — a mechanism that systematically conceals massive energy and resource consumption behind user-facing interfaces of 'freedom of access'?

## Question

Can Michel Foucault's concept of governmentality, as analyzed in *Security, Territory, Population*, be extended as a framework for analyzing the ecological conditions of today's hyperscale AI data centers (Google, Microsoft, OpenAI cloud infrastructure)? Data centers consume enormous amounts of electricity (hundreds of MWh per training run), cooling water (millions of gallons daily), and rare earth minerals (mining for hardware production), yet these material conditions are thoroughly concealed from end users behind a 'clean interface'. Is this concealment — the systematic removal of material infrastructure from visibility — the digital version of what Foucault identified as a key feature of modern governmentality: 'governing through freedom'? In other words, does guaranteeing users the infinite freedom of access (the freedom to ask ChatGPT anything at any time) prevent the enormous energy and resource consumption infrastructure from becoming the subject of political debate? Can this mechanism be called 'digital governmentality'? And how does this structural concealment define the core dilemma of Eco-Digital — the paradox where efficiency justifies infrastructural expansion?

## Introduction — Preliminary Position of the Analysis

This question concerns whether Foucault's concept of governmentality — a tool designed for analyzing 18th-century liberal governance — can be 'extended' to the ecological conditions of 21st-century digital infrastructure. Extension differs from application. What is required here is not a simple conceptual transfer, but a theoretical work that involves the reconfiguration of the concept itself.

## 1. The Core of Foucauldian Governmentality: The Paradox of Governing Through Freedom

The core of modern governmentality that Foucault traces in *Security, Territory, Population* is a paradox. While traditional sovereign power moved from "to let live and make die" to disciplinary power's "to make live and let die," governmentality through security dispositifs is more subtle. It constitutes 'population' as the object of governance — population is neither the sovereign's subjects nor discipline's individual bodies, but a probabilistic entity with statistical regularities (birth rates, death rates, crime rates).

And the key to governing this population is **not prohibition or coercion, but the production and management of freedom**. Free markets, free movement, free speech — when these freedoms are guaranteed, the population governs itself more effectively. Foucault's analysis of German Ordoliberalism and American Chicago School neoliberalism in *The Birth of Biopolitics* radicalizes this principle: the governor does not intervene in market outcomes, but only sets the 'frame' (institutional and legal conditions) within which the market can operate.

Crucial to this mechanism of 'governing through freedom' is the **transparentization of the material infrastructure that enables freedom**. For the market to operate 'naturally,' the legal system, monetary system, transportation infrastructure, and statistical apparatus behind it must all remain invisible. That is, governmentality operates on the systematic concealment of its material conditions.

## 2. The Data Center: The Material 'Milieu' of Digital Governmentality

A particularly notable concept in Foucault's framework is **milieu**. In the January 11 lecture of *Security, Territory, Population*, Foucault conceptualizes the operational space of security dispositifs as 'milieu.' Milieu is an intermediary zone where natural and artificial elements intersect. The governor does not directly control the variables of this milieu (circulation, wind, water, disease paths), but intervenes indirectly by adjusting the conditions of their circulation.

AI data centers can be seen as the digital-material version of this 'milieu' concept:

> **(1) Milieu of Material Circulation**: Data centers are located at the intersection of electricity (the grid), water (cooling systems), minerals (hardware supply chains), and air (climate conditions for cooling). These elements are not objects the data center 'controls,' but elements within which the data center is 'inserted,' adjusting circulation conditions. Google's data center locations — Finnish coast (seawater cooling), Oregon (cheap hydroelectric power), Singapore (strategic location despite tropical climate) — are strategic positionings responding to the variables of this milieu.

> **(2) Milieu of User Experience**: For the end user, the data center does not exist. The ChatGPT interface — a clean white chat window, smooth animations, instantaneous responses — constitutes a field where the user can 'freely' ask questions. This interface is Foucault's 'production of freedom' operating as a digital apparatus. Users do not need to know, and should not know, that their questions operate on top of a training run consuming hundreds of MWh, or that each prompt consumes real-time energy in watts. Because that knowledge would constrain their free use.

Here, Foucault's archive concept as mentioned in Khoj Document 2 becomes meaningfully connected. For Foucault, the archive was the general system of statements that determines 'what can be said.' The data center is the material condition of this archive. It determines not only **what can be said** (which models trained on what data can generate what responses) but also **what cannot be said** (whether this energy and resource consumption can become the subject of political debate). The data center is the archive of archives — a meta-archive.

## 3. The Structure of Concealment: Three Layers

The concealment of data centers' material conditions operates not as a single action, but across three overlapping layers:

### 3.1. Spatial Concealment
Data centers are deliberately located in remote places — Iceland, desert outskirts, suburban industrial parks. Their architecture reveals nothing of their interior from the outside. Windowless gray buildings, excessive security, signs reading 'LOGISTICS CENTER' instead of 'DATA CENTER,' or blank buildings. Data centers are built to be 'invisible.' This is the exact opposite of the visibility principle of the panopticon analyzed in *Discipline and Punish*. While the panopticon was architecture designed to make the surveilled always visible, the data center is architecture that prevents the surveiller (the user) from seeing the material conditions of surveillance. An **invisible panopticon**.

### 3.2. Interface Concealment
The interface is a device that removes materiality. Google's white background and monochrome logo, ChatGPT's clean dark/light modes, Siri's clear voice — all produce an 'illusion of immateriality.' Data resides in the 'cloud,' and the 'cloud' appears to float in the air. This concealment is not merely a matter of ignorance, but constitutes the very condition of possibility of user experience. If every time you opened ChatGPT a message appeared saying "This query consumes 0.002 MWh. Current grid carbon intensity: 0.4 kgCO₂/kWh," user behavior would fundamentally change. The aesthetic purity of the interface is maintained by systematically excluding this uncomfortable information.

### 3.3. Discursive Concealment
The most structural level of concealment operates at the level of discourse itself. Big tech companies declare 'carbon neutrality,' promise '100% renewable energy,' and promote 'efficiency innovations.' These discourses, rather than problematizing energy consumption itself, make only the 'source' and 'efficiency' of energy the object of debate. This is exactly the operation of what Foucault called 'procedures for controlling discourse' — prohibition, rejection, and true/false distinction. Within this discursive system, the permitted questions are "How renewable?" and "How efficient?" while the prohibited questions are "Is all this computation truly necessary?" and "How much AI is globally tolerable?" The latter are discursively excluded.

## 4. The Thesis of Digital Governmentality

Now we return to the core question: **Can this structure be called 'digital governmentality'?**

I propose a **conditional affirmation** of this thesis. Conditional because three theoretical modifications are necessary.

### 4.1. From Population to User — A Redefinition of 'Freedom'

In Foucault's governmentality, population is a statistical entity, and freedom is the freedom exercised by this population in markets, movement, and speech. In digital governmentality, this 'population' is replaced by the **user**. The user is constituted as both a statistical collective and simultaneously an individual consumer. The freedom granted to the user is 'freedom of access' — the freedom to ask anything, anytime, anywhere.

This 'freedom of access' radicalizes the paradox of Foucauldian governmentality in the digital domain. Just as the freedom of markets implies the possibility of market failure, the freedom of access implies the possibility of infrastructural depletion. But while liberal governance merely corrects market failures post hoc, digital governmentality **systematically pushes the consequences of infrastructural depletion out of visibility by guaranteeing freedom of access**. The more freely users ask questions (exercising freedom of access), the more data centers must expand, and this expansion enables even freer access for even more users. This positive feedback loop can reach the point where governmentality's 'governing through freedom' consumes its own material conditions.

### 4.2. From Security Dispositif to Sustainability Dispositif — A Shift in Normativity

Foucault's security dispositif operates on the principle of *laisser-faire* — not eliminating crime entirely, but maintaining it at an acceptable level, managing market fluctuations, preserving order. The digital governmentality dispositif is more active. It manages precisely the acceptable level of environmental destruction under the name of 'sustainability.' Carbon neutrality pledges, energy efficiency improvements, renewable energy investments — these are all dispositifs for maintaining infrastructural expansion at an 'acceptable' level. **This could be called the 'sustainability dispositif' (dispositif de soutenabilité).**

The crucial point is that this dispositif does not problematize infrastructural expansion itself. Rather, the sustainability discourse justifies expansion: "We're becoming more efficient, so we can continue to expand." This connects to the core paradox of Eco-Digital.

### 4.3. The Polarization of Freedom: User Freedom vs. The Invisible Bondage of Infrastructure

Digital governmentality deepens the paradox of Foucauldian governmentality further. Users are given nearly unlimited freedom of access, but this freedom operates on the premise of thorough ignorance of the material conditions of infrastructure. The user's freedom is **conditioned by the invisibility of infrastructure**. This extends the neoliberal subject analyzed in *The Birth of Biopolitics* — the self as enterprise — into its digital version. The digital subject is constituted as a 'subject of ignorance,' unaware that their free use is depleting the infrastructure.

## 5. Connection to the Paradox of Eco-Digital

The final part of the question — "the paradox where efficiency justifies infrastructural expansion" — constitutes the most important theoretical yield of this analysis of digital governmentality.

In Foucault's analysis of governmentality, liberal governance took market efficiency as its principle of rationalization. Markets are efficient (optimal resource allocation), so they should be left to themselves. Digital governmentality exactly repeats this logic, but shifts the object of 'efficiency.'

Data center energy efficiency (PUE, Power Usage Effectiveness) has been continuously improving. From an average of 2.0 in 2010, PUE has reached 1.1-1.2 in hyperscale data centers by 2025. This **efficiency improvement** is precisely the core discourse that justifies infrastructural expansion. The logic: "We're becoming more efficient, so we can do more computing." But this efficiency is a classic case of the Jevons paradox: efficiency improvements, rather than reducing resource consumption, actually induce more consumption. More efficient data centers attract more AI training and accommodate more users, resulting in increased absolute energy consumption.

Here, digital governmentality defines the dilemma of Eco-Digital as follows:

> **The Core Paradox of Digital Governmentality**: The freedom of access granted to users (unlimited questioning, unlimited generation) is constrained by the material limits of infrastructure (energy, water, minerals), but this constraint is perpetually 'deferred to the next stage' by the sustainability dispositif (efficiency improvements, renewable energy, carbon offsets). Therefore, the paradox of Eco-Digital can be defined as the 'systematic deferral of limits' — a structure that is conditioned by its limits while refusing to acknowledge them.

## 6. Limits of the Extension — A Critical Examination

For this analysis to be valid, the following critiques of the very possibility of extending Foucauldian governmentality must be confronted:

> **First, Foucault's governmentality analyzes the governing of human populations.** Data center energy consumption is closer to the operation of material infrastructure than to the governing of human behavior. Extending Foucault's framework to the 'governing of things' involves conceptual tension. While Foucault himself mentions the definition of governance as "the arrangement of things (disposition des choses)" in the February 1 lecture of *Security, Territory, Population*, this refers to the relationship between humans and things, not the governance of things themselves.

> **Second, does Foucault's governmentality analyze deliberate strategy or unintentional structure?** Is the concealment structure of data centers 'intended'? It is difficult to claim that big tech companies have deliberately adopted a concealment strategy. Rather, this concealment is closer to a structural effect of the combination of capitalist acceleration and technical convenience. Considering that Foucault's concept of governmentality can operate as a structural analysis without intentionality, this is not so much a limit as a point requiring analytical precision.

> **Third, the most fundamental limitation: the substantive difference of 'freedom.'** The 18th-19th century liberal freedom analyzed by Foucault was political and economic freedom (market freedom, freedom of speech, freedom of movement), taking the form of rights. 'Freedom of access' in the digital environment is closer to a technical condition (accessibility) than a right. Users do not have the 'right' to access ChatGPT; they find themselves in a technically accessible condition. This 'technicalization of freedom' — the transformation of freedom from rights to accessibility — is not adequately captured by Foucault's conceptual framework alone.

---

## 7. Conclusion: The Thesis of Digital Governmentality, Conditionally Approved

In conclusion, extending Foucault's concept of governmentality to the ecological conditions of AI data centers is **conceptually productive yet in need of theoretical modification**.

- **Extendable aspects**: The systematic concealment of data center infrastructure, governance through freedom of access, the dispositif function of sustainability discourse, and the paradox of efficiency — all these structures can be understood as digital variants of the governmentality analyzed by Foucault in *Security, Territory, Population*.
- **Aspects requiring modification**: The transition from population to user, the transformation of freedom from rights to technical accessibility, and the possibility of governing material infrastructure itself — these points require conceptual extensions beyond Foucault's framework.

Under this conditional approval, 'digital governmentality' can be defined as follows:

> **Digital governmentality**: A form of governance that, by guaranteeing users technical freedom of access, systematically isolates the consumption of the massive material infrastructure behind it (energy, water, minerals) from political debate. This is a digital version of governing through freedom, but unlike Foucauldian governmentality, it is based on 'freedom as accessibility' rather than 'freedom as right,' and its material limits are perpetually deferred through sustainability dispositifs (efficiency discourse, renewable energy pledges).

This definition situates the core paradox of Eco-Digital — the structure where efficiency justifies infrastructural expansion — within a Foucauldian analytical framework, while simultaneously demonstrating the need to modify and extend that framework.
