# Critical Revision and Authorship in Generative AI Writing Classes — A Triple Lens of Barthes, Flusser, and the AI Player

## Introduction: Situating the Problem

In a 2026 Korean university writing class utilizing generative AI, the question of how to classify a student's **critical revision** and submission of AI-proposed sentence structures and references carries theoretical depth beyond a simple policy decision. This question simultaneously entails tensions on three levels:

1. **Ontological level**: Who is the 'author,' and how is authorship of a text involving generative AI to be defined?
2. **Institutional level**: How does the Korean university assessment system respond to the above ontological question, and what contradictions does that response contain?
3. **Practical level**: How does the act of 'critical revision' reconstitute the boundaries of authorship?

This analysis adopts Lee Yong-wook's (2025) "AI Players and the Future of Knowledge Structuring," retrieved via Khoj RAG, as its primary conceptual framework, and intersects it with two theoretical lenses—Barthes and Flusser—to attempt a more precise analysis.

## 1. Barthes's 'Death of the Author' and the Ontological Paradox of AI Writing

### 1.1 Reconstructing Barthes's Thesis

The core thesis Roland Barthes advanced in "The Death of the Author" (1967) can be summarized as follows: the meaning of a text is not determined in advance by the author's intention (the author's 'voice' or 'life'), but is constituted only in the scene (場面) where the reader interprets the text after it has been written. According to Barthes, writing does not converge toward a transcendental origin called the 'author,' but is rather a 'scene' (場, scene) where various citations intersect and are woven together.

> For Barthes, the 'death of the author' is simultaneously the 'birth of the reader.'

When this thesis is applied to generative AI writing, the following intriguing paradox emerges.

### 1.2 The 'Death of the Author' in AI Writing — A Double Death

In the writing scene involving generative AI, Barthes's declaration that 'the author is dead' occurs **twice**:

1. **The first death — the disappearance of the traditional human-author**: When AI 'generates' sentences, there is no longer an 'author' in the traditional sense (a conscious subject writing as self-expression) in those sentences. AI output is a probabilistic recombination of patterns from a vast corpus, not the expression of any inner intention.

2. **The second death — the dissolution of the student-author**: When the student revises and submits the AI's output, the student likewise does not exist as an 'author' in the Barthesian sense. The student's revision is merely part of "a scene where various citations intersect"—that is, a scene where the AI's output, the student's judgment, past learning texts, and the internalization of assessment criteria all operate simultaneously.

### 1.3 The Paradox: An Institution Trying to Revive the 'Dead Author'

Here a decisive tension arises. Regardless of Barthes's theoretical declaration of the 'death of the author,' **the university assessment system presupposes that the author is alive**. Grades are awarded to individuals (students), and plagiarism judgments depend on the distinction between 'original text' and 'derivative text.' That is:

> **Barthes's ontology**: The author is dead → all texts are weaving
> **The university's institutional doctrine**: The author is alive → assessment is attributed to individual responsibility

This gap between ontology and institutional doctrine is precisely the fundamental tension facing Korean universities in generative AI writing classes today.

## 2. Flusser's 'Technical Image-Making' and the Remediation of Authorship

### 2.1 Flusser's Stage Theory of Image-Making

Vilém Flusser's *Towards a Philosophy of Photography* (1983/2013) argues that human perception and image-making (abbilden) have developed historically through three stages:

| Stage | Mode of Image-Making | Medium | Characteristics |
|-------|----------------------|--------|-----------------|
| Stage 1 | Traditional image-making | Paintings, sculptures | Directly imitates the concrete world |
| Stage 2 | Text (writing) | Linear writing | Explains the world through abstract concepts |
| Stage 3 | Technical image-making | Photography, digital images | Re-visualizes concepts (texts) — but through computational construction |

For Flusser, technical images (photographs, digital images, etc.) are not simple representations of the world. They are **texts (concepts, theories, science) re-imaging themselves through the mediation of computational devices (cameras, computers)**. That is, technical images are the product of a double process of abstraction and concretization: 'world → text → image.'

### 2.2 Generative AI Writing and the Extension of Technical Image-Making

Applying Flusser's framework to generative AI writing, AI-generated text can be understood as a 'textual version of the technical image.' That is:

> **Traditional writing**: world → human perception → text
> **AI-generated text**: vast corpus (a world of texts) → algorithmic probabilistic computation → output text

AI-generated text holds the same ontological status as what Flusser called the 'technical image.' It is no longer the expression of direct human experience or intention, but rather **the product of a self-productive process in which texts give birth to texts**. This corresponds remarkably with Barthes's thesis of the 'weaving of citations.'

### 2.3 The Flusserian Player: Play on the 'Program'

For Flusser, what matters is not that humans become prisoners of technical apparatuses, but their ability to understand the operating principle (program) of the apparatus and to **play (spiel/play)** upon it. Flusser uses the photographer as an example: the photographer understands the camera's program (shutter speed, aperture, focus, etc.) and makes creative choices within the range that program permits.

From this perspective, the student as AI player must:

- Understand the AI's 'program' (the operating principles of LLMs, prompt design methods, and patterned biases in output)
- Recognize the program's limitations and perform creative choices (critical revision, reconstruction, supplementation) upon it
- In this case, the student's act is defined not as 'creative authorship' but as 'designerly play upon the program'

This corresponds precisely to Lee Yong-wook's (2025) definition of the AI player: "an AI player is not merely a user of AI tools or a passive participant on a platform, but one equipped with the capability to skillfully operate technologies and tools to maximize one's own creativity."

## 3. The Student's Critically Revised Submission — Analysis by Issue

### 3.1 Is It Co-authorship?

**Arguments against**:
- International academic publishing ethics bodies (COPE, ICMJE, WAME) have consistently stated since 2023 that AI is not recognized as an author
- AI cannot bear responsibility, and legal and institutional authorship is attributed to humans—therefore 'co-authorship' cannot be established
- The very fact that the student 'critically revised' the AI output can instead serve as grounds for strengthening the student's sole authorship (since the student exercised final control)

**Perspectives in favor**:
- From Barthes's viewpoint, after the death of the author, the very concept of 'co-authorship' is called into question—neither human nor AI can be a true 'author,' and therefore 'co-authorship' is also impossible
- From Flusser's viewpoint, the student and AI can rather be seen as a hybrid that jointly participates in 'play upon the program'—this is closer to a 'technical-human composite' than to 'co-authorship'

> **Judgment**: Under strict legal and institutional criteria, AI co-authorship is not recognized, so 'co-authorship' is not an appropriate classification within the current system. However, from the perspective of the ontological reconstruction of authorship, thinking that goes beyond the 'co-authorship' frame itself is necessary.

### 3.2 Is It Tool Assistance?

**Arguments in favor**:
- Lee Yong-wook's (2025) AI player concept views AI not as a 'tool' but as a 'complex mediator,' yet its practice remains a structure in which the human subject 'operates' the AI—that is, an extension of 'tool assistance'
- According to Xu's (2025) analysis of AI use declarations in academic journals, 57.8% of researchers' AI use purposes focus on 'readability improvement,' which does not depart from traditional tool-use patterns
- The student's 'critical revision' may not be qualitatively distinguishable from proofreading and editing in traditional assignment performance

**Problems**:
- The 'tool assistance' frame fails to capture AI's generative nature (unlike existing tools, it produces content itself)—its ontological status is fundamentally different from that of a calculator or grammar checker
- If the student's critical revision was applied to the 'content and structure' of the AI output, it goes beyond simple correction and takes on the character of 'co-design'
- From a Flusserian perspective, the term 'tool assistance' oversimplifies the complexity of technical image-making—technical apparatuses are not mere tools but media that constitute the world

> **Judgment**: 'Tool assistance' is the most realistic classification from an institutional standpoint, but theoretically it does not adequately explain AI's generative nature. The 'tool assistance' classification carries the risk of reducing AI to a 'passive extension.'

### 3.3 Is It Plagiarism?

**Grounds for not viewing it as plagiarism**:
- If the student applied 'critical revision' and disclosed the source (AI use), it does not meet the requirements of traditional plagiarism (presenting another's work as one's own)
- Since AI output is an 'artifact' without a specific individual author, applying the concept of 'plagiarism' within the existing copyright law framework is difficult

**Transformation of the plagiarism debate**:
- A more fundamental problem is the crisis of the concept of 'originality'—in AI-dependent writing, the distinction between what the student 'thought for oneself' and what 'the AI thought' becomes impossible
- As Marina et al. (2026) point out, there is a risk of losing "the cognitive value of the writing process"—even if the student critically revised, if the 'messy, slow, often frustrating' process of struggling with writing is omitted, the essential value of learning is undermined
- According to Wasi et al.'s (2024) survey, participants tend to claim 'authorship' of LLM-generated content while not feeling 'ownership' of it—that is, a psychological separation exists between ownership and authorship

> **Judgment**: Although it is not plagiarism in the strict sense, within an assessment system that relies on the traditional concept of 'originality,' the 'authenticity' of this submission faces fundamental questions.

## 4. Cross-Analysis of the Three Theoretical Frameworks

### 4.1 How Each Theory Explains the Deconstruction and Remediation of Authorship

| Theory | Mode of deconstructing authorship | Mode of remediating authorship | Application to AI writing |
|--------|----------------------------------|-------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Barthes** | Text is not the author's intention but a weaving of citations → the author is dead | There is no new author—instead, the reader constitutes the meaning of the text | The AI-student composite is merely a 'scene' in which all participate in the weaving of citations |
| **Flusser** | The traditional author (the subject of self-expression) disappears with the advent of technical apparatuses | In its place emerges the 'player upon the program'—a creative designer under technical conditions | The student is a player who understands the AI's 'program' and 'plays' upon it |
| **AI Player** | Deconstructs the author-reader dichotomy | Redefines the subject as 'one who designs and publishes meaning' | The student is an integrated agent who designs knowledge structures through real-time collaboration with AI |

### 4.2 Points of Conflict with the Assessment System — Three Specific Issues

**Issue 1: The problem of attributing responsibility**

The university assessment system attributes responsibility to individuals. However, as Barthes points out, if a text is a weaving of citations, then distinguishing 'the student's contribution' from 'the AI's contribution' in an AI-involved text is ontologically impossible. Regarding the student as bearing full responsibility for the text merely because the student applied 'critical revision' may be an institutional mechanism that systematically conceals AI's generative contribution.

> If Barthes deconstructed traditional authorship through the 'death of the author,' the university maintains a fictional stability of responsibility attribution by forcing the 'resurrection of the author.'

**Issue 2: The criteria for plagiarism judgment**

Existing plagiarism judgments rely on the dichotomy of 'the relationship between original and copy.' However, AI-generated text (1) has no clear 'original,' (2) functions with the entire training corpus as a probabilistic 'original,' and (3) produces different outputs each time for the same prompt. Therefore, the traditional concept of plagiarism is nearly inapplicable in the AI writing context.

From Flusser's perspective, a more fundamental problem is that the AI output's 'reference suggestions' are highly likely to be hallucinations. If the student critically revised these, this can be evaluated as an act of recognizing and correcting the AI's programmatic limitations. However, traditional assessment systems lack the tools to measure this 'program recognition-correction' ability.

**Issue 3: The criteria for evaluating creativity**

Korean university writing assessments generally adopt 'originality,' 'logicality,' and 'critical thinking' as primary criteria. In AI-involved texts:

- Can 'originality' still be applied to a result produced by revising AI-generated sentence structures?
- If the 'logical structure' was proposed by AI, can the student's 'logical thinking' be evaluated?
- Must 'critical thinking' be converted into 'meta-criticism' (criticism of criticism) of AI proposals?

To evaluate the core competency of the AI player as defined by Lee Yong-wook (2025)—the ability to "design one's own knowledge structure by repeating the processes of questioning, editing, curation, and publishing"—a shift from evaluating the 'completed text' to evaluating 'process design' is essential.

## 5. Synthesis: Beyond 'Tool Assistance' Toward 'Programmatic Collaboration'

### 5.1 The Optimal Classification Within the Current System

Given the current institutional and legal reality, classifying the student's critically revised submission as 'tool assistance' is the most realistic option. The reasons are:

1. AI cannot be a legal author, and all responsibility is attributed to humans
2. The student's 'critical revision' of AI proposals means the student exercised final control
3. Consistency with the guidelines of major academic ethics bodies such as COPE and ICMJE

### 5.2 The Fundamental Limitations of the Classification Frame — The Need for the Concept of 'Programmatic Collaboration'

However, the 'tool assistance' classification is theoretically insufficient for the following reasons.

First, unlike traditional tools (calculators, grammar checkers, dictionaries), AI **generates content**. The concept of 'tool' fails to capture generativity.

Second, according to Flusser, technical apparatuses are not 'tools' but 'media.' Media are not neutral means of conveying human intention; they themselves embody a specific constitution of the world. The AI's 'program' (biases in training data, probabilistic characteristics of output, tendencies toward hallucination) fundamentally conditions the student's writing act.

Third, when Lee Yong-wook (2025) defines the AI player as a "complex mediator connecting technology and context," this redefines the student-AI relationship not as 'tool use' but as **'collaborative design.'** The student as AI player understands the AI's program, makes strategic choices within the range that program permits, and recognizes and corrects the AI's limitations.

At this point, a new classification concept is needed: **'programmatic collaboration'** instead of 'tool assistance.' This concept:

- Acknowledges that AI is not a mere tool but a medium with its own 'program' (operating logic, biases, limitations)
- Captures that the student's role is that of a 'player' who understands the program and strategically designs upon it
- Does not conceal AI's generative contribution while still attributing institutional responsibility to humans
- Shifts assessment criteria from 'completeness of the final product' to 'ability to understand, utilize, and critique the program'

### 5.3 Proposals for the Assessment System

Institutional proposals for 2026 Korean university generative AI writing classes:

1. **Mandate transparency**: Students must specifically disclose the scope of AI use (sentence structure suggestions, reference recommendations, editing assistance, etc.)
2. **Make the revision process visible**: Require submission of the history of 'original AI output → student revision → final submission'
3. **Evaluate program awareness**: Establish as a separate assessment item how accurately the student recognized and corrected AI's limitations (hallucination, bias, pattern repetition)
4. **Process-oriented assessment**: Include not only the completed text but also question design, editing strategies, and curation processes as objects of assessment

---

## 6. Conclusion: The Tension Between Ontological Instability and Institutional Stabilization

In a 2026 Korean university generative AI writing class, classifying the student's critically revised submission as 'tool assistance' is the most appropriate within the current institutional framework. However, this classification is a product of 'institutional stabilization' that conceals ontological instability.

Barthes declared the death of the author, but the university assessment system resurrects the author to attribute responsibility. Flusser raised the composite subjectivity of the human-apparatus with the advent of technical image-making, but the university still assumes a 'pure human subject.' Lee Yong-wook's AI player concept is an attempt to bridge the gap between the two, but for it to be fully accommodated within the assessment system, a fundamental reconstruction of the 'author' concept and a paradigm shift in assessment are necessary.

> **Summary**: The student's critically revised submission is classified as 'tool assistance' under the current system, but theoretically it should be redefined as 'programmatic collaboration.' Maintaining this distinction without reforming the assessment system is nothing other than institutional conservatism that conceals AI's generative contribution and forcibly sustains the traditional 'author' concept. True AI player education requires teaching students to read the AI's 'program,' recognize its limitations, and creatively 'play' upon it—that is, it demands a paradigm shift from 'tool assistance' to 'programmatic collaboration.'
