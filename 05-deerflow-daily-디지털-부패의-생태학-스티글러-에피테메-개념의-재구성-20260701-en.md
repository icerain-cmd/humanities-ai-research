> Does Stiegler's concept of epiphylogenesis need to be reconfigured from a condition of accumulation to a condition of erasure under digital conditions?

## Question

Bernard Stiegler, in *Technics and Time (La Technique et le Temps)*, diagnosed epiphylogenesis — the accumulation of memory through technological exteriorization — as humanity's unique evolutionary condition. However, smartphone replacement cycles, SaaS platform shutdowns, and file format obsolescence — the planned obsolescence of digital objects — undermine the accumulation that Stiegler positively evaluated. Unlike physical artifacts (stone tools, books), digital objects experience 'digital decay' rather than 'knowledge sedimentation' due to storage medium degradation, platform dependency, and format evolution. Does this ecology of digital decay — the organic disappearance of information — demand a material and ecological reconfiguration of Stiegler's concept of tertiary retention? In other words, must epiphylogenesis be rethought not only as a condition of accumulation but also as a condition of erasure?

# The Ecology of Digital Decay: Toward a Material and Ecological Reconfiguration of Stiegler's Epiphylogenesis

---

## 1. Problem Setting: Stiegler's Hidden Premises of Epiphylogenesis

Stiegler's concept of epiphylogenesis is grounded in the insight that humanity has accumulated transgenerational memory through technological exteriorization. In *Technics and Time*, Volume 1, he sought to capture humanity's unique mechanism of cultural evolution — one that cannot be explained by organic evolution (epigenesis) alone. The conditions Stiegler implicitly assumed for epiphylogenesis to function are as follows:

**(a) Physical durability:** Technical objects must be able to physically outlast the era in which they were created. A handaxe endures tens of thousands of years; a parchment manuscript survives centuries; a printed book, under proper conditions, lasts hundreds of years.

**(b) Carrier-content inseparability:** In pre-digital technical objects, content and carrier are inseparable. The use-wear on a stone tool is etched into that stone; the letters of a manuscript are inscribed on that parchment. This inseparability guarantees the object's 'authenticity' and 'originality.'

**(c) Environment independence:** Physical artifacts can be read or function on their own, independent of the environment in which they are found. The Rosetta Stone, when discovered in 1799, already contained its information; only linguistic knowledge was needed for decipherment.

**(d) Gradual sedimentation:** Epiphylogenesis accumulates as layers of time sediment onto material objects. Subsequent generations excavate and reinterpret this sedimented memory, maintaining cultural continuity.

These four conditions, rather than being explicitly thematized in Stiegler's discussion, were ontological properties of physical artifacts that he naturally presupposed in his case analysis. How these premises are deconstructed by digital objects forms the starting point of this analysis.

---

## 2. Ontological Specificity of Digital Objects

Digital objects violate all four of the above conditions. The nature of this violation must be understood not as mere 'difference' but as an 'ontological rupture.'

### 2.1 Carrier-Content Separation and the Loss of Originality

The most fundamental ontological characteristic of digital objects is the **separation of bit-string from physical carrier**. The same PDF file can exist simultaneously on an SSD, HDD, cloud server, and USB drive, with no distinction between 'original' and 'copy' among these replicas. This stems from the ontological property of **zero marginal replication cost**.

This separation produces the following consequences:
- **Disappearance of originality:** Walter Benjamin's diagnosis of the loss of aura in *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* becomes a structural necessity for digital objects.
- **Loss of indexicality:** Borrowing from Charles Sanders Peirce's semiotic framework, pre-digital artifacts bear indexical traces of the time and space in which they were situated. The use-wear on a specific handaxe, the ink bleed on a particular manuscript — these testify to the object's unique historicity. Digital objects, permitting only identical replication of zeros and ones, possess no such indexical traces.

### 2.2 Execution Environment Dependency and Virtualized Existence

The most radical property of digital objects is their **total dependency on an execution environment**. A .doc file stored on a floppy disk requires a specific operating system, application, and hardware to 'actualize' as meaningful information. A physical book can be read directly by the human eye in ambient light, but the 'virtual existence' and 'actual existence' of a digital object are separated.

This dependency operates on three levels:

1. **Hardware level:** Evolution of physical interfaces (IDE → SATA → NVMe), drive mechanisms (magnetic tape → HDD → SSD → flash)
2. **Software level:** Operating system evolution (MS-DOS → Windows 95 → Windows 11), file format changes (.doc → .docx → .docm), encoding shifts (EUC-KR → UTF-8)
3. **Platform level:** Cloud service lifecycles (Google Reader → discontinued), social media (MySpace → Facebook → TikTok), SaaS tool lifespans

This triple dependency fundamentally reconfigures the ontological question of what a digital object 'is.' A digital object is a relational being that only 'exists' when the entire hardware-software-platform network is operational.

### 2.3 Structural Internalization of Planned Obsolescence

The 2-3 year replacement cycle of smartphones, the discontinuation of SaaS platforms, the obsolescence of file formats — these are not mere technical evolution but the **operating mode of capitalist value realization**. The crucial point is that this planned obsolescence is internalized into the 'essence' of the digital object itself.

The obsolescence of physical artifacts is **accidental**. A book may get wet, burn, be eaten by insects, or decompose due to paper acidification — but these are results of external conditions, not the artifact's 'mode of being.' In contrast, the obsolescence of digital objects is **structural**. File format obsolescence arises from the evolutionary logic of the format itself; SaaS service termination is a consequence of the platform business model. In other words, digital objects embed the seed of their own destruction within their condition of existence.

## 3. Four Layers of Digital Decay

To reconfigure Stiegler's concept of tertiary retention under digital conditions, I decompose 'digital decay' into four layers.

### 3.1 Layer 1: Carrier Decay

The physical carriers of digital objects exist materially and therefore undergo physical degradation: HDD platter wear, SSD cell lifespan limits (PE cycles), CD-R dye deterioration (5-10 year lifespan), flash memory charge leakage.

**Characteristic:** This layer appears superficially similar to the physical decay of pre-digital artifacts, but with a critical difference. Physical artifacts (stone tools, pottery) preserve most of their information despite partial damage (e.g., the Rosetta Stone's cracks still leave most inscriptions legible). Digital storage medium damage, by contrast, is **critical** — a single bit error can render an entire file unreadable. This stems from the **discreteness** of digital information.

### 3.2 Layer 2: Platform Decay

The termination of SaaS platforms, social networks, and cloud services represents the most visible form of digital object disappearance. Google Reader (2013), MySpace (massive music data loss, 2019), Yahoo! GeoCities (2009), Vine (2016) — numerous platforms have caused mass data disappearance upon service termination.

**Characteristic:** At this layer, digital objects are physically preserved (data remains on server hard drives), but the access infrastructure disappears, rendering them 'existing but inaccessible.' This is the digital realization of what Paul Virilio called the 'desert of the real' — the ontological ambiguity of that which can no longer be perceived. The data still exists, but it no longer **presents** itself.

**Case Study — Google Reader:**
- Launched 2005, terminated July 1, 2013
- Approximately 300 million+ RSS feed subscriptions disappeared
- Partial user data was exportable via Google Takeout, but the network relationships between feeds (the social layer) were irrecoverable
- This case demonstrates that digital objects are built not merely on data but on **relationality**

### 3.3 Layer 3: Format Obsolescence

File format evolution is the most insidious form of digital decay. Formats do not explicitly 'die'; rather, they become gradually unreadable as current software ceases to support them.

**Historical cases of format obsolescence:**
- WordStar .wsd (1978): effectively defunct since the early 1990s
- Lotus 1-2-3 .wk1 (1983): unreadable after the 2000s
- Corel WordPerfect .wpd: support discontinued in current Word
- Flash .swf (1996-2020): Adobe officially ended support December 31, 2020
- QuickTime .mov (legacy codecs): unplayable on current macOS

**Characteristic:** Format obsolescence takes the form of 'slow violence' (Rob Nixon's concept) — not a catastrophic event but a gradual progression that, once past a critical threshold, becomes irrecoverable.

### 3.4 Layer 4: Semantic Context Decay

This is the most subtle yet most severe form of digital decay. Even when data is technically fully preserved, the format remains readable, and the platform still functions, the cultural and cognitive context necessary to produce and interpret that data may have disappeared.

**Examples:**
- The HTML/CSS code of 1990s personal homepages is fully preserved, but without the context of the era's web aesthetics, networking culture (webrings, guestbooks), and information retrieval methods (Yahoo directory-based search), we cannot understand why these pages were structured in particular ways.
- Early digital art works (.exe format, interactive) — the executable files themselves are preserved, but the original intended experience cannot be reproduced without the era's hardware speed (CPU, RAM), monitor resolution (640×480 CRT), and input devices (keyboard-centric rather than mouse-driven).

**Theoretical implication:** This layer signifies the collapse of the cognitive circulation that Stiegler explained through the relationship between tertiary retention, primary retention (perceptual experience), and secondary retention (memory and the unconscious).

## 4. Ecological Reconfiguration of Stieglerian Tertiary Retention

### 4.1 Accumulation-Erasure Duality

Stiegler's epiphylogenesis is fundamentally a concept of **accumulation**. Technological exteriorization functions as a mechanism enabling humanity to accumulate knowledge beyond individual lifespans. Under digital conditions, however, this accumulation always entails simultaneous erasure.

This **accumulation-erasure duality** can be schematized as follows:

> **Physical artifacts:** Accumulation (A) approximates a monotonic increasing function over time (t). Loss is an exceptional event.
> **Digital objects:** Accumulation (A) and erasure (D) proceed simultaneously. Erasure is a structural condition.

**Example:** As of 2025, the world produces approximately 180 zettabytes of digital data annually. Yet simultaneously, hundreds of terabytes of user-generated content are deleted or rendered inaccessible daily on Facebook, YouTube, and Instagram servers. Accumulation and erasure are simultaneous phenomena of the digital ecosystem.

### 4.2 Virtuality-Actuality Structure of Tertiary Retention

The execution environment dependency of digital objects demands a reconceptualization of tertiary retention through the distinction between **virtuality** and **actuality**:

- **Virtual tertiary retention:** Existence as a bit-string encoded on a storage medium — a 'virtual memory' that can only be actualized when the execution environment is provided.
- **Actual tertiary retention:** Memory that is actually read and experienced within an appropriate hardware-software-platform environment.

The significance of this distinction: in physical artifacts, virtuality and actuality are effectively identical (a book becomes readable the moment it is physically opened). In digital objects, the two are separate, and the degree of this separation determines the severity of digital decay. If the file remains (virtuality preserved) but the means to read it is lost (actuality lost), tertiary retention is effectively extinguished.

### 4.3 Network Ontology of Tertiary Retention

The relational mode of being of digital objects demands a shift from the **single-object model** to the **network model** of tertiary retention.

**Pre-digital model:** Individual artifacts independently carry tertiary retention. Just as a single Rosetta Stone provided the key to deciphering Egyptian hieroglyphs, a single object functions as an independent memory store.

**Digital model:** Tertiary retention is an **emergent property** of the entire network. A single photo uploaded to Facebook does not merely exist as a JPEG file; it acquires meaning within the entire network of the file, tags, comments, shares, metadata, and the platform's UI display mode. If one node of this network (e.g., the comment database) disappears, the photo's status as tertiary retention is partially damaged.

This network model forms a point of contact with Bruno Latour's **Actor-Network Theory (ANT)**. Tertiary retention is no longer a property attributable to individual objects but must be understood as a non-local effect of the entire human-technology-platform-format network.

## 5. Conceptual Resources: Han Byung-Chul, Virilio, Crary

### 5.1 Han Byung-Chul: Digital Ontology's 'Non-Reality' and Decay

In *The Disappearance of the Real (Verschwinden)* (2020), Han Byung-Chul captures a central tension of digital ontology. Digital images, he argues, "do not replace reality. Rather, they cause reality to disappear," emphasizing the **non-reality** of digital existence.

According to Han's framework, the 'disappearance' of a digital object can be understood not as physical destruction but as a return to **non-reality**. When a physical artifact burns or breaks, it undergoes real destruction. When a digital object becomes unreadable, it loses its reality itself. Digital decay is thus a form of **annihilation of reality**, ontologically distinct from physical destruction.

However, Han's analysis also has limitations. He diagnoses the non-reality of digital existence primarily from a negative perspective, but fails to account for the **resilience** unique to digital objects — the fact that identical data can exist in countless copies.

### 5.2 Paul Virilio: Dromology and Accelerated Obsolescence

Virilio's thesis in *Speed and Politics (Vitesse et Politique)* (1977) and *The Aesthetics of Disappearance (Esthétique de la Disparition)* (1980) — that the acceleration of technological development fundamentally transforms the conditions of human perception and memory — provides a direct key to understanding the accelerated nature of digital decay.

Virilio's 'dromology' diagnoses that when the speed of technological development exceeds a threshold, human cognitive and perceptual frameworks can no longer keep pace. Applied to digital decay:

- **Format evolution speed vs. human memory persistence:** The average lifespan of file formats (10-20 years) is significantly shorter than human biological memory lifespan (70-80 years).
- **Accelerated platform cycles:** The average lifespan of SaaS platforms is even shorter (5-10 years).

### 5.3 Jonathan Crary: 24/7 Temporality and the Impossibility of Memory Persistence

Crary's analysis in *24/7: Late Capitalism and the Ends of Sleep* (2013) of digital capitalism's temporality — uninterrupted operation, the end of sleep, continuous wakefulness — illuminates another layer of digital decay.

According to Crary, the 24/7 operation of digital networks compresses past and future into the present. This **persistent present** eliminates the temporal interval needed for memory to sediment. Memory accumulation requires intervals in which content can 'settle'; the 24/7 regime blocks these intervals.

From this perspective, the cause of digital decay lies not merely in technical conditions (format evolution, platform termination) but in **capitalist temporality itself**. Digital objects are designed to become 'obsolete' through constant updates, new versions, and innovative replacements, destroying the temporal conditions in which memory can stably reside.

## 6. Epiphylogenesis's Turn to Erasure — From Condition of Accumulation to Condition of Erasure

Returning to the essential question: **Must epiphylogenesis be rethought not only as a condition of accumulation but also as a condition of erasure?**

### 6.1 Thesis: Stieglerian Epiphylogenesis — Philosophy of Accumulation

Stiegler's epiphylogenesis explains how humanity has evolved by depositing layers of time onto material objects through technology. In this model, technical objects perform the function of 'preservation,' ensuring the continuity of cultural memory. Epiphylogenetic accumulation functions as the **species' memory storage**.

### 6.2 Antithesis: The Erasure Dialectic of Digital Conditions

Digital objects, due to their ontological specificity (carrier-content separation, execution environment dependency, network ontology), exist in a paradoxical condition where the mechanism of accumulation simultaneously operates as a mechanism of erasure. The same structures (platform dependency, format evolution, technological acceleration) both enable accumulation and necessitate erasure.

### 6.3 Synthesis: Accumulation-Erasure Duality

Under digital conditions, epiphylogenesis must be reconceptualized as an **accumulation-erasure duality**. This does not simply mean 'failed accumulation' or 'memory loss.' Rather, digital epiphylogenesis operates as **accumulation that always presupposes erasure** — accumulation that includes erasure within itself.

Concrete expressions of this duality:

1. **The paradox of preservation-erasure:** The infinite replicability of digital objects (maximized accumulation) produces, for exactly the same reason, the loss of originality (a form of erasure). What can be infinitely replicated does not 'truly' exist anywhere.
2. **The paradox of acceleration-sedimentation:** The production speed of digital information is unprecedented in human history, but this acceleration eliminates the temporal interval needed for information to 'sediment' into cultural memory.
3. **The paradox of storage-loss:** The plummeting cost of storage allows us to preserve more data than ever before, yet the lifespan of the infrastructure needed to access this data (software, hardware, platforms) grows shorter.

### 6.4 Concluding Proposition

**Epiphylogenesis in digital conditions is simultaneously a condition of accumulation and a condition of erasure.** This is not a mere correction of Stiegler's model but a productive expansion of his concept. The epiphylogenetic condition Stiegler diagnosed in *Technics and Time* must be understood in the digital age not as a condition of 'pure accumulation' but as a **complex ecology in which the possibility of accumulation and the inevitability of erasure coexist**.

---

## 7. Conclusion: Toward an Ecology of Digital Decay

This analysis has sought to identify the structural transformation that Stiegler's concept of epiphylogenesis undergoes under digital conditions. In conclusion, the ecology of digital decay demands a **material and ecological reconfiguration** of Stiegler's concept of tertiary retention, proceeding in the following directions:

1. **From accumulation to circulation:** Instead of viewing epiphylogenesis as simple accumulation, reconceptualize it as a cyclical process of accumulation and erasure.
2. **From individual object to network:** Redefine tertiary retention not as a property of individual digital objects but as a non-local effect of the entire human-technology-platform-format network.
3. **From physical durability to ecological resilience:** Shift the goal of digital preservation from physical durability (permanent preservation) to ecological resilience (the ability to maintain meaning across changing environments).

Digital decay is not merely a technical problem or a matter of information loss. It is a philosophical challenge demanding that the very way humanity has entrusted its memory to material objects through technological exteriorization — the way we have been 'human' — be fundamentally reconfigured under digital conditions.
