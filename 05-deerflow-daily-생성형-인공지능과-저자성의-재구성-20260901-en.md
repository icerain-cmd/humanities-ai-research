## 1. Tension Between Barthes's "Death of the Author" and AI Collaboration — Reconstructed Meaning

### 1.1 What Barthes Said and Did Not Say

Barthes (1967) argued for the deconstruction of the myth that "the author is the source of meaning." In *The Death of the Author*, the core claim is the declaration that "writing is the destruction of every voice, every point of origin." The text is not a single origin (the author's intention) but a tissue of quotations drawn from countless cultural sources, and meaning is produced not by the sender but in the field of the receiver (the reader).

However, two points must be clarified:

① **What Barthes 'killed' was not the empirical writer but the transcendental Author.** In other words, Barthes did not invalidate the question "Who wrote this text?" itself; rather, he invalidated the hermeneutic premise that "the ultimate meaning of this text lies in the author's intention."

② **Barthes's discussion is unrelated to copyright or evaluation systems.** For Barthes, the question "Who is responsible?" does not exist. This is not a limitation of Barthes, but a difference in context.

### 1.2 The Paradox AI Collaboration Poses to Barthes's Discussion

Collaboration with generative AI **paradoxically both 'proves' the death of the author and simultaneously demands a reconstruction of authorship.**

If the text produced by AI is merely a probabilistic arrangement of a language database (i.e., no true 'author' exists), then all utterances exchanged between the student and the AI are under the same condition. However, the problem is as follows:

> When Barthes says that the meaning of the text lies with the reader, that reader bears no specific responsibility. But the student submitting the assignment bears institutional responsibility for evaluation results, grades, and degrees.

This is the key reason why Barthes's discussion cannot be directly applied to this case. **Barthes discussed the politics of interpretation, not the politics of evaluation.**

## 3. Tension Between Modern Copyright's Human-Author Standard and AI Collaboration

Article 2 of the Korean Copyright Act defines a work as "a creation that expresses human thoughts or emotions." This definition includes three elements:

1. **Human** — corporations or AI cannot be authors
2. **Expression of thought/emotion** — mere data processing results are excluded
3. **Originality/creativity** — a minimum level of creative contribution is required

The U.S. Copyright Office (USCO) stated in its March 2023 guidance that AI-generated content without sufficient human creative contribution is not registrable (*Copyright Registration Guidance: Works Containing Material Generated by Artificial Intelligence*, 2023). The key criterion is **"how much creative control the human exercised."**

**Specific Points of Tension:**

| Dimension | Traditional Copyright Standard | AI Collaboration Situation |
|-----------|-------------------------------|----------------------------|
| Intention | The author's internal intent is concretized through expression | Intent is modified and reconstructed by AI output |
| Expression | The human directly performs linguistic choices | AI performs primary linguistic choices |
| Originality | Original contribution of the human mind | Human contribution may be limited to editing, selection, revision |
| Responsibility | The author bears legal and ethical responsibility | Shared responsibility with AI? → Institution cannot accept |

**The most acute tension arises in the dimensions of 'intention formation' and 'responsibility.'**

Traditionally, the author's intention was considered an independent mental event existing 'before' the work. However, in AI collaboration, the student's intention is negotiated and modified while accepting AI's rebuttals and suggestions. This is a different dimension from Barthes's 'death of the author.' Barthes expelled the author's intention from the criterion of meaning interpretation, but AI collaboration requires an epistemological shift: intention itself is negotiable and dynamically formed.

## 4. Minimum Conditions for Recognizing Student Authorship

Synthesizing the above analysis, the minimum conditions for recognizing student authorship in the context of domestic university assignment evaluation are presented. These conditions represent a 'pragmatic compromise' that accepts Barthes's hermeneutic subversion while not abandoning the necessity of institutional evaluation (attribution of responsibility).

### Condition 1: Origination of Inquiry

The student must be able to demonstrate that they **initially set the question, thesis, or analytical direction** of the assignment. This is the first editorial judgment. As Document 1 notes, "how creative the user's question is determines the level and reliability of the answer."

- **Measurability**: Initial assignment proposal, brainstorming notes, the way the first prompt sets the problem

### Condition 2: Evidence of Critical Evaluation

Evidence is needed to show that the student **critically evaluated, rather than passively accepted, the AI output**. This includes:

- Records of rejected outputs among AI suggestions
- Traces of rebuttals and alternative proposals to AI output
- Existence of AI-generated text not included in the final result

This implies that authorship lies not only in "what was included" but also in "what was excluded."

### Condition 3: Structural Authorship

It must be demonstrated that the student's judgment intervened not at the sentence level but at the level of **argument structure, citation placement, arrangement of claims, and derivation of conclusions**. As Document 2 indicates, 'editing' means not just partial revision but restructuring of the overall structure.

- **Measurement**: Comparison of the assignment outline and final version, student's meta-explanation of the arrangement of argument stages

### Condition 4: Assumption of Responsibility

This is the most important yet most overlooked condition. The student must **express willingness to accept ultimate responsibility for errors, potential plagiarism, and logical flaws in the assignment**. This includes transparent disclosure of AI usage (e.g., describing the usage process in an appendix).

- **Institutional Implication**: If this condition is not met, the student is merely a 'transmitter of AI output,' not an 'author.'

### Condition 5: Traceability of Editorial Thinking

This is the most practical condition. What universities should require is not the final product of AI collaboration itself, but the **editorial trace** of editorial thinking. When Document 1 states that "the submitted answer is not a completion but a stage of knowledge structuring," it suggests that the focus of evaluation should shift from the final text to **the quality of the generative process**.

- **Implementation**: Attach AI conversation logs, submit version history, require reflective commentary

## 5. Implications for University Evaluation Context — Conclusion

Barthes's 'death of the author' and modern copyright's human-author standard **generate two incompatible demands** in the face of AI collaboration:

- **Barthes's demand**: Deconstruct the authoritative intention of the author and acknowledge the open meaning of the text
- **Copyright's demand**: Designate a human subject who can bear responsibility

Between these two demands, the minimum condition for recognizing student authorship lies in **making visible the trajectory of cognitive work—intention formation → critical evaluation → structural judgment → assumption of responsibility**. This is precisely the core of editorial thinking performed by the human as an 'AI player.'

Ultimately, in AI collaboration, student authorship is not the authorship of 'original utterance' but the authorship of **'architect of knowledge restructuring.'** This authorship stands on an institutional compromise between the 'dead author' of Barthes and the 'responsible author' demanded by modern copyright.
