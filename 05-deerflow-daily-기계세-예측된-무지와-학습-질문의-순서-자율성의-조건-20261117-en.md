## Generative Learning Platforms and the Anticipation of Ignorance: The Machinic-Age Transformation of Education from Simondonian and Stieglerian Perspectives

### 1. Situating the Problem: Two Readings of the Machinic-Age Transformation

The scenario you describe—a platform that analyzes incorrect-answer histories, response times, and gaze data in order to adjust the difficulty and explanatory method of a problem **before the learner becomes aware of having difficulty**—is not merely a case of applying technology. It is an experimental scene that asks what the **machinic-age transformation** concretely means in the domain of education. According to Khoj Reference 1, the machinic age is “a civilizational-historical transformation in which platforms, algorithms, and nonhuman orders reconstruct the human condition” [Reference 1]. When this definition is applied to education, two opposing readings become possible.

**Reading A—the Automation Thesis**: The machinic-age transformation consists in making education more efficient. The teacher’s intuitive judgments—where a student is stuck, what kind of explanation is needed—are **replaced** by data-driven predictive models. The purpose of education remains the same as before—transmitting knowledge and developing competence—while only the means are optimized.

**Reading B—the Anticipation Thesis**: This transformation reconstructs the **temporality of education itself**. The issue is not the replacement of the teacher’s labor. Rather, the sequence through which the learner encounters their own ignorance—feeling that something is “difficult,” forming a question, and seeking an answer—is **anticipated** by the model’s prediction. The learner’s question is no longer a spontaneous emergence, but a subsequent event responding to a “probabilistic profile of ignorance” that the model has already calculated.

My argument is as follows: **The automation thesis captures only the surface of the machinic-age transformation. The anticipation thesis is fundamental.** Yet anticipation does not necessarily mean the disappearance of the learner’s subjectivity. Under certain conditions, anticipation can open the learner’s possibilities; under others, it can confine the learner to particular paths. Learners can also develop distinctive strategies of resistance to the system. I will analyze these conditions below through the concepts of Simondon and Stiegler.

### 2. Simondon’s Theory of Individuation: The Learner’s Preindividual Charge and the Paradox of Prediction

For Simondon, individuation does not mean an already formed individual interacting with an environment. It is the process through which **preindividual reality** resolves itself through a problematic situation. The individual always remains in a state of being “more than one” (*plus qu’un*), possessing a **preindividual charge**. This charge receives the opportunity for further individuation only when it encounters new problem-conditions.

#### 2.1 Conditions Under Which Prediction Opens Possibilities: Maintaining Problematization

The platform’s prediction opens the learner’s possibilities when the prediction functions as a **medium for transformation**. In Simondonian terms, prediction should provide an occasion for **metastabilization**—an occasion that enables the learner to move beyond their current state. More specifically:

1. **The Paradoxical Maintenance of Optimal Difficulty**: When the platform continually places the learner in a state that is “neither too difficult nor too easy,” it maintains the conditions under which the learner can activate their preindividual charge. This corresponds to what Simondon calls the **modulation of individuation**—the continuation of individuation when the environment is modulated in accordance with the individual’s rhythm. Prediction functions as the sensory organ of this modulation.

2. **Preserving Ignorance**: Possibilities open up when prediction does not eliminate every difficulty in advance, but instead **leaves room** for the learner to encounter their own “not-knowing.” For Simondon, individuation is not only a process of solving problems; it also includes the way in which a problem is experienced **as a problem**. If a platform provides a hint too early, the problem may be eliminated, but individuation does not take place.

3. **Juxtaposing Heterogeneous Data Series**: Gaze data, response times, and patterns of incorrect answers each have a different temporality (*temporalité*). When they are not integrated into a single score but presented to the learner as distinct series, the learner can gain **critical distance** from their own cognitive process. This makes possible the transductive dimension of individuation—the cycle through which the individual objectifies the conditions of its own activity and then re-subjectivizes them.

#### 2.2 Conditions Under Which Prediction Constrains Possibilities: The Interruption and Formalization of the Cycle

Prediction confines the learner to particular paths when it replaces **open temporality** with **closed probability**.

1. **The Statistical Domination of the Past**: Platform predictions are always based on the learner’s **past behavioral data**. From Simondon’s perspective, this is a fatal limitation. Individuation includes a rupture with the past. When a learner begins to think in a qualitatively different way from before, past data is no longer a reliable indicator of future behavior. Yet predictive models essentially assume an **extension of the past**. As a result, the platform fails to recognize the learner’s qualitative leap and continues to confine the learner within a probabilistic profile derived from the past.

2. **Algorithmic Synesthesia in the Adjustment of Explanations**: A more serious problem arises when the explanatory method is pre-adjusted. If the platform analyzes the learner’s pattern of incorrect answers and **optimizes the next explanation in advance**, the learner loses the opportunity to reconstruct for themselves why they chose the incorrect answer or what conceptual confusion they experienced. From Simondon’s perspective, this is the moment when the most important phase of individuation—**discovery**—is replaced by **transmission**. What reaches the learner is not the process of solving a problem, but the result of a problem that has already been solved.

3. **The Imposition of Schematization**: For Simondon, cognition involves schematization—the active formative capacity that organizes sensory material. Yet when a platform automatically constructs the chain “the learner is looking at this section → the learner is stuck on this concept” by analyzing gaze data, the learner’s own **process of schematization is externalized**. The learner loses the opportunity to experience for themselves how their attention is flowing. Once the direction of attention becomes a visualized output of the platform rather than an immanent activity of the learner, schematization becomes an instrument of control rather than an occasion for individuation.

### 3. Stiegler’s Technological Memory: Tertiary Retention and the Grammatisation of Learning

In *Technics and Time*, Stiegler argues that human memory consists of three levels. **Primary retention** is the immediate retention of present perception; **secondary retention** is the recollection of past experience; and **tertiary retention** is memory stored in external technical media such as writing, recordings, and databases. Tertiary retention enables human beings to objectify and transmit their cognitive processes, but it also **discretizes** those processes by dividing continuous flows into discontinuous units.

#### 3.1 The Grammatisation of Learning and the Structure of Anticipation

The operation performed by a generative learning platform is, in Stiegler’s terms, the **grammatisation of learning**. The learner’s history of incorrect answers, response times, and gaze data are externally stored as tertiary retention. Yet this stage is not simply storage. These data are subsequently **reconstructed by algorithms into predictive models** and used to **anticipate** the learner’s future behavior.

Here Stiegler provides an important insight that extends beyond Simondon. According to Stiegler, tertiary retention does not merely preserve the past; it **conditions the temporality of the future itself**. When the learner’s “not-yet-knowing”—what Stiegler describes as the “positive dimension of ignorance”—is replaced by a probabilistic profile generated by the platform, the learner’s future is no longer a field of open possibilities. It becomes a **set of trajectories that have already been calculated**.

This parallels precisely the logic of the **prediction industry** that Stiegler criticizes in *The Symbolic Misery of Consumer Society*. Predictive models present individual behavior as “free choice,” but in practice they structure the space of choices in advance. In the educational context, this produces the following paradox: the learner feels that they are “learning freely,” but that very feeling is constituted within the platform’s predictive range.

#### 3.2 How the Anticipation of Ignorance Reorders the Sequence of Question Formation

The most radical effect of this platform lies in the **reconstruction of the temporal sequence through which questions are formed**. In traditional learning, the formation of a question follows this sequence:

```text
(1) Encounter with a problem → (2) Cognitive resistance (sensing difficulty)
→ (3) Formation of a question → (4) Searching for an answer
```

A generative platform reorganizes this sequence as follows:

```text
(1) Encounter with a problem → (2) [The model predicts difficulty on the basis of data]
→ (3) The difficulty/explanatory method is adjusted in advance
→ (4) The learner is exposed to the adjusted environment
```

What matters is that the learner’s **experience is omitted** at stage (2). Before the learner feels that something is difficult, that feeling has already been predicted and addressed. This is a new form of what Stiegler discusses in *Technics and Time 2* as the **“appropriation of time.”** Someone—the model—experiences the learner’s future sensation in advance, while the learner is presented only with the result as their “present.”

The reorganization of question formation has deeper implications. The learner’s question is no longer an **expression of their own ignorance**, but a **response to the model’s prediction**. Rather than asking, “Why is this difficult?” the learner enters a metacognitive loop: “The model predicted that this would be difficult for me, so am I currently in a difficult state?” In Stiegler’s terms, **tertiary retention—the model’s prediction—is inserted between primary retention—the sensory experience of difficulty—and secondary retention—the cognitive expression of that difficulty**. The immediacy of the learner’s experience is thereby interrupted.

### 4. Moments of Resistance: The Strategic Value of Incorrect Answers, Silence, and Detours

Yet the learner is not passive in relation to this system. As you point out in the question, learners can deliberately choose incorrect answers, silence, or detours in order to disrupt the model’s inferences. The theoretical analysis of these moments reveals the political dimension of education in the machinic age.

#### 4.1 Deliberate Incorrect Answers: Revolt Against the Model’s Labels

For a model to learn the learner’s “pattern of incorrect answers” means that it classifies incorrect answers under **specific labels**, such as “confusion between Concept A and Concept B.” When a learner **deliberately chooses an incorrect answer to a concept they understand well**, this constitutes a **meta-intervention** into the model’s classification system. The learner sends the signal: “You have classified me in this way, but I do not recognize that classification.”

From Simondon’s perspective, this is a **positive manifestation of the preindividual charge**. The learner rejects an external determination—the model’s profile—and asserts their own singular path of individuation. What matters is that this rejection is not a rejection of learning, but a rejection of classification. The learner continues to learn, but does so outside the range of the model’s prediction.

From Stiegler’s perspective, the deliberate incorrect answer is an attempt to **restore primary retention—the learner’s own cognitive state—against tertiary retention—the model’s classification**. The learner asserts: “I know better than your prediction what I currently know and do not know.” This is a form of the strategy of **restoration**, an attempt to reconstruct subjectivity within technical conditions.

#### 4.2 Silence and Delayed Responses: Disrupting the Flow of Data

The collection of response time as data means that the learner’s **time has been discretized into measurable units**. When the learner deliberately delays a response or chooses silence, they disrupt the model’s **temporal data flow**. The model assumes that a “rational learner” will respond within a certain amount of time. When the learner violates this assumption, the model’s predictive accuracy declines.

In Simondonian terms, silence is an act that **breaks the rhythm of modulation**. The platform attempts to induce individuation by modulating itself according to the learner’s rhythm, but when the learner deliberately chooses a timeout or delay, such modulation becomes impossible. By generating no data, silence interrupts the circulation of the data economy—the attention economy.

More deeply, silence can be read as an attempt to recover the distinctive temporality of learning. The “response time” demanded by the model is an externally imposed rhythm. Genuine learning sometimes requires a **pause**—a period in which no productive action takes place. In *Individuation in Light of the Notions of Form and Information*, Simondon argues that individuation is not continuous but **rhythmic**. Learning does not take place at every moment; it takes place through the alternation of activity and rest, output and silence. Silence is an essential component of this rhythm.

#### 4.3 Detour (*détour*): Asserting Nonlinear Learning Paths

Platform prediction always assumes an **optimal path**. The model calculates that “the most efficient way for this learner to move from current state B to state C is X.” Yet when the learner deliberately solves a different problem, ignores the recommended sequence, or searches for material that the system has not suggested, they enact a **politics of the non-optimal**.

The detour has especially rich implications within Simondon’s theory of individuation. For Simondon, the path of individuation is not determined in advance. Each event of individuation occurs in a **unique** manner, and this uniqueness is an essential property of individuation. When the learner takes a detour, they are making an ontological claim: “No model can predict the path of my individuation.”

Reference 5, “Conceptual Formulation in the Age of Technogenesis,” addresses this problem within a broader framework. The transition to the age of technogenesis brings together “the co-generation of humans and AI, the co-individual subject WE, and the generative platform order” as a single historical phase [Reference 1]. The learner’s detour is an act of renegotiating the conditions of this co-generation. The learner does not passively accept the AI’s outputs—the recommended path or optimized explanation—but feeds their own outputs—their choices, paths, and strategies—back into the system.

### 5. Redefining Educational Autonomy: From the Inner Self to Distribution

To answer your final question—whether educational autonomy can be redefined not as an inner, independent will, but as a problem of learning environments in which prediction, feedback, refusal, and redesign are distributed—I will synthesize the preceding analysis.

#### 5.1 The Limits of the Inner Model of Autonomy

Modern educational philosophy, from Kant to developmental psychology, has primarily understood autonomy as **inner independence**: the capacity to judge and decide for oneself without external influence. Within this model, the learning environment is positioned as “external,” and the autonomous subject proves its independence by maintaining distance from that external environment.

Yet the scenario you describe shows that this model no longer functions adequately. The learning environment—the platform’s predictions, difficulty adjustments, and explanatory methods—already **intervenes internally** in the learner’s cognitive process. The learner can no longer secure autonomy through “distance from the outside,” because that distance itself is measured, analyzed, and predicted by the platform.

#### 5.2 Conditions of Distributed Autonomy

Drawing on the insights of Simondon and Stiegler, educational autonomy can be redefined as a **property of environments in which the cycle of prediction, feedback, refusal, and redesign is distributed**. This redefinition includes four conditions.

**Condition 1: The Visibility and Contestability of Prediction**

The first condition of distributed autonomy is that the platform’s predictions must be **visible** to the learner and **open to contestation**. Most learning platforms currently render prediction as a black box: learners cannot see the profile constructed about them or understand how that profile was produced. To distribute autonomy, learners must be able to view the platform’s predictions, challenge them, and revise their own data.

This is a question of what Stiegler calls **the right of access to tertiary retention**. If the learner’s cognitive process has been externalized as tertiary retention—data, models, and profiles—then this tertiary retention must be **open to the learner and negotiable**. Otherwise, tertiary retention becomes an instrument of control.

**Condition 2: The Duality of Feedback**

The platform’s feedback should not be one-directional—from system to learner—but bidirectional: system → learner ↔ learner → system. The learner must not only receive feedback about their learning state; they must also be able to **submit their own feedback** concerning the system’s predictions and adjustments.

In Simondonian terms, this means that the **feedback loop must be designed to support, but not dominate, the modulation of individuation**. Feedback should sustain the conditions of individuation without determining its direction. This is similar to the way an ecosystem’s circulation of nutrients supports life without determining the evolutionary direction of any particular species.

**Condition 3: Securing Spaces of Refusal**

One of the most important conditions of distributed autonomy is the existence of a **space in which refusal is possible**. Learners must be able to reject the system’s predictions, and such rejection must not be labeled as “noncooperation.” This becomes possible when the environment recognizes the strategic value of deliberate incorrect answers, silence, and detours analyzed above.

The key point is to recognize refusal as **not an interruption of learning but another mode of learning**. Simondon’s theory of individuation suggests that paths of individuation are diverse and that no single path can be designated as the “correct” one. Refusal is the moment when one path of individuation shifts into another; this shift itself is an essential occasion for individuation.

Reference 3’s discussion of “the design of co-individual learning environments and distributed methods of knowledge assessment” acquires concrete significance in this context [Reference 2]. Refusal is an essential function of a co-individual subject. Co-individuation is not complete synchronization; it is maintained through a rhythm of synchronization and divergence. The learner’s refusal is a moment of divergence, revealing that the co-individual subject is not simple synchronization but **negotiated coordination**.

**Condition 4: Distributing the Authority to Redesign**

The most radical condition is to grant learners **some authority to redesign the environment**. Learners must not merely respond to given conditions; they must be able to modify the conditions themselves. This includes access to parameters for adjusting difficulty, the ability to choose explanatory methods, and the capacity to reconstruct the learning path.

Reference 4’s discussion of “the transition from the age of technical editing to the age of technogenesis” provides an important implication here [Reference 4]. Just as technical editing mediated between reproduction and generation, learners in an environment of distributed autonomy occupy the position of **editors of their own learning environments**. Learners are no longer merely consumers of content, nor simply generators of content; they are editors of the conditions themselves. This is the most positive possibility that education in the machinic age may open.

#### 5.3 A New Definition of Autonomy

On the basis of the preceding analysis, I propose redefining educational autonomy as follows:

> **Educational autonomy is not the expression of an individual’s inner will, but a systemic property of a learning environment in which prediction, feedback, refusal, and redesign are distributed. In such an environment, autonomy is realized when the learner (1) can see and contest predictions made about them, (2) can submit their own feedback concerning the system’s feedback, (3) is guaranteed a space in which to refuse the system’s predictions, and (4) possesses some authority to redesign the conditions of the learning environment.**

The central point of this definition is that autonomy is understood not as a **state**, but as a **relation**—specifically, as the way in which four relational axes are distributed: prediction, feedback, refusal, and redesign. The learner is not autonomous simply because they are a “free individual.” The learner can be autonomous when embedded in an environment organized in a particular way. And the organization of that environment is determined by the design of its technical infrastructure—algorithms, data, and interfaces.

This is the most radical implication that Simondon’s theory of individuation offers to educational philosophy. Individuation occurs not through separation from the environment, but within a particular relationship with the environment. An environment that supports individuation is not merely one that does not restrict the individual’s movement—passive freedom—but one that continually provides occasions for the individual to activate their preindividual charge—active conditioning.

### 6. Conclusion: The Dialectic of Anticipation and Openness

Let us return to your first question. Does the machinic-age transformation consist in automating education, or in reorganizing the sequence of questions through the anticipation of ignorance?

My answer is as follows.

**The machinic-age transformation lies fundamentally in anticipation.** Yet this anticipation does not produce a single outcome. It can operate in two directions.

**Closed anticipation** shortens the temporal and cognitive space in which the learner’s question can form, placing the learner perpetually in a state of being “already answered.” The learner loses the opportunity to confront their own ignorance and moves passively along the optimized path provided by the system. This is especially likely in the absence of the “algorithmic regulatory framework” and “digital ecosystem management principles” cautioned against in Reference 2 [Reference 2].

**Open anticipation** gives the learner distance from which to objectify their own cognitive process. When the platform shows the learner, “This is how I predict you,” the learner can ask, “In what ways might I differ from this prediction?” Prediction then becomes not the endpoint of learning, but an **occasion** for the learner to question themselves. This is the creative possibility that the machinic age may open.

The shared insight of Simondon and Stiegler is that these two directions are not inherent in technology itself. They are determined by the **political conditions of technological design and use**. The same predictive algorithm can produce entirely different educational effects depending on how its predictions are presented to the learner and how the learner is able to respond to them.

Therefore, the central question of education in the machinic age should not be, “Will AI automate education?” It should be: **“Under what conditions does prediction open the learner’s possibilities, and under what conditions does it close them?”** Answering this question requires considering technical design—the visibility of prediction, its contestability, and the authority to redesign—together with institutional conditions—data ownership and algorithmic auditing—and educational practices, including a culture that recognizes learners’ resistance and refusal.

Within your research framework, this issue occupies a central place among the tasks of the **age of technogenesis**. As Reference 1 points out, the age of technogenesis is the work of “bringing the co-generation of humans and AI, the co-individual subject WE, and the generative platform order together as a single historical phase” [Reference 1]. Understanding the machinic-age transformation of education as a problem of anticipation provides a theoretical foundation for constructing a new form of educational subjectivity under the conditions of the age of technogenesis: the learner as a co-individual subject.
