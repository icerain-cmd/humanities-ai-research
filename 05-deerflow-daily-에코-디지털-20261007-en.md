## 1. Framing the Problem: Epistemological Limits of Efficiency Indicators

A pilot project that circulates public data center waste heat to local greenhouses yields ostensibly clear "environmental achievements." Carbon emissions are reduced, fossil-fuel-based heating is replaced, and the data center's energy efficiency (PUE, Power Usage Effectiveness) improves. These achievements are positively quantified within the framework of **efficiency indicators**.

However, the paradox you have precisely identified—that waste heat reuse operates as a mechanism to expand computational demand and surveillance infrastructure—implies that the evaluation framework itself must be called into question. This paradox is not merely an "unintended consequence" but an **epistemological blind spot structurally produced by efficiency indicators**.

## 2. The Digital Version of Jevons' Paradox: The Efficiency Paradox

Web search results show that this paradox has already been empirically confirmed:

- **Digital Reenactment of Jevons' Paradox**: SIGARCH and numerous academic papers have demonstrated that improvements in data center efficiency actually lead to an explosive increase in computational demand—a rebound effect[citation:The Jevons Paradox: Why Efficiency Alone Won’t Solve Our Data Center Carbon Challenge](https://www.sigarch.org/the-jevons-paradox-why-efficiency-alone-wont-solve-our-data-center-carbon-challenge). Since 2020, driven by AI and digitalization, data center power consumption has transitioned from a plateau to a rapid growth trend, suggesting that efficiency alone cannot control total resource consumption.

- **Transfer of the Urban Heat Island Effect**: Empirical research in Phoenix has shown that data center waste heat raises surrounding surface temperatures by up to 16°F and air temperatures by up to 4°F, with effects extending to a radius of 0.5 km[citation:Data Centers, Urban Heat, and AI Growth - Yale Environment](https://environment.yale.edu/news/article/data-centers-urban-heat-and-ai-growth). Even if waste heat is circulated to greenhouses, the problem remains localized because the increasing computational density of data centers themselves (over 100 kW/rack) creates new heat sources.

- **"Nature Cities" Study**: A recent study (2025) by Chen et al. formalized the "digital Jevons paradox" in urban data center energy systems, showing that improvements in algorithmic efficiency do not reduce AI's energy footprint but rather expand it[citation:Digital Jevons paradox in urban data center energy systems](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44284-025-00289-9.pdf). This indicates that the limits of efficiency indicators are not merely a theoretical warning but a quantitatively confirmed phenomenon.

These pieces of evidence support the accuracy of your problematization: **Efficiency indicators fail to capture the cycle of causal responsibility.** As efficiency improves, the additional computation enabled by that improved efficiency generates new environmental burdens—a self-acceleration structure that lies outside the purview of efficiency indicators.

## 4. Reconstructing Three Evaluation Indicators: Analysis of Material, Energy, and Space Arrangement

Synthesizing the above theoretical resources, an evaluation framework to replace efficiency indicators can be constructed by tracking the redistribution process along three axes: **material, energy, and space**.

### 4.1. Material: Redistribution of Flows

Efficiency indicators focus on the final figure of "reduced carbon emissions." In contrast, the perspective of material flow redistribution asks:

- **How has the physical path of waste heat been reconfigured?** (Previously: released into the atmosphere → New path: circulation to greenhouses)
- **What new material dependencies has this reconfiguration created?** (The greenhouse now depends on the continuity of the data center—a data center shutdown means a heating shutdown for the greenhouse)
- **Has the reconfigured flow replaced existing flows (fossil-fuel-based heating) or augmented them?** (If waste heat supplied to the greenhouse replaced existing heating, that is positive; but if it enabled the construction of additional greenhouses, an augmentation effect occurs)

This perspective is important because while efficiency indicators only quantify simple **"reduction,"** material flow analysis captures **"creation of dependency"** and **"augmentation"** as well.

### 4.2. Energy: Topology of Transformation

Efficiency indicators (PUE, power efficiency) focus on the energy conversion efficiency inside the data center. In contrast, the topological perspective of energy transformation considers:

- **Across what temperature gradient does energy move?** (Data center waste heat is low-temperature heat at 25–60°C, sufficient for greenhouse heating but unsuitable for high-temperature processes)
- **How does this movement change the topology of the overall energy system?** (The energy coupling of data center and greenhouse synchronizes the operation of the two systems—an increase in synchronization rate SR(t))
- **What energy hierarchies are created or destroyed in the transformation process?** (High-quality electricity is converted to low-quality heat, and then that heat is redefined as "useful energy")

The concept of synchronization rate from Document 3 plays an important role here: **an increase in SR(t) means that the energy relationship between the two systems takes on emergent characteristics**, which is the emergence of a new energy topology that cannot be explained by the efficiency of individual systems.

### 4.3. Space: Deterritorialization and Reterritorialization

This is the most easily overlooked yet most important axis. Efficiency indicators almost completely omit the spatial dimension. In contrast, the spatial redistribution perspective asks:

- **How is the location of the data center redefined?** (Waste heat circulation redefines the data center from a mere "computational facility" to a "local energy hub"—a change in the political and ecological meaning of its location)
- **What new spatial dependencies does the greenhouse's location create?** (The greenhouse must be adjacent to the data center—this affects land use, logistics, and labor markets)
- **What spatial exclusion does this redistribution entail?** (Not every region can install a greenhouse next to a data center—differential access occurs based on real estate prices, infrastructure accessibility, and local political bargaining power)

The redistribution of space directly connects to the dimension you call "living conditions." The siting decision for a data center is no longer merely a matter of technical and economic efficiency but becomes a political question of **who receives heat and who endures the heat island effect**.

The theoretical basis for this framework shift can be supported by concepts you have developed:

**Asymmetric Responsibility of WE/Resonant Subject**: Waste heat circulation creates "resonance" between two systems (data center and greenhouse), but this resonance is not symmetric. The greenhouse is dependent on the data center's computational activity (if the data center stops, the greenhouse stops), while the data center gains an opportunity to improve its efficiency indicators through the greenhouse's existence. This asymmetric dependency relationship is not captured by individual efficiency figures.

**Two Layers of Environmentalization**: The circulation of data center waste heat to greenhouses superficially corresponds to primary environmentalization (turning waste into a resource). However, simultaneously, secondary environmentalization proceeds—the process by which the data center, by functioning as part of the local ecosystem, becomes "naturalized" in its very existence. When the data center is no longer seen as an "intruder" but as "part of the environment," the critical distance for its expansion disappears. Efficiency indicators fail to capture this secondary environmentalization.

**The Pharmakon Structure of M₄**: Waste heat circulation clearly possesses the character of a remedy (pharmakon)—reduced carbon emissions, improved energy efficiency. But it also possesses the character of a poison—expansion of computational demand, reinforcement of surveillance infrastructure, creation of local dependencies. As the M₄ model shows, this remedy and poison are inseparable; they are two faces of the same topological movement.
