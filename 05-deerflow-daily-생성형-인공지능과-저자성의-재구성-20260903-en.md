## The Authorship Problem in Generative AI Assignments — A Contrastive Analysis of Traditional Authorship Concepts and the Actual Work Process

### 1. Problem Setting: Three Axes of the Traditional Authorship Concept

The traditional concept of authorship has been established around three main requirements: **Intention** — the text must express the author's thoughts and intentions; **Expression control** — the author must substantially determine the linguistic form and content of the text; **Responsibility** — the author must be able to bear ultimate responsibility for the truthfulness and impact of the text. These three axes appear repeatedly in different normative contexts, from the "creativity" standard of copyright law to the ICMJE authorship criteria (substantial contribution, drafting or critical revision, final approval, accountability).

The case posited by the question — a student uses their own notes and references to instruct an AI to generate a draft, then revises some sentences of the generated text before submitting — simultaneously pressures all three axes. The student's notes contain intention, but the AI's predictive process transforms that intention; the student exercises partial control through prompts and post-editing, but the sentence-level generation itself is delegated to the AI; the student formally bears submission responsibility, but structural tension arises because they assume responsibility without fully understanding the generation process.

---

### 2. The Problem of Intention: The Gap Between Continuous Intention and Discrete Generation

The student's pre-written notes and references clearly contain the student's cognitive intention. These notes are the product of the student's reading, understanding, selection, and structuring. However, the process by which this intention is translated into an AI draft is not merely a "tool extension" but a "delegation involving transformation."

**Draxler et al. (2023)'s AI Ghostwriter Effect** study empirically captures this point. Participants showed a tendency to attribute ownership of AI-generated text to the AI, yet still listed their own names in authorship declarations. This suggests a paradoxical split in intention. The student feels that the text generated by the AI based on their own notes "came from my intention," while simultaneously holding the perception that "these sentences are not what I wrote." The study found that this inconsistency was observed in over 70% of participants, and crucially, **a subjective sense of control over the text was the strongest predictor of ownership perception**.

The key issue here is the gap between the "continuity" of intention and the "discreteness" of generation. The intention embedded in the student's notes is a product of a continuous cognitive process, but the AI's text generation occurs through probabilistic leaps. When the student writes concept 'A' in their notes, the AI may generate 'B' that is semantically related, but 'B' may contain implications, assumptions, or argumentative paths the student did not intend. Current LLM architectures lack mechanisms to identify or block such "unintended implications" during generation.

Local reference 1 (Document 1, score 0.038) offers meaningful insight at this point. The statement "Generative AI can now directly engage with our thinking beyond being a simple editing tool because the knowledge structuring of artificial nature internalizes the order of artificial nature" implies that the AI does not simply 'transmit' intention but 'reconstructs' it according to its own structuring principles. The 'natural' knowledge order of the student's notes is transformed during the AI's 'artificial-natural' knowledge structuring process. The degree of this transformation can be quantified with current technology (e.g., Guo et al. 2025's BERTScore-based approach), but whether the transformation lies within the author's intended scope is empirically difficult to determine.

### 3. The Problem of Expression Control: Replacement by "Editing"

Expression control, as required by traditional authorship, means substantial decision-making power over the linguistic form of the text (word choice, sentence structure, argument arrangement, rhetorical strategies). In the case presented, the student's control operates at two levels.

**Pre-generation control (prompt level):** The student gives generation instructions to the AI based on notes and references. At this stage, the student exercises macro-level control over topic, scope, and perspective. However, no matter how sophisticated the prompt, it does not control the "linguistic form of the text to be generated" but rather sets "content conditions for the text to be generated." The BERTScore-based Recall measure proposed by Guo et al. (2025) quantifies how much human information from the prompt is preserved in the generated text. Even if this value is close to 1 (i.e., all prompt information is included in the generated text), the "linguistic form" in which that information is expressed is determined by the AI's predictive process.

**Post-generation control (editing level):** The student revises "some sentences" of the AI-generated draft. This corresponds to the 'Editing' condition in Draxler et al.'s experiment (up to 30 words editable). The results showed that the Editing condition induced a lower sense of ownership than Writing (direct writing) but higher than Choosing (selection only) or Getting (use without modification). However, interestingly, **the ownership differences among all AI-related conditions (excluding Writing) were not statistically significant**. That is, whether participants edited 30 words, selected one of three options, or simply copied the text, they perceived all these conditions as qualitatively different from "I wrote it entirely myself."

This suggests that the act of "revising some sentences" is insufficient to meet the standard of "expression control" required by traditional authorship. More fundamentally, as local reference 2 (Document 2, score 0.039) points out — "Instead of 'authority' and 'hierarchy', 'density' and 'probability' become the basis of trust, so 'reading' is replaced by 'editing'" — the very nature of expression control is changing. A traditional author exercises control as the "producer" of the text, whereas in AI-assisted writing, the student exercises control as the "selector" or "editor" of the text. These two modes of control are qualitatively different, and the traditional concept of authorship does not recognize the latter as a sufficient condition for authorship.

### 4. The Problem of Responsibility: Separation of Epistemic and Formal Responsibility

**Stoyanov (2026)'s Ethical LLM-Assisted Research Framework** provides the most systematic analysis of this issue. Stoyanov models the responsibility structure of human-AI collaborative research through five functions:

- **O(g)**: provenance of proposition g — human/machine/mixed
- **V(g)**: whether appropriate verification of g has been completed
- **R(g)**: set of human agents responsible for evaluating g
- **M(g)**: whether a responsible human has explicitly approved the epistemic disposition of g and accepted responsibility
- **E(g)**: epistemic outcome of verification (+1 accept, 0 undecided, -1 reject)

The core proposition of this framework is that **"the justification of scientific reasoning is determined primarily by appropriate verification and accountable human ownership, not by the degree of machine involvement."** More importantly, Stoyanov defines the acceptance condition as **E(g)=+1 AND M(g)=1**, and **does not include O(g) (provenance) in the acceptance condition**.

This has the following implications for the case in question. The condition for the student's output to be recognized as a work of authorship should not be "how much did the AI generate (O(g))" but rather "did the student appropriately verify the generated content (V(g)=1)" and "did the student explicitly accept responsibility for that content (M(g)=1)". However, in reality, can the student properly verify the arguments, references, and factual claims generated by the AI? Given the nature of current LLMs, the generated text may appear factual but actually contain hallucinated content, and verifying it requires the student to possess domain knowledge surpassing the AI — a paradox.

Draxler et al.'s study supports this paradox. Participants showed a tendency to **list their own names while not feeling ownership** over AI-generated text. The researchers termed this the AI Ghostwriter Effect, and it was more pronounced in the AI ghostwriter condition compared to the human ghostwriter condition. That is, people more easily "exploit" AI ghostwriters than human ghostwriters (algorithm exploitation). This risks a dual avoidance of responsibility: the student partially avoids responsibility for the accuracy of the content on the grounds that "the AI generated it," and the AI bears no responsibility because it is not a legal subject.

Another passage from local reference 1 connects this issue to the knowledge crisis of artificial nature: "A knowledge crisis occurs when existing knowledge cannot interpret new phenomena. And this is closely related to changes in the mechanisms of knowledge production and consumption." This suggests that the difficulty of applying traditional responsibility concepts to AI-involved writing is not merely a technical problem but stems from **a change in the mechanism of knowledge production itself**.

### 5. Overall Assessment and Theoretical Implications

#### 5.1 Assessment: Conditional Authorship

A definitive "yes/no" answer to the case is not academically appropriate. Instead, we need to introduce the concept of conditional authorship.

**The student's output does not sufficiently meet the criteria of intention, expressive control, and responsibility required by the traditional concept of authorship.** Specifically:

- **Intention**: The intention embedded in the student's notes is transformed during the AI's probabilistic generation process. It is difficult to consider that the student intended all implications of the final text.
- **Expression control**: Sentence-level generation was handled by the AI, and the student's control was limited to prompts and limited post-editing. This is not the level of expression control required by traditional authorship.
- **Responsibility**: While the student can bear formal responsibility (submission, signing), it remains questionable whether they can fully bear epistemic responsibility for the generation process.

However, this assessment must acknowledge two important limitations.

**First, the traditional concept of authorship itself is in flux.** Major academic institutions such as ICMJE, Nature, and arXiv are developing policies that, rather than prohibiting the use of AI tools, require transparent disclosure and place ultimate responsibility on human authors. This means the traditional concept of authorship is evolving in response to the challenge of generative AI.

**Second, the alternative criteria proposed by Stoyanov's framework offer meaningful insight.** When using V(g) (verification) and M(g) (responsibility acceptance) as criteria instead of O(g) (provenance), if the student thoroughly verifies the AI-generated content and explicitly accepts responsibility, the reasons to completely deny authorship of the output diminish. The problem is how thoroughly such verification can be carried out in practice.

#### 5.2 Solution: Application of the 4+4 Environmentalization Mechanism

Here, we can extend the indirect implications of the local references. The 4+4 environmentalization mechanism proposed by Artificial Nature theory (4 machine elements + 4 human elements) allows the interpretation that the AI functions not merely as a tool but as an environment in which the student performs the cognitive practice of writing. From this perspective, the student's authorship should be evaluated based on the **'mode of participation' in the process** rather than on 'ownership' of the output.

Specific measures include:
1. **Mandatory submission of prompt-generation-revision logs**: to make the student's cognitive contribution demonstrable.
2. **Introduction of epistemic audits**: as proposed by Stoyanov, requiring structured records that trace the provenance, verification, and responsibility for each key claim.
3. **Explicit separation of AI usage transparency and human responsibility**: the student transparently discloses the AI's contribution and specifies which parts they have verified and can take responsibility for.

---

### 6. Conclusion

The authorship problem of university writing assignments involving generative AI is a complex question that cannot be resolved with a binary "yes/no" answer. There exists a structural gap between the criteria of intention, expressive control, and responsibility demanded by the traditional concept of authorship and the actual work process. This gap is empirically and theoretically confirmed at various levels, including the AI Ghostwriter Effect (Draxler et al. 2023), participation detection obfuscation (Guo et al. 2025), and the problem of delegating epistemic responsibility (Stoyanov 2026).

While it is difficult to recognize the student's output as a "work" in the traditional sense, this does not necessarily mean it is "academic misconduct." A more productive approach is to develop a **conditional authorship model** conditioned on transparency, verifiability, and explicit assignment of responsibility. This model can be established at the intersection of existing ICMJE criteria, the CRediT taxonomy, and emerging Artificial Nature theory, and will provide a new normative foundation for writing education and assessment in the era of generative AI.
