## Introduction: The Paradox of Restoration

Ecological restoration in former mining areas is not merely a technical task. A restoration platform that combines eDNA analysis, drone imagery, soil sensors, and residents’ ecological observations appears, on the surface, to realize scientific objectivity and democratic participation simultaneously. Yet the question of whether this system is truly a tool for “objectively” restoring the ecosystem that existed before its degradation—or instead a device that newly selects ecological objects according to which species and traces it recognizes as “nature to be restored”—raises a more fundamental ontological question.

This question reaches the core of the concept of artificial nature. Artificial nature refers to a hybrid reality that can no longer be captured through a binary opposition between nature and technology. A former-mine restoration platform is a paradigmatic example of such artificial nature. Within a cycle that “datafies” nature and “naturalizes” data, it constructs the object of restoration. Drawing on Isabelle Stengers’s concept of cosmopolitics and Gilles Deleuze’s concept of the virtual, this essay argues that the platform is not simply a measuring tool but an apparatus of ontological selection. It also analyzes the conditions under which changes in microorganisms that sensors fail to detect, residents’ seasonal memories, and the reproductive rhythms of species excluded by the model could cause restoration plans to be suspended or revised. Furthermore, when administrative success indicators conflict with the unpredictable recovery of living beings in the field, it will argue whether ecological facts can be understood not in terms of data accuracy or the restoration of an original state, but as a procedure through which heterogeneous actors renegotiate the range of restoration possibilities through intervention, objection, and waiting.

## 1. The Politics of Measurement: eDNA and Drones Construct “Nature to Be Restored”

### 1.1. The Ontology of a Datafied Ecosystem

The eDNA analysis, drone imagery, and soil sensors used by the former-mine restoration platform each “translate” the ecosystem in a particular way. eDNA estimates the presence of species through genetic traces remaining in soil or water, but it does not capture the behavior, physiological condition, or interactions of living organisms. Drone imagery visualizes the distribution and changes of vegetation, but renders invisible the mycelial networks beneath the surface and microbial communities in the soil. Soil sensors measure physicochemical indicators such as moisture, pH, and temperature, but the qualitative significance of these indicators for living beings lies outside their range of measurement.

These systems of measurement exemplify what Stengers calls the “cosmopolitics of modern science.” According to Stengers, science does not discover neutral facts; rather, it presents as “reality” facts constructed under particular “laboratory conditions.” The sensors of a former-mine restoration platform are devices that extend precisely these laboratory conditions into the field. eDNA analysis defines “present species” through the PCR amplification processes of the laboratory, while drone imagery determines “visible vegetation” through the frame of remote sensing. In this process, what is not measured—that is, life processes outside the sensors’ resolution or frequency range—is ontologically excluded.

### 1.2. Restoration as Selection: Which Nature Is to Be Restored?

When a restoration platform determines “priority restoration zones” and the “timing of intervention,” it already presupposes a particular view of nature. Establishing the ecosystem that existed before degradation as the “original state” is an act of making the ecological condition of a particular historical moment normative. Yet the ecosystem of a former mining area has already undergone decades of mining activity, soil contamination, and hydrological change. The notion of “the period before degradation” is therefore closer to an abstract construction.

More importantly, ontological selection begins at the moment the restoration platform determines which species and traces will be recognized as “nature to be restored.” For example, a particular tolerant plant species inhabiting a former mining area may occupy a unique ecological niche adapted to contaminated soil, but it may be targeted for removal according to the standard of “pre-degradation” conditions. Conversely, microbial communities that are not detected through eDNA analysis may be completely ignored in the restoration plan. This resembles what Deleuze and Guattari call “territorialization”: the process by which certain modes of existence are made normative while others are excluded.

## 2. Cosmopolitics and the Virtual: The Politics of Life That Escapes Capture

### 2.1. Stengers’s Cosmopolitics: The Construction of Scientific Facts and the Possibility of Objection

Stengers’s cosmopolitics emphasizes that scientific knowledge is a political process that constructs a particular “cosmos.” She argues that science does not discover neutral facts but produces “facts” through specific ontological assumptions and practices. In the case of the former-mine restoration platform, eDNA, drones, and soil sensors each enact a particular “politics of evidence.”

One of Stengers’s key concepts is the procedure of “objection.” She holds that scientific claims must be tested and revised through the objections of diverse actors. In ecological restoration, such objections may be raised not only by human actors—residents, environmental activists, and administrative officials—but also by nonhuman actors, including microorganisms, seasonal rhythms, and the reproduction of unpredictable species. The question is whether the restoration platform possesses procedures capable of accommodating these objections.

### 2.2. Deleuze’s Virtual: Ecological Possibilities Not Yet Actualized

Deleuze’s concept of the virtual refers to a field of possibilities that, although not actualized, nevertheless exists. The virtual is not an abstract possibility opposed to the actual; it is an ontological dimension that conditions the process of actualization itself. The virtuality of an ecosystem includes future states that are immanent within its current species composition, population densities, and material cycles but have not yet been actualized.

In former-mine restoration, the virtual operates at several levels. First, microbial communities in the soil may undergo changes that cannot be captured by current measurement technologies. Changes in the metabolic activity of particular microbial species, the reconfiguration of mycelial networks, and the activation of dormant spores may not be immediately reflected in the physicochemical indicators measured by sensors, yet they possess the potential to fundamentally alter the direction of ecosystem recovery. Second, residents’ seasonal memories contain temporal rhythms that the restoration platform’s data cannot capture. The appearance of mushrooms during a particular season, the migration routes of birds, and patterns of changes in stream water levels provide ecological information that exceeds the temporal resolution of scientific measurement. Third, the reproductive rhythms of species excluded by the model reveal the unpredictability of restoration planning. For example, if the reproductive cycle of a particular insect fluctuates due to climate change, a restoration schedule that fails to account for this change may fail.

### 2.3. Sensor Blind Spots and the Politics of the Virtual

The domains that sensors fail to capture are not merely technical limitations but ontological blind spots. eDNA analysis cannot distinguish between genetic traces left by dead organisms and living populations, while drone imagery cannot see understory vegetation beneath the canopy or underground mycelial networks. Soil sensors measure physicochemical indicators at particular points, but fail to capture the fine-grained heterogeneity of soil or biological interactions.

These blind spots are sites where the politics of the virtual operates. Changes in microorganisms that sensors cannot measure may transform the ecosystem in directions not anticipated by the restoration plan. For example, a particular microorganism introduced to neutralize acid mine drainage may produce unexpected metabolic by-products. Alternatively, fungi that had remained dormant may become active in response to changes in soil pH and alter patterns of vegetation. Such changes may not be captured by the restoration platform’s data feedback loop, but they fundamentally reconstitute the ecological reality of the field.

## 3. Administrative Indicators and the Unpredictability of Life: Procedures of Conflict and Negotiation

### 3.1. The Ontological Violence of Success Indicators

Success indicators established by administrative agencies reduce the goals of restoration to particular measurable variables. Specific vegetation coverage rates, water-quality indicators, and population sizes of target species, for example, become criteria for success. Although such indicators are necessary for administrative efficiency and accountability, they simultaneously enact ontological violence. In other words, only what can be measured is recognized as part of “restored nature,” while ecological processes that cannot be measured are ignored or excluded.

From Stengers’s perspective, these success indicators are components of a “modern cosmos.” They presuppose a particular ontology—measurability, reducibility, and predictability—and invalidate ecological realities that do not conform to it. For example, when the natural succession of a former mining area proceeds more slowly than the timetable established by administrative indicators, it may be regarded as a “failure.” Ecologically, however, slow succession may lead to a more stable ecosystem.

### 3.2. Unpredictable Recovery and the Conditions of Objection

When the unpredictable recovery of living beings in the field conflicts with administrative indicators, it creates conditions under which the restoration plan must be suspended or revised. These conditions consist of the following elements.

First is temporal mismatch. Ecosystem recovery follows rhythms and cycles rather than linear time, and these may conflict with administrative schedules. For example, the germination of a particular plant species may occur later than expected, or the reorganization of a soil microbial community may take several years. Such temporal mismatches may require the judgment of “success” to be postponed or redefined.

Second is spatial heterogeneity. A former mining area is not a homogeneous space but consists of diverse microenvironments shaped by varying degrees of contamination, topography, and hydrological conditions. The sensors of a restoration platform average this heterogeneity at a particular spatial resolution, whereas living beings in the field respond within concrete microenvironments rather than within the averaged space. The unexpected emergence or disappearance of a species at a particular site may require the spatial priorities of the restoration plan to be rearranged.

Third is relational unpredictability. Members of an ecosystem generate unpredictable patterns through their interactions. For example, a particular plant species introduced for restoration may attract an unexpected insect, which in turn may affect the reproduction of another species. Such cascading effects are difficult for a model to predict. This relational emergence demands revisions to the restoration plan.

### 3.3. Ecological Facts as a Procedure of Negotiation

When administrative indicators conflict with living beings in the field, ecological facts can be understood not in terms of data accuracy or the restoration of an original state, but as a procedure through which heterogeneous actors renegotiate the range of restoration possibilities through intervention, objection, and waiting. This is a concrete example of the “cosmopolitical procedure” proposed by Stengers.

The first stage of this procedure is the acceptance of objections. When residents object to a particular aspect of a restoration plan on the basis of their seasonal memories, this should be treated not merely as a complaint but as alternative evidence concerning ecological facts. For example, a resident’s observation that “there used to be many fireflies in this valley, but they disappeared after the restoration” may indicate an ecological change that eDNA and drones fail to capture.

The second stage is the practice of waiting. Administrative efficiency demands rapid decisions and implementation, but ecosystem recovery sometimes requires waiting. The natural recolonization of a species, the reorganization of soil microbial communities, and the stabilization of ecological interactions must follow the pace of life processes rather than the speed of human intervention. Waiting is not simply passivity; it is an active practice that provides the temporal conditions under which potentiality can be actualized.

The third stage is the renegotiation of the range of restoration possibilities. Initial restoration goals—such as a particular vegetation coverage rate or population size of a target species—may be redefined in light of the unpredictable recovery occurring in the field. For example, instead of restoring the vegetation that existed before degradation, it may be necessary to recognize a new ecological assemblage adapted to current environmental conditions as “restored nature.” This means redefining restoration not as the recovery of a fixed original state, but as an open-ended process.

## 4. Simondon’s Concretization and Generative AI Feedback Loops: The Evolution and Regression of Technical Objects

### 4.1. Simondon’s Concept of Concretization and the Restoration Platform

Gilbert Simondon’s concept of concretization explains how a technical object gradually develops from an initially abstract design through interaction with its environment. An initial technical object has an abstract structure in which multiple functions are separated. Through use and environmental adaptation, however, these functions become integrated, and the object itself becomes concretized as part of its environment.

A former-mine restoration platform possesses the potential to follow Simondon’s process of concretization. At first, eDNA analysis, drone imagery, soil sensors, and resident observations operate as separate data-collection channels. Over time, however, they may adapt to the ecological conditions of the site and become concretized as an integrated system. For example, residents’ seasonal observations may refine the interpretation of eDNA data, drone imagery may supplement the spatial resolution of soil sensors, and all these forms of data may converge in an integrated model for restoration decisions.

Simondon also points out, however, that concretization does not always succeed. A technical object may regress because of incompatibility with its environment, internal contradictions, or external constraints. In the case of the restoration platform, when administrative requirements—such as reporting results within a fixed period—conflict with the temporal rhythms of ecological processes, the platform may fail to become concretized and remain at an abstract level.

### 4.2. Synthetic-Data Feedback Loops in Generative AI and Model Collapse

Synthetic-data feedback loops in generative AI reveal a structurally similar problem in the operation of restoration platforms. When generative AI repeatedly trains on synthetic data that it has generated itself, its initial diversity gradually disappears, and “model collapse” occurs as the model becomes fixed on particular patterns. This is a process in which the self-referential closure of data suppresses emergence.

A similar regressive closed loop may arise in a former-mine restoration platform. If the platform makes restoration decisions by referring only to its own data—sensor measurements, eDNA analysis results, and drone imagery—and then collects the outcomes of those decisions as new data, it may amplify initial data biases. For example, if eDNA analysis is designed to detect only particular species, a model trained on this data may overestimate the presence of those species and reinforce the underestimation of others. If residents’ observations and unpredictable changes in the field are not incorporated into this feedback loop, the platform risks gradually regressing into a self-referential system increasingly detached from reality.

### 4.3. Comparing the Evolution of Technical Objects with Regressive Closed Loops

Simondon’s concretization and generative AI’s model collapse reveal two contrasting evolutionary paths for technical objects. Concretization is a process through which a technical object develops richer relations through interaction with its environment, generates new functions, and becomes integrated as part of that environment. A regressive closed loop, by contrast, is a process through which a technical object becomes fixed in its own logic, severs its relationship with the environment, and reinforces only its internal consistency.

A former-mine restoration platform stands at a crossroads between these two paths. To follow the path of concretization, the platform must be capable of incorporating residents’ seasonal memories, unpredictable changes in microorganisms, and the reproductive rhythms of species excluded by the model into its data feedback loop. This does not simply mean collecting more data. It means reconsidering the ontological premises of data, acknowledging domains that cannot be measured, and accepting uncertainty and waiting as parts of the restoration procedure.

If the platform follows the path of the regressive closed loop, it will become increasingly detached from ecological reality in the field by fixating on administrative efficiency and measurable indicators. In that case, restoration will mean restoring “nature in the data,” rather than restoring “nature in the field.”
