# Generative AI Restoration and the Mediality of Memory: A Theoretical Test Using the National Library of Korea Case

## 1. The Frame of the Problem: Preservation or Generation?

The scenario in which the National Library of Korea uses generative AI to restore missing and damaged sections of modern and contemporary literature and incorporates them into a digital archive simultaneously challenges two traditional premises of archival science. First, the premise that 'preservation' means maintaining the physical state of an original, and 'restoration' means reversibly repairing damage inflicted upon that original. Second, the premise that a record is a **trace** of an event that *existed* in the past.

The moment generative AI intervenes, 'restoration' is no longer reversible recovery. The task of inserting the most probabilistically plausible token sequences into blank sentences and illegible sections by AI is, strictly speaking, **not restoration but generation.** This generated part is not a trace of the original text, but a simulacrum produced by the statistical patterns of the training data. The problem lies in the fact that when this simulacrum is incorporated into a digital archive with the institutional declaration that it has been 'restored', the user loses the means to distinguish between the original and the generated product.

## 3. Independent Theoretical Analysis of the Core of the Question

Acknowledging the limitations of the Khoj references, we now move to the theoretical issues inherent in the question itself.

### 3.1. The Mediality of Memory: Deficiency of Traces vs. Plausible Continuity

The question contrasts two positions:

- **Position A (Deficiency of traces)**: The role of the medium is to reveal that the traces of the past *no longer exist*. The blank spaces on damaged pages, the illegible sections, are themselves material evidence of the passage of time and forgetting.
- **Position B (Plausible continuity)**: The role of the medium is to restore broken memory, even if only plausibly, to recover narrative continuity. Damaged text should not be left as 'unreadable'; rather, it should be supplemented with the most probable content to maintain the flow of meaning.

This opposition reflects a classic tension in archival studies between **originality and accessibility**. However, generative AI pushes this tension into a qualitatively different dimension.

Traditional preservation and restoration (physical restoration, digital imaging) involved intervening in the materiality of the original, but aimed to visualize information *already present* in the original (e.g., letters visible only under UV light, traces readable with magnification). In contrast, generative AI restoration *creates* information **that does not exist** in the original. This is not preservation but creation, shifting the ontological status of the record from 'discovered' to 'produced'.

### 3.2. The Aesthetic Device That Organizes the Boundary Between Forgetting and Restoration

The premise of the question—that the mediality of memory should be seen not as storage technology but as an aesthetic device organizing the boundary between forgetting and restoration—is supported in memory studies by scholars like Aleida Assmann and Anne Rigney, as well as by Bernard Stiegler's concept of 'tertiary retention'. That is, media are not simply containers for storing memories, but **systems that determine what to remember and what to forget**.

The National Library of Korea case tests this viewpoint as follows:

**Test 1: Erasure of the Visibility of Forgetting**

If generative AI restores damaged sections to produce a seamless text, the traces of forgetting—the material damage time has inflicted upon the text—**disappear from the realm of visibility**. The user no longer perceives the fact that 'this part was not in the original'. As an aesthetic device, the medium is transformed into a device that hides forgetting.

This is paradoxical. Existing digital archives functioned as devices that **made forgetting visible** by displaying images of damaged pages as they are. Seeing illegible sections, the user gains the recognition: 'There was something here, but it can no longer be read.' This recognition triggers a media-epistemological reflection on the incomplete representability of the past. AI restoration blocks this opportunity for reflection.

**Test 2: The Question of Authenticity of Generated Continuity**

The text generated by AI is unrelated to the intention of the original author. For example, when AI fills damaged sections of a 1920s colonial-era newspaper with 'most probable' words, the product is merely a statistical result overlaying the contemporary censorship system, limitations of printing technology, and historical usage frequency of specific vocabulary. This raises the fundamental issue of **the gap between historical truth and statistical probability**.

An even more complex problem is the **inductive bias** of AI restoration. Language models tend to reproduce the dominant narratives of their training data—such as colonial discourse, gender norms, and political legitimacy narratives. If the damaged historical text contained voices different from these mainstream narratives, AI restoration risks erasing that difference and incorporating it into the continuity of dominant discourse. This is not merely a technical error, but a form of **destructive preservation**—the paradox where the act of preservation actually erases the heterogeneity of the record.

**Test 3: Institutional Declaration of Restoration and Authority**

The moment the National Library of Korea, a state institution, incorporates AI-restored text into its digital archive, the product acquires institutional authority. The user cannot distinguish whether the restored sections are part of the original or AI-generated. Even if identification markers exist, the approval of a state institution grants the product the status of 'factuality'.

This sheds new light on what Jacques Derrida problematized in *Speech and Phenomena* and *Archive Fever*: the **power effect of the record**. A record does not simply preserve the past; it determines what will be recognized *as having been the past* in the future. Under AI restoration technology, this determination operates even more subtly and authoritatively—because it overwrites the traces of the original while being justified in the name of 'restoration'.

## 4. Conclusion: Results of the Test on the Existing Viewpoint

The viewpoint presented in the question—that the mediality of memory should be seen not as storage technology but as an aesthetic device organizing the boundary between forgetting and restoration—is tested through the National Library of Korea case as follows:

**Reinforced Aspect**: This case vividly demonstrates that media are not mere repositories but **aesthetic devices that determine the boundaries of visibility**. By deciding 'what is to be seen' and removing forgetting from the realm of visibility, AI restoration reveals the constitutive role of media far more actively than traditional preservation technologies.

**Challenged Aspect**: However, this case also shows that the boundary between forgetting and restoration is no longer solely a matter of human aesthetic judgment. This boundary is determined by three axes: **probabilistic algorithms, the statistical distribution of training data, and institutional approval processes.** In other words, the mode of operation of the aesthetic device is shifting towards technical infrastructure and institutional authority. This demands that the concept of 'aesthetic device' presupposed by the existing viewpoint be expanded to include technical and institutional dimensions.

**Final Judgment**: Does the role of the medium lie in revealing the deficiency of traces, or in generating plausible content to restore continuity? The answer is not one side or the other, but depends on an **institutional design that consciously maintains this tension**. When text restored by generative AI is incorporated into a digital archive, the following three conditions must be met:

1. **Transparency**: AI-generated parts must be visually distinguishable, and the generation process (model used, probability information, scope of generation) must be recorded as metadata.
2. **Preserved Deficiency**: Images of the damaged state of the original must be preserved separately from the AI-generated text, so that the material traces of forgetting are not erased.
3. **Open Critique**: The AI restoration results should not be presented as a finalized 'restored version', but as temporary and hypothetical, open to critical scholarly review.

Without these conditions, generative AI restoration risks degenerating into a technology that **replaces memory rather than preserving it**. And precisely in recognizing that risk, we confirm that media have always already been constitutive, and that technological progress only makes the depth of this constitution more visible.
