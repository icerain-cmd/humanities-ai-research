# Media Aesthetics in the Age of Technological Generation: WE (Whole-Emergent) and the Asymmetry of Responsibility Distribution

## Introduction: The Relational Field and the Problem of Responsibility

Media aesthetics in the age of technological generation takes as its core analytical object the relational field co-produced by humans and technological actors. The key concept emerging from this relational field is WE (Whole-Emergent), namely the whole-emergent subject. WE refers not to a mere collective existence of human and non-human actors, but to a techno-generative form in which new agency and relational subjectivity—irreducible to any single side—emerge through a continuous and recursive process of mutual transformation.

However, this framing of the relational field immediately raises the problem of responsibility distribution. If it is a relational field co-produced by humans and technological actors, and if the outcomes arising from that field are complex products that cannot be attributed to a single actor, then should responsibility also be distributed equally among all actors participating in that relational field? This paper takes a negative stance on this question and seeks to derive the reasons from the inequality of design power and operating conditions.

The existence of relational entanglement does not mean that responsibility is equally distributed. This is similar to how, even if all species in an ecosystem are interdependent, the responsibility for protecting endangered species is not equally imposed on humans and sea otters. The distribution of responsibility must go beyond the factual dimension of relational entanglement and consider the normative dimension of power, design, and condition-setting.

## 1. The Conditions for WE Formation and Asymmetric Interdependence

For WE (Whole-Emergent) to form, at least the condition of multiple agency must be satisfied. This refers to a situation in which two or more human and non-human actors contribute to the production of an outcome. However, the crucial point is that the ways these actors contribute are not symmetrical.

According to the claims of techno-generative media aesthetics, WE emerges in the process where asymmetrically interdependent actors continuously and recursively transform each other. Here, the expression 'asymmetric interdependence' is decisive. Interdependence itself is merely a description at the factual level, but asymmetry implies that this interdependence does not occur under equal conditions.

For example, consider the relationship between generative AI and human users. Users depend on AI outputs, and AI depends on user inputs and feedback. This is an interdependent relationship. However, this interdependence is asymmetric. The basic operating principles, training data, and objective functions of AI are determined not by users but by designers and operators. Users can only interact within the given interface and parameters, and they have no authority to change the fundamental conditions of the AI.

This asymmetry becomes even clearer through Gilbert Simondon's concept of concretization (concrétisation). Simondon emphasizes that a technical object is not simply the implementation of an abstract principle, but a process that gradually concretizes itself in relation to internal and external conditions. A technical object starts from an abstract principle but gradually concretizes its structure through interaction with environmental conditions. In this process, the technical object not only adapts to its given environment but also reconstructs the environment to fit its own operating conditions.

However, if Simondon's concept of concretization emphasizes the evolutionary aspect of technical objects, we can contrast it with the possibility of a degenerative closed loop. In the case of generative AI, the feedback loop through synthetic data is a representative example. Model collapse, which occurs when AI uses data it generated itself as training data again, demonstrates the risk of a technical object falling into a degenerative closed loop. This is not merely a technical defect but suggests that the relational field of WE can transform self-destructively under certain conditions.

## 2. The Inequality of Design Power

The first axis for arguing the asymmetry of responsibility distribution is the inequality of design power. Design power refers to the authority and ability to determine the basic structure, operating principles, purposes, and constraints of a technical system.

### 2.1. Concentration of Power in the Design Phase

The design of technical systems takes place within specific social, economic, and political contexts. Designers inject their intentions, values, and goals into the system. This injection can be explicit or implicit. For example, a designer of a recommendation algorithm may configure the system to prioritize sensational content in order to maximize user engagement. Users interact with this algorithm, but the basic conditions of that interaction were determined not by users but by designers.
