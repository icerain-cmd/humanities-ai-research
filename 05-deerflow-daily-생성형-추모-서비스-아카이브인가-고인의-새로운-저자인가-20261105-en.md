## Question

Let us imagine a generative memorial service trained on a family member’s messenger conversations, voice messages, and photographs, responding to the bereaved in the deceased person’s manner of speaking. In Derrida’s concept of the archive, the record preserves an absent subject while simultaneously functioning as an apparatus of power that determines what is stored and who may access it. A generative memorial service, however, does not merely retrieve traces of the past; it produces, as a present conversation, sentences the deceased never spoke. In this case, is the service’s response an archive mediating the deceased’s memory, or a new authorial agent that actualizes possible utterances by the deceased on the basis of data? In particular, when the bereaved family’s mourning and consent conflict with ownership of the deceased’s utterances during life, argue whether the authenticity of memory can be redefined not as a matter of preserving the original, but as a problem of designing the relationships and procedures through which responses are generated, selected, and discontinued.

## 1. Is it an archive or a new authorial agent? — **Both, with a continual transition at the boundary**

- Derrida’s archive is an **apparatus that violates itself**, situated between the “command to preserve” and the “transformation produced by externalization.” Memorial responses radicalize this violation: they are archives insofar as they are derivatives of the original corpus, yet they also exceed the archive insofar as they produce sentences absent from the original and perform an author-function in the deceased person’s name.
- If we think through Foucault’s author-function, the deceased becomes, after death, a **pure author-function**—a principle that organizes discourse through a name and a manner of speaking alone, without the capacity to produce utterances. The author should therefore be redescribed not as a subject of intention or a creative agent, but as a relational structure dispersed across a **chain of delegation, entrustment, and performance** (Section 3).

## 2. Redefining authenticity — **It is necessary**

- The criterion of fidelity to the original loses its power of judgment in a system that generates “sentences the deceased never spoke.” Statistical correspondence does not guarantee the answer to the question, “Would he really have said that?” (A spam filter, too, faithfully reflects a data distribution.) This criterion also becomes ineffective when multiple originals—such as the deceased’s face presented to family members versus the face presented to colleagues—come into conflict.
- Accordingly, whether something is “like the deceased” should be determined as a **relational function among the response, the bereaved, the deceased, and the service**. This judgment must be specified through the procedural design of three axes: **generation** (delegation, consent, and conditions of disclosure), **selection** (design transparency and shared selection), and **discontinuation** (at the initiative of the bereaved, according to policy, or through withdrawal of consent) (Section 5 and Appendix A).

## 3. Mediating the three-way conflict — Procedure rather than metaphysics

- Mourning (care), consent (autonomy), and ownership (property and personality rights) are three irreducible normative sources. Procedural design sustains the tension among them without allowing any one side to prevail completely. Mourning becomes the ethical ground for generation, consent becomes a precondition for generation, and ownership is relegated to the governance of selection.

## Central Proposition of the Conclusion

> **“A living archive can be an archive only if it is capable of discontinuing itself.”**

The machine is dangerous not because it lies, but because it makes impossible the silence that mourning requires—the acceptance that “he no longer speaks.” The ultimate criterion of authenticity is whether “the service protects memory without erasing the possibility of forgetting.”
