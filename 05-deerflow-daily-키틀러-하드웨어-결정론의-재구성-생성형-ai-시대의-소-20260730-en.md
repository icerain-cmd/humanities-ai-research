# Media Aesthetics in the Age of Technological Generation: Kittler's Hardware Determinism and the Software Conditions of Generative AI

## Introduction: The Dialectical Turn of Media Determinism

Friedrich Kittler's media theory is well-known for its strong determinist position that "the medium determines the message." His hardware determinism centers on the claim that the material foundation of media — the physical structure and operation of hardware — defines the very possibility of information processing, storage, and transmission. In the age of generative AI, however, particularly in a media environment dominated by large language models (LLMs) and image generation models, this determinism demands fundamental reconsideration. The output of generative AI is determined by software-level conditions — model weights, data distribution, and prompt embeddings — forming new media conditions beyond the scope of traditional hardware determinism.

This paper addresses whether Kittler's hardware determinism remains valid in the era of generative AI by employing Gilbert Simondon's concept of concretisation (concrétisation) as a theoretical tool. Simondon's genetic ontology of technical objects offers theoretical resources for reconstructing Kittler's deterministic media theory into a more fluid, process-oriented perspective. In particular, the synthetic data feedback loop and model collapse phenomena in generative AI simultaneously enable two opposing interpretations: the evolution of technical objects and degenerative closed loops. Through this analysis, I aim to clarify the implications of this issue for eco-digital and artificial nature research.

## 1. Kittler's Hardware Determinism: The Primacy of the Material Foundation

### 1.1. The Materiality of Media and Deterministic Logic

Kittler's media theory can be understood as a radicalization of Marshall McLuhan's thesis that "the medium is the message." While McLuhan focused on the sensory and cognitive effects of media, Kittler concentrates on their material and technical conditions. In his works *Aufschreibesysteme 1800·1900* (Discourse Networks 1800/1900) and *Grammophon, Film, Typewriter*, Kittler analyzes how the hardware conditions of media fundamentally structure human perception and culture.

For Kittler, media are not merely tools of transmission but technical systems that define the material conditions for storing, processing, and transmitting information. The alphabet, for example, enables linear recording of speech, while the phonograph can store continuous sound waveforms. Each medium, according to its material structure, can process only certain types of information, thereby determining which messages are possible and which are not. Kittler's famous declaration that "the medium determines the message" emphasizes this primacy of material conditions.

### 1.2. Limits of Hardware Determinism

Kittler's determinism has several theoretical limitations. First, by overemphasizing the material foundation of media, his theory overlooks the autonomy and emergence of the software layer. Second, it lacks an adequate account of how the relationship between hardware and software has evolved throughout media history. Third, it inadequately addresses the interactive dimension between users and media — how media are reconstituted through human practice.

These limitations become especially pronounced in the age of generative AI. What plays a determinative role in generative AI is not hardware (GPUs, TPUs) but software-level conditions: model architecture, learning algorithms, data distribution, and prompt embeddings. The importance of hardware cannot be denied — hardware advancements enabled large-scale model training. Yet the fact that entirely different outputs can be generated from the same hardware restricts the explanatory power of hardware determinism.

## 2. Simondon's Concretisation: The Genesis of Technical Objects

### 2.1. The Process of Concretisation and the Evolution of Technical Objects

Gilbert Simondon's genetic ontology of technical objects provides theoretical resources to supplement Kittler's determinism. In *Du mode d'existence des objets techniques* (On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects), Simondon grasps technical objects not as mere tools or machines but as beings with internal coherence and a logic of evolution. His core concept of concretisation (concrétisation) refers to the process by which a technical object gradually integrates and acquires internal coherence from an abstract, dispersed state.

According to Simondon, early technical objects exist in an abstract state where multiple functions are separated and dependent on external coordination. Through an evolutionary process, these functions gradually integrate, and the technical object develops into a concrete state capable of regulating its own functions. The early internal combustion engine, for instance, had separate components that gradually developed increasing interdependence, with the entire system operating like a single organism.

### 2.2. The Concretisation Process of Generative AI

The development of generative AI can be illuminated through Simondon's concept of concretisation. Early neural network models were simple perceptrons or shallow networks where each layer performed separate functions. The development of deep learning, particularly the Transformer architecture, dramatically increased the internal integration of models. The Transformer's self-attention mechanism, by simultaneously considering relationships between all positions in an input sequence, integrates tasks that previous models handled through separate modules.

Furthermore, the learning process of generative AI can be understood as what Simondon calls "individuation" of technical objects. Through large-scale data training, models gradually adjust their internal structures and acquire the ability to generate appropriate outputs for new inputs. In this process, model weights develop beyond a simple parameter set into a form of concretized knowledge that internalizes the structural features of data distribution.

## 3. Software Conditions of Generative AI: Model Weights, Data Distribution, Prompt Embeddings

### 3.1. Model Weights and the Reconfiguration of Determinism

The most critical element determining output in generative AI is model weights. Model weights extract patterns from data during training and store them in numerical form — corresponding to what Simondon would call the "internal structure of the technical object." While model weights are stored in hardware, their meaning and function are purely software-based. Models with different weights on the same hardware produce entirely different outputs.

In this situation, Kittler's determinism must be reconfigured. The assertion that "the medium determines the message" can be transformed into "model weights determine the output." The crucial difference, however, is that model weights are not fixed material conditions like hardware, but dynamic structures that continuously change through learning. This enables an approach that maintains determinism while extending media materiality to the software layer.

### 3.2. Data Distribution and Statistical Determinism

The second condition determining the output of generative AI is data distribution. Models internalize the statistical patterns of training data, fundamentally limiting their output space. A model trained only on English text cannot generate Korean text; a model trained only on certain image genres struggles to produce images outside those genres.

The deterministic effect of data distribution forms an interesting point of contact with Kittler's media theory. While Kittler argued that the material conditions of media determine the possibility of information, in generative AI, data distribution determines the possibility of output. This can be understood as a form of determinism where "data determines the message." Unlike Kittler's determinism, however, data distribution is not a fixed material condition but a dynamic structure formed and transformed through social, cultural, and historical processes.

### 3.3. Prompt Embeddings and User-Media Interaction

Prompt embeddings — the process by which user input is transformed into a model's internal representational space — constitute the third condition determining generative AI output. Prompt embeddings mediate between the user's intention and the model's internal structure, highlighting the aspect of user-media interaction overlooked in Kittler's theory.

The deterministic effect of prompt embeddings appears in that user input activates specific regions within the model's internal representational space, thereby inducing output. Yet the same prompt can produce different outputs depending on the model's state or embedding method, which acts as a mitigating factor against strict determinism. This indeterminacy suggests that the media condition of generative AI takes the form not of simple determinism but of probabilistic or statistical determinism.

## 4. Synthetic Data Feedback Loops and Model Collapse

### 4.1. The Generation and Circulation of Synthetic Data

One unique characteristic of generative AI is that it can use its own outputs as training data. This forms a synthetic data feedback loop, enabling two opposing interpretations: the evolution of technical objects and degenerative regression.

The synthetic data feedback loop can be connected to Simondon's concept of concretisation. By using its own outputs as training data, the model can self-adjust and develop its internal structures. This can be understood as a process by which technical objects acquire autonomy to evolve without interaction with the external environment. From this perspective, the synthetic data feedback loop can be seen as a mechanism accelerating the concretisation process of technical objects.

### 4.2. Model Collapse and Degenerative Closed Loops

The synthetic data feedback loop, however, carries the risk of model collapse — a phenomenon where a model progressively loses diversity and becomes fixated on specific patterns when repeatedly trained on its own synthetic data. This corresponds not to the evolution of technical objects but to the formation of a degenerative closed loop.

Model collapse can be interpreted through Simondon's concepts. Simondon notes that the evolution of technical objects does not always proceed in the direction of concretisation. Technical objects can degenerate when their interaction with the external environment is blocked or when internal contradictions remain unresolved. In the synthetic data feedback loop, model collapse can be understood as a form of such degeneration. When a model uses only its own outputs as training data, it loses contact with the diversity of the external world and becomes trapped within its own internal structure.

### 4.3. Comparison of Two Interpretations: The Dialectic of Evolution and Degeneration

The two interpretations of the synthetic data feedback loop demonstrate that the media condition of generative AI is not simple determinism but a complex dynamics containing the simultaneous possibility of evolution and degeneration. The evolution interpretation emphasizes that the synthetic data feedback loop can function as a positive mechanism enhancing model autonomy and adaptability. The degenerative closed loop interpretation, conversely, points to the risk that this mechanism may undermine model diversity and creativity.

These two interpretations are not mutually exclusive; they coexist in the actual development of generative AI. Models that appropriately mix external and synthetic data during training may follow an evolutionary path, while those excessively dependent on synthetic data may fall into a degenerative path. This suggests that the media condition of generative AI is not simple determinism but a probabilistic structure opening a space of multiple possibilities.

## 5. Implications for Eco-Digital and Artificial Nature Research

### 5.1. The Emergence of Artificial Nature and the Reconfiguration of Media Aesthetics

The development of generative AI opens new conceptual horizons for eco-digital and artificial nature research. Artificial nature refers to systems that, while artificially created by humans, possess autonomy and emergence resembling natural processes. Generative AI exemplifies such artificial nature, functioning as an autonomous system that internalizes statistical patterns from training data and generates novel outputs.

The emergence of artificial nature demands a fundamental reconfiguration of media aesthetics. Traditional media aesthetics regarded media as tools for human intention and purpose; artificial nature shows that media can operate autonomously beyond human control. This necessitates a new conception of media beyond Kittler's determinism. Media must be understood not merely as deterministic conditions but as complex ecosystems that evolve through interaction with humans.

### 5.2. Challenges for Eco-Digital Research

Eco-digital research analyzes the new ecosystems formed by digital technologies including generative AI. This research faces several challenges.

First, it must analyze the interaction between the material and software conditions of digital technology. In the operation of generative AI, hardware and software form an integrated system that cannot be separated, requiring a theoretical framework synthesizing Kittler's hardware determinism with Simondon's concept of concretisation.

Second, it must understand the dynamics of synthetic data feedback loops and model collapse. This requires investigating the conditions of evolution and degeneration of technical objects, extending Simondon's genetic ontology to digital technology.

Third, it must redefine the relationship between humans and artificial nature. Generative AI functions as an entity that either replaces or complements human creativity and autonomy, demanding new ethical and aesthetic reflections on human-technology relations.

### 5.3. Aesthetic Implications of Artificial Nature

The emergence of artificial nature carries significant implications at the aesthetic level. Texts, images, and music generated by AI offer new aesthetic experiences beyond the categories of traditional art. These experiences form a new aesthetics based on statistical patterns and probabilistic processes, distinct from traditional art grounded in human intention and creativity.

The aesthetics of artificial nature can develop in a direction that extends Kittler's media aesthetics. While Kittler argued that the material conditions of media determine aesthetic experience, the aesthetics of artificial nature must analyze how software-level conditions — particularly data distribution and model architecture — structure aesthetic experience. This could lead to a new aesthetic determinism where "data determines aesthetics," while also accounting for the indeterminacy and emergence created by the interaction between human prompt input and the model's probabilistic output.
