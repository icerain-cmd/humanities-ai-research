## Question

Suppose a local broadcaster operates an archival service that combines audio transcripts of old radio interviews with listeners’ present-day questions to generate follow-up utterances that were not part of the original broadcasts. This service does not merely preserve voices from the past; it rearranges utterances, silences, and traces of editing from different periods within present-day conversations. By examining Walter Benjamin’s philosophy of history alongside Michel Foucault’s concept of the archive, argue whether these generated follow-up utterances constitute a practice of memory that actualizes forgotten voices, or instead function as an apparatus of power through which the broadcaster newly edits the continuity and perspective of the past to be remembered. In particular, analyze the process through which listeners discover the vocal unnaturalness and editorial gaps in a generated response, consult the original materials, and delete or place on hold portions of the response. Discuss whether the authenticity of memory can be redefined not as a question of whether the original has been restored, but as a problem of designing a listening procedure that makes replay, doubt, interruption, and recontextualization possible.

## Analysis Results

This question requires engagement with a broad theoretical spectrum. I will begin with the key texts of Benjamin and Foucault, then analyze the specific archival-service scenario posed by the question, and finally extend the discussion to the problem of designing a listening procedure.

## 1. Benjamin’s Philosophy of History: The Politics of Memory That Captures Moments of Rupture

In *On the Concept of History* (*Über den Begriff der Geschichte*), Benjamin directly rejects historicism’s view of history as a continuous process of progress. His “historical materialist” does not regard the past as “something fixed once and for all and gone forever”; rather, this figure captures the moment when fragmented remnants of the past explosively become present within “now-time” (*Jetztzeit*). Benjamin’s following thesis is crucial here:

> “The past can be seized only as an image which flashes up at the moment of its recognizability.” — Thesis VI

This thesis has two decisive implications for the scenario in question. First, the old radio interview is not a **completed continuum of the past**. It exists as something that is **uttered as a fragment** only through the listener’s present question—the listener’s awareness of danger, namely, “Why does this voice sound so unnatural?” For Benjamin, the illusion that the past can be reproduced “as it really was” is historicism’s most dangerous form of self-deception. Second, the generated follow-up utterance is not merely a technical product but a **configuration of now-time**. The utterance produced when a model trained on past modes of speaking encounters a present-day question most directly embodies the moment of the historical image—the “expression that the past directs toward the present,” in Benjamin’s sense.

From a Benjaminian perspective, therefore, this service can be defended as an **actualization of forgotten voices**. The silences, traces of editing, and interrupted sentences in the radio archive are voices that have been concealed by the continuity of dominant discourse and have disappeared into the background of “progress.” If the service enables them to speak again within present-day conversations, it constitutes an attempt to rescue the “tradition of the oppressed” in opposition to the “festival of the rulers” of history.

At the same time, Benjamin offers the following warning:

> “The historical materialist grasps the constellation in which his own era has come together with a definite earlier one. Thus he establishes a conception of the present as the ‘time of the now’ which is shot through with chips of Messianic time.” — Thesis V

The decisive difference from historicism becomes visible here. Generated follow-up utterances risk being captured as **images of the past fixed by the present**. The more natural the AI-generated voice becomes, the more that voice may lose the historical singularity of the original interview and function as an **illusion of continuity without an original**. It is precisely at this point that Foucault’s concept of the archive must intervene.

## 2. Foucault’s Archive: Conditions of Emergence and the Arrangement of Power

For Foucault, the “archive” is not simply a repository for documents. In *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (*L’Archéologie du savoir*), the archive is a **“system of the formation and transformation of statements”**—the totality of historical conditions that determine what can and cannot be said in a particular period. More decisively, Foucault defines the archive not as a fixed repository but as **a system of transformation that continually redraws the boundary between what has been said and what has not been said**.

This definition enables us to dismantle the service in our scenario as follows.

**First, the radio archive is never neutral.** Through the concept of the “positivity” of the archive, Foucault argues that when an archive defines the conditions of statements in a particular period, it simultaneously determines what cannot be stated. An old radio interview already contains numerous silences and gaps produced by broadcast duration, editorial rules, studio power relations, and the social taboos of the time. When an AI learns from this archive and generates follow-up utterances, the model is not simply reproducing vocal patterns. It is **reproducing the conditions of discourse that made the original interview possible**.

**Second, the generated follow-up utterance is the operation of a new system of statements.** In Foucault’s later work, including *The History of Sexuality* and his discussions of biopolitics, the archive expands into the governmentality of disciplinary power. By designing the range of what listeners may ask and how the interview subject may respond, the broadcaster’s service **redefines the boundaries of what can be said**. This is not merely the provision of information, but a technology of power that arranges the relationship between listeners and voices from the past in a particular way.

It is at this point that a decisive intersection between Foucault and Benjamin emerges. Benjamin’s proposition that “the true image of the past flits by” can, when reread through Foucault, be understood as **a moment of power in which certain images of the past gain an opportunity to reappear while others disappear forever, as the archive redraws the boundaries of what can be stated**.

## 3. Scenario Analysis: Between a Practice of Memory and an Apparatus of Power

### 3.1 As a Practice of Memory

- **Restoring forgotten voices**: Radio archives contain utterances cut during the broadcasting process, portions lost through technical defects, and the speaker’s hesitation or emotional interruptions. If these traces of **negativity**—voices in which, to borrow Spinoza’s terms, more remains unsaid than said—are included in the training data, the follow-up utterance can do more than reproduce the archive. It can reconstruct the archive’s silences as an **alternative continuity**.

- **Generative resignification of editorial gaps**: From a Benjaminian perspective, “vocal unnaturalness and editorial gaps” are **decisive resources**. These discontinuities mark the points at which the model fails to reconstruct historical continuity smoothly. They can therefore prompt listeners to become consciously aware of the relationship between past and present. A generated product that retains some unnaturalness, rather than presenting a perfectly natural synthetic voice, preserves the **visibility of the mediation between past and present**.

### 3.2 As an Apparatus of Power

- **Re-editing broadcast continuity**: Through this service, the broadcaster reconstructs old programs as a **continuity deemed worthy of remembrance**. The process of deciding which materials among countless recordings will be archived, and which “speaking style” the model will learn, is already a political choice. In Foucault’s terms, it is a **redesign of the “system of the formation of statements.”**

- **Contemporary recontextualization of past voices**: A follow-up utterance generated in response to a listener’s present-day question may acquire a meaning entirely different from that of the original interview. If an ambiguous statement made by a local politician in the 1980s is placed within a completely different framework by a specific question from a listener in 2026, this is not simply the provision of information. It is an act of **shifting the political center of gravity of the past**.

- **Editing without an editor**: Traditional broadcasting had a visible subject—the editor—responsible for editorial decisions. In contrast, an AI-generation system delegates decisions about what to generate, how natural to make it, and in what style to deliver it to an **invisible algorithm**. This is a new digital transformation of the characteristic of disciplinary power analyzed by Foucault in *Discipline and Punish*: the paradox that the more visible the operation of power becomes, the more invisible its subject becomes.

### 3.3 The Intersection of Benjamin and Foucault: The Duality of Generated Follow-Up Utterances

Benjamin and Foucault interpret generated follow-up utterances differently, but their intersection reveals the following fissure.

| Dimension | Benjaminian interpretation | Foucauldian interpretation |

| Status of the follow-up utterance | A historical image that the past gives to the present within now-time | A new statement produced by the archive’s system of statements |

## 4. Redefining the Authenticity of Memory: Designing a Listening Procedure That Enables Replay, Doubt, Interruption, and Recontextualization

The central question is whether the “authenticity of memory” can be shifted from the question of **whether the original has been restored** to the **problem of designing a listening procedure**. I argue that this is not only possible but necessary.

### 4.1 Critique of the Illusion of Restoring the Original

In *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction*, Benjamin argues that “the aura of the original disappears through reproductive technology.” Yet the point of this argument is not **mourning the loss of the original**, but the **new political possibilities created by reproduction**. Reproduction frees the original from being an object of worship and transfers it into the political field.

By the same logic, “restoring the original voice” in a generated follow-up utterance is an impossible project from the outset. The voice of a particular radio host in the 1980s was a total event that included the studio microphone, the characteristics of the recording tape of the period, analog cutting in the editing room, the broadcast-transmission system, the listener’s radio receiver, and even the social atmosphere of that moment. The idea that AI can “restore” all these conditions is an illusion. Indeed, **the more one clings to the illusion of the original, the more the conditions under which the generated utterance is constructed—biases in the training data, limitations of the model architecture, and the broadcaster’s curatorial decisions—become invisible**.

At this point, the local reference literature’s discussion of **“the transparency of friction and the transparency of the subject”** has decisive implications. As Document 3 points out, when friction becomes transparent—that is, when the system’s errors and limitations become visible—the subject can become conscious of the attitude it is taking toward technology. The **unnaturalness** of the generated follow-up utterance is a key condition that makes this transparency of friction possible.

### 4.2 Four Design Principles for a Listening Procedure

The listener’s actions described in the question—consulting the original materials and deleting or placing portions of a response on hold—can be systematized as the following redesigned listening procedure.

#### Stage 1: Replay — Exposing the Conditions of Generation

A generated follow-up utterance should not simply be played back. **The conditions of its generation should be displayed simultaneously with playback.** For example, the listener interface should display the following information in real time:

- “This response was generated in response to your 2026 question, ‘B,’ based on the vocal patterns of Speaker A in a radio interview dated March 14, 1987.”
- “Original archival materials referenced during generation: [link]; edited sections: [timestamps].”
- “Model confidence score: 0.73 (includes segments with high uncertainty).”

This design makes visible from the moment of generation what Foucault calls the **materiality of the statement** in *The Archaeology of Knowledge*: statements exist only within particular media, institutions, and conditions.

#### Stage 2: Doubt — Infrastructure for Comparative Listening

When listeners notice vocal unnaturalness in a generated response, they need **infrastructure that allows them to switch immediately to the original material and compare the two**. This is not merely a fact-checking system. In a Benjaminian sense, it is a workbench for **constructing a dialectical image between past and present**.

More specifically:

- **Split interface**: synchronized playback of the generated voice on one side and the original archive on the other
- **Edit map**: visualization of which parts of the original—or which model-generated or model-inferred elements—correspond to each segment of the generated response
- **Silence timeline**: separate markers for silences, interruptions, and editorial cuts in the original interview

This design translates the methodology of Benjamin’s materialist philosophy of history into a principle of system design. History does not simply bring the past into the present; it constructs “the moment when past and present collide in a tense relationship like a constellation.”

#### Stage 3: Interruption — The Right to Partially Capture and Place Responses on Hold

The most decisive design principle is to grant listeners **the right to selectively reject or place portions of a generated response on hold**. Listeners should be able to carry out actions such as the following:

- Mark a particular segment of a generated utterance as “irrelevant to the current question” and place it on archival hold.
- If a particular response appears to have “smoothed over” an editorial gap in the original, attach an “editorial hold annotation” to that segment.
- Reject the generated response in its entirety and post an independent interpretive response—an omission, question, or counterargument—to the platform.

This is a digital version of the **counter-practice against the proposition that “history is written by the victors.”** In traditional broadcasting, listeners were passive recipients and had no veto over editorial decisions. Under this design, however, listeners become **active subjects capable of interrupting portions of the generated “history,” requesting reconsideration, and proposing alternative interpretations**.

#### Stage 4: Recontextualization — Structuring the Generated Response as Knowledge

Finally, the trajectories of listeners’ doubts, interruptions, and annotations must be incorporated into the **metadata of the archive itself**. In other words, the information that “someone annotated this segment of the generated response as having ‘covered over an editorial gap’” should become part of the archive and be made available to subsequent listeners.

This implements, at the level of system design, the concept of **“knowledge structuring in the age of technological generation”** from the local reference literature (Document 1). “Knowledge should be understood not as a list of individual propositions, but as a distributed order that includes similarities between concepts, criteria of distinction, the margins of incorrect answers, and contexts of use.” This system continuously reconstructs that distributed order **through the practices of listeners**.

At this stage, the generated response is no longer a fixed product but an **open-ended event that includes the trajectories of doubt, interruption, and recontextualization produced by multiple listeners**. In Foucauldian terms, the archive’s system of statements has been redesigned as a **self-critical apparatus that transforms itself through the practices of its listeners**.

## 5. Overall Argument

The generated follow-up utterance is **simultaneously** a Benjaminian practice of memory and a Foucauldian apparatus of power. This duality is not a contradiction that must be resolved, but rather **the political character of the generated utterance itself**.

The central question, however, is whether this can be shifted from a problem of the authenticity of memory to a problem of designing a listening procedure. The answer is as follows:

**This shift is possible, and it is the only productive path.** Instead of pursuing the illusory restoration of the original, we should design the generated response as **an open-ended event that enables comparison with the original materials, doubt, selective interruption, and recontextualization through annotation**. Under such a design, “authenticity” no longer resides in the naturalness of the voice. It is redefined as **the procedural condition under which listeners can actively construct the relationship between past and present**.

The criteria for this procedural authenticity are as follows:

1. **Transparency**: The conditions of generation must be made fully visible.
2. **Comparability**: Comparison with the original materials must always be possible.
3. **The right of refusal**: Listeners must be able to place portions of a generated response on hold or reject them.
4. **Collaborative archiving**: The trajectories of listeners’ doubts and interruptions must be incorporated into the archive’s metadata.
5. **Preservation of friction**: Unnaturalness and editorial gaps must not be eliminated; they must remain as traces of the generation process.

When these conditions are met, the generated follow-up utterance cannot be reduced entirely to the broadcaster’s apparatus of power-based editing. The listener functions not as a mere recipient but, in Benjamin’s terms, as **a historical subject who captures the past in the face of danger**. This subject formation becomes possible through a practice that makes the invisible operation of power visible **through friction**—that is, through the moment when the transparency of friction and the transparency of the subject form a mirror relation.

Ultimately, whether this service is **an actualization of forgotten voices or an apparatus of power-based editing** is not determined a priori by the technology itself. It is determined by how the listening procedure is designed: by the **procedural conditions** under which friction is preserved, doubt is institutionalized, and interruption is recognized as a right. Without these conditions, even the most faithful voice restoration amounts only to an intensification of the broadcaster’s power to edit history. With them, however, even the most imperfect generation can become a small victory for historical materialism—a moment of rupture that undermines the continuity of the victors.
