# The Governance of the Data Double and the (Im)possibility of Resistance: Algorithmic Governmentality and the Foucauldian Subject in the Case of John Hancock Vitality

## 1. Introduction: The Ontological Shift of Insurance

In 2018, the U.S. life insurance company John Hancock completely abolished its traditional life insurance products and began selling only a fitness-linked insurance plan called "Vitality," in which the policyholder's heart rate, step count, and sleep data are directly reflected in the insurance premium. This decision was not merely a change in business model but an event that shook the very ontological foundation of insurance. Traditional insurance has converted individual uncertainty into collective predictability by calculating premiums based on statistical risk groups. However, Vitality directly reflects individuals' real-time data in premium determination, thereby fundamentally restructuring the temporality and subjectivity of insurance.

This case provides a testing ground to concretely examine the tension between Michel Foucault's disciplinary power and Antoinette Rouvroy's algorithmic governmentality. While Foucault's disciplinary power operated by making the body and soul objects of discipline to produce obedient subjects, Rouvroy's conceptualized algorithmic governmentality is argued to govern potential behavioral possibilities derived from statistical correlations directly, without passing through the subject. How do these two modes of power intersect or diverge in the John Hancock case? And more fundamentally, under the conditions of algorithmic governmentality, in what direction can Foucault's care of the self (le souci de soi) and parrhesia (the courage to speak the truth), which he posited as the starting point of resistance, operate?

This paper will examine the practical possibility of distinguishing between Foucault's disciplinary power and Rouvroy's algorithmic governmentality, focusing on the John Hancock Vitality case, and raise the need to redefine the Foucauldian concept of the subject and resistance under data-driven governance. ## 2. Foucault's Disciplinary Power and Biopower: A Genealogy of Insurance

### 2.1 The Operation of Disciplinary Power

In *Discipline and Punish* (1975), Foucault analyzed how disciplinary power takes individual bodies as objects of training to produce obedient and productive subjects. Disciplinary power operates through three techniques—hierarchical observation, normalizing judgment, and the examination (examen). In particular, the examination documents and records individuals, thereby constituting them as comparable and measurable objects. Examinations in the military, school, hospital, and prison turn individuals into "cases." Through this process, individuals come to discipline themselves through an internalized awareness of being observed and evaluated.

For Foucault, the core of disciplinary power lies in "normalization." Norms are derived from statistical averages but simultaneously function as rules that compel individuals to conform to that norm. Disciplinary power directly trains the body and actions of individuals, producing a "docile body" as a result of that training. Here, the target of power is the concrete body and its temporal and spatial arrangement.

### 2.2 Biopower and the Governance of Populations

In *The History of Sexuality, Volume 1: The Will to Knowledge* (1976), Foucault presents another axis of power alongside disciplinary power: biopower. Biopower is not the discipline of individual bodies but the management of life processes—birth, death, health, illness—at the level of the population. This power is a technology of governance that uses statistics and demography to make the vital processes of populations predictable and to optimize them.

Insurance is one of the typical apparatuses of biopower. Life insurance, which developed after the 17th century, has calculated risk based on mortality statistics, collectivized risk, and managed individual uncertainty. As François Ewald analyzed in *The Insurance State* (1986), insurance transforms chance into a calculable object through the concept of "risk" and creates new forms of social solidarity. In traditional insurance, the individual exists only as a member of a statistical group. The concrete life of the individual is reduced to the average characteristics of the risk group.

### 2.3 Insurance Before John Hancock: The Junction of Disciplinary Power and Biopower

Traditional life insurance was a typical apparatus of biopower. Insurance companies used population statistics, mortality tables, and disease statistics to calculate risk and set premiums accordingly. Individuals were assigned to risk groups through health screenings or questionnaires, but once enrolled, their daily behaviors did not directly affect their premiums. Insurance did not discipline individual lives. Rather, insurance functioned to socialize risk and convert individual uncertainty into a collective safety net.

However, John Hancock Vitality breaks this tradition. Vitality continuously monitors policyholders' daily behaviors—step count, heart rate, sleep patterns—and directly reflects this data in their premiums. Policyholders who move more, sleep more regularly, and maintain stable heart rates receive premium discounts. This no longer remains at the level of biopolitical demographic management. The individual body becomes an object of continuous surveillance and evaluation, and the results of that evaluation are directly linked to economic incentives.

## 3. Rouvroy's Algorithmic Governmentality: Bypassing the Subject and Governing Virtuality

### 3.1 The Concept of Algorithmic Governmentality

Antoinette Rouvroy and Thomas Berns presented the concept of algorithmic governmentality in their 2013 paper "Algorithmic Governmentality and Prospects of Emancipation." This concept analyzes how big data and algorithms fundamentally transform traditional modes of governance. According to Rouvroy, algorithmic governmentality bypasses the subject instead of disciplining or training it. That is, it targets the potential behavioral possibilities of individuals directly through statistical correlations extracted from data, without passing through individual consciousness, will, or identity.

While traditional governance operated through the subject's interior (conscience, will, desires), algorithmic governmentality ignores the subject's interior. What matters is not who the individual is or what they want, but what statistical patterns the individual's data shows. These patterns predict future behavior independently of the individual's conscious decisions. Rouvroy calls this the "governance of virtuality." Power no longer disciplines actual behavior or consciousness but manages statistically calculated potential possibilities.

### 3.2 The Emergence of the Data Double

The core apparatus of algorithmic governmentality is the "data double." The data double is a digital representative of the individual that exists independently of the actual body in data space. It is composed of numerous data points extracted from the individual's behavior, preferences, health status, social relationships, etc., and is continuously updated and evaluated by algorithms.

The data double exists independently of the individual's subjective experience. Even if the individual is unaware of their data double, it has real effects on their life—credit ratings, insurance risk, employability, etc. More importantly, the data double is treated as more "real" than the individual's actual behavior. Algorithms make decisions based on the data double, and individuals must act to improve their data double.

### 3.3 Algorithmic Governmentality in John Hancock Vitality

John Hancock Vitality is a typical case of algorithmic governmentality. Policyholders wear wearable devices like Fitbit or Apple Watch, which continuously collect data on heart rate, step count, sleep patterns, and activity levels. This data is fed into the insurer's algorithm to calculate an individual's "health score." This score determines the premium discount rate, and policyholders try to increase their score by walking more, sleeping more regularly, and maintaining a stable heart rate.

What is important here is the mode of power operation. Vitality does not demand "change your body" from the policyholder. Instead, it demands "raise your score." Bodily change is merely a means to raise the score; the direct target of power is not the body but the score—that is, the data double. The policyholder does not directly train their body but acts to optimize their data. This is fundamentally different from the way Foucault's disciplinary power directly trained the temporal and spatial arrangement of individual bodies.

## 4. Foucauldian Subject or Data Double?

### 4.1 Continuity and Discontinuity Between Disciplinary Power and Algorithmic Governmentality

The John Hancock case shows that disciplinary power and algorithmic governmentality are not completely disconnected but intersect in complex ways. On one hand, Vitality clearly includes elements of disciplinary power. Policyholders are continuously monitored, internalize the awareness that their data is being evaluated, and adjust their behavior accordingly. This is similar to the mechanism of self-discipline that Foucault analyzed in the Panopticon. The policyholder becomes a subject who internalizes the insurer's surveillance and disciplines themselves.

On the other hand, Vitality's power goes beyond the typical characteristics of disciplinary power. While disciplinary power produced a "docile body" through the concrete training of individual bodies, Vitality is more interested in the body's data than the body itself. Whether the policyholder actually becomes healthier is secondary. What matters is that the health score goes up. This score may not perfectly correspond to actual health status. For example, a policyholder might take medication to lower their heart rate or engage in meaningless walking to increase step count. This is behavior that prioritizes data optimization over genuine bodily health.

### 4.2 The Position of the Subject: Transformation of the Care of the Self

For Foucault, the subject is not a passive product of power. Power constitutes the subject, but at the same time, the subject has the capacity to resist power. In his later work, especially the 1982 lectures *The Hermeneutics of the Subject*, Foucault explores how the subject can resist power and speak truth through care of the self and parrhesia. Care of the self is a practice of constituting oneself as an ethical subject, and parrhesia is the courage to speak truth at the risk of danger.

How is care of the self transformed under the conditions of algorithmic governmentality? The Vitality policyholder performs "care of the self" by managing and optimizing their health data. However, this care of the self is subordinated to the standard of "health" defined by the insurer's algorithm. The policyholder is more interested in strategic actions to raise their score than in a genuine understanding of their own body. This is far from the authentic care of the self that Foucault spoke of—knowledge of oneself, ethical reflection on oneself.

So, is the Vitality policyholder still a subject in the Foucauldian sense? Partially, yes. The policyholder is aware of their data and consciously acts to improve it. This presupposes the active participation of the subject. However, this subject is defined by their data double and instrumentalizes their body for the optimization of that data double. The subject no longer has a direct relationship with their body but an indirect relationship mediated by data. This signifies the emergence of a new form of subject—the "data subject."

### 4.3 The Paradox of Raising the Score: Internalization of Discipline or Gamification?

The "raise your score" demanded by Vitality reveals a tension between the internalization of disciplinary power and the gamification of algorithmic governmentality. The policyholder adjusts their behavior to raise their score. This seems similar to the typical mechanism of disciplinary power—internalizing external norms to discipline oneself. However, the norms of disciplinary power were socially constructed standards of "normality." In contrast, Vitality's score is based on statistical correlations calculated by an algorithm. This score is not a social norm but the result of a predictive model derived from data patterns.

This difference has important implications for the possibility of resistance. Resistance to disciplinary power could take the form of challenging the norm itself—that is, problematizing the standard of "normality." However, in algorithmic governmentality, the score is produced by an opaque algorithm, and the criteria for its calculation are not disclosed or are difficult to understand. The policyholder may know which actions are effective for raising the score, but not why those actions are reflected in the score. This obscures the target of resistance.

## 5. The (Im)possibility of Resistance: Reconstructing Care of the Self and Parrhesia

### 5.1 Changing Conditions of Parrhesia

Foucault's parrhesia is the courage to speak truth, an act of speaking truth against power. Parrhesia occurs when the speaker takes a risk in their truth-telling. It operates when an ancient Greek citizen speaks truth to a tyrant, or when a philosopher speaks truth to a powerful person without flattery.

Under the conditions of algorithmic governmentality, toward what can parrhesia operate? First, parrhesia as speaking the truth of data is possible. When a policyholder's health data is inaccurate or distorted, they can speak up to correct it. For example, if a wearable device incorrectly measures heart rate or sleep data, the policyholder can challenge the insurer. This is parrhesia demanding the truth of data.

Second, parrhesia against the opacity of algorithms is possible. Policyholders can demand the disclosure of scoring criteria or criticize the discriminatory effects of algorithms. This is parrhesia demanding transparency in the operation of power.

Third, more fundamentally, parrhesia that resists datafication itself is possible. Policyholders can assert their right not to consent to the collection and commodification of their data. This is parrhesia that problematizes the very foundation of algorithmic governmentality.

### 5.2 Reconstructing Care of the Self: The Ethics of the Data Subject

Foucault's care of the self is a practice of constituting oneself as an ethical subject. This practice involves reflecting on one's desires, actions, and thoughts, and through this, making oneself free. How can care of the self be reconstructed under the conditions of algorithmic governmentality?

First, cultivating data literacy can become a form of care of the self. By understanding how their data is collected, used, and processed by algorithms, policyholders can secure a reflective distance from their data double. This is a process of deepening self-understanding as a data subject.

Second, asserting data sovereignty can become a practice of care of the self. Policyholders can demand control over their data and decide for themselves the scope and purpose of data use, thereby establishing a subjective relationship with their data double.

Third, care of the self can be extended into collective forms of resistance. Individual-level data management alone is insufficient to counter the structural power of algorithmic governmentality. Therefore, it is necessary to check data power through collective organizations such as data cooperatives, insurance policyholder solidarity groups, and citizen groups for algorithmic oversight. This is a way of connecting what Foucault called "care of the self" with "care of others."

### 5.3 Possibilities of Resistance in the John Hancock Case

What specific forms of resistance might exist in the John Hancock Vitality case? First, policyholders can manipulate or distort their data. For example, they might shake their wearable device to inflate step count or manipulate heart rate data. This is a form of "gamification" of the scoring system, a micro-resistance that disrupts the operation of power.

Second, policyholders can refuse or limit data sharing. Vitality makes data sharing a condition of insurance enrollment, but policyholders can resist by sharing only minimal data or blocking data during certain time periods.

Third, as a more systematic form of resistance, insurance policyholders can collectively demand transparency and fairness in data use. For example, they can demand the disclosure of the scoring algorithm or call for regulations to prevent certain health conditions or lifestyles from being disadvantaged.

However, all these forms of resistance operate within the basic framework of algorithmic governmentality. Manipulating data acknowledges the scoring system; refusing data sharing means forgoing insurance benefits. More fundamental resistance would problematize the ontological foundation of algorithmic governmentality itself—that is, the individualization and commodification of risk through data. This would require redefining the social function of insurance and reviving the principle of solidarity, where risk is borne collectively rather than individually.

## 6. The Evolution of Technical Objects and Regressive Closed Loops: Simondon, Generative AI, and Model Collapse

### 6.1 Simondon's Concept of Concretization and Vitality

In *On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects* (1958), Gilbert Simondon argues that technical objects evolve from an abstract stage to a stage of concretization. In the abstract stage, each component of the technical object functions independently; in the concretization stage, components become mutually coordinated and functions overlap, forming a more efficient and stable system.

Vitality can be interpreted through Simondon's concept of concretization. Early fitness trackers were merely independent devices that measured step count. However, Vitality connects these devices to the insurance system, integrating multiple functions—data collection, risk assessment, premium calculation, and behavioral inducement—into a single system. This can be seen as a process of technical concretization. The wearable device evolves from a simple measurement tool into a core component of insurance, and the insurance system becomes a self-regulating system that optimizes itself through data feedback.

However, Simondon's concretization presupposes the "openness" (ouverture) of the technical object. A concretized technical object has the potential to continue evolving through interaction with its environment. Does Vitality possess this openness? On one hand, Vitality has the openness to integrate new data sources (e.g., blood glucose monitors, stress sensors). On the other hand, Vitality's algorithm enforces specific health norms and penalizes behaviors that deviate from these norms, thereby fixing user behavior in a particular direction—a form of closure.
