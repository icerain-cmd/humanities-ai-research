# Media Aesthetics-Value in the Age of Technogenesis: Generative AI Advertising Images, Eco-Friendly Representations, and the Tension of Platform Logic

## 1. Introduction: The Point of Conflict Between Technical Efficiency and Ecological Value

Since 2024, the full-scale introduction of generative AI technology into the advertising industry has brought about a fundamental change in the production methods of advertising images that claim to be eco-friendly. In the past, 'eco-friendly' images, which were built through actual photography or graphic design, are now being mass-produced using only text prompts. Visual elements that instill an environmentally friendly impression in consumers—such as clear air in forests, pristine beaches, or the texture of recycled materials—no longer require actual photography or manual labor. This is a revolutionary change in terms of advertising production efficiency and scalability, but it simultaneously raises serious aesthetic and ethical problems.

The core of the problem is that the massive energy consumption and carbon emissions involved in the process of generative AI producing these images are not made visible. The advertising images claim to be eco-friendly, but their production itself relies on a technical infrastructure that burdens the environment. Moreover, the logic of the platform economy systematically conceals the carbon costs and accountability of the production process by treating the efficiency of image production and the scale of distribution themselves as evidence of value. In this situation, by what criteria should media aesthetics determine the value conflicts between beauty, efficiency, and sustainability? To answer this question, this paper aims to develop an argument by connecting Gilbert Simondon's concept of the concretization of technical objects, the synthetic data feedback loop of generative AI, and the phenomenon of model collapse.

## 2. The Production Mechanism of Generative AI Advertising Images and Platform Logic

### 2.1. The Aesthetics of Efficiency: How Generative AI Advertising Works

The process by which generative AI produces advertising images is fundamentally different from traditional methods. In the past, advertising photography involved a process that required material and human resources: actual location shooting, prop preparation, model casting, and lighting setup. In contrast, generative AI probabilistically synthesizes images corresponding to text prompts based on vast training data. In this process, 'eco-friendly' visual elements such as natural landscapes, recycled materials, and green energy facilities exist as pre-learned patterns within the database and are recombined according to user requests.

The important point here is that these images are no longer the result of 'representation' but of 'synthesis.' While traditional advertising photography was an act of capturing an actually existing subject, generative AI images create 'plausible' images based on statistical probability. This fundamentally shakes the issue of advertising truthfulness. Eco-friendly advertising images no longer serve as evidence proving the environmental impact of an actual product but function merely as 'plausible' visual representations.

As can be seen in the provided literature, generative AI establishes a new cognitive system where "density and probability become the basis of trust instead of authority and hierarchy" (Document 1). In other words, while in traditional advertising, the authenticity of the shoot, the authority of the professional photographer, and the physical connection to the actual product were the basis of trust, in the era of generative AI, the 'density' of the image (how detailed and high-quality it is) and its 'probability' (how well it conforms to typical patterns) become the new standards of trust. This means that eco-friendly representations can be produced as a probabilistic combination of images that merely look statistically 'eco-friendly,' regardless of actual environmental impact.

### 2.2. Platform Logic and the Inversion of Value

The core logic of the platform economy is based on 'economies of scale' and 'network effects.' The more advertising images are created and distributed, the more their value increases. Generative AI perfectly aligns with this platform logic. It can instantly generate near-infinite variations of images and, through A/B testing, select the most effective images for mass distribution.

The problem is that this quantitative proliferation itself replaces a qualitative assessment of environmental value. Advertisers and platforms use "how many images were exposed" as a metric of success, and whether the image is genuinely eco-friendly or merely appears eco-friendly becomes a secondary issue. More seriously, the fact that the image generation process of generative AI itself consumes enormous energy is systematically concealed. The power consumed to generate a single high-resolution image is equivalent to hundreds of search queries, which directly translates into carbon emissions from data centers. However, the 'eco-friendliness' that the advertising image claims completely ignores the material conditions of this production process, emphasizing only the visual representation of the final output.

From the perspective of media aesthetics, this phenomenon can be understood as a process where efficiency and distribution scale are transformed into criteria for judging aesthetic value. While traditional aesthetics explored the essence of 'beauty,' platform logic places 'efficiency' and 'spreadability' at the core of aesthetic value. Images produced "more, faster, and wider" come to be evaluated as 'good' images. This connects to the analysis in the provided literature that "as density and probability become the basis of trust, 'reading' is replaced by 'editing'" (Document 1). A cultural shift is occurring where 'editing' (efficient combination) is prioritized over 'reading' (interpretation of meaning) of advertising images.

## 3. The Synthetic Data Feedback Loop and Model Collapse: Degenerative Closure of the Technical Object

### 3.1. The Operation of the Synthetic Data Feedback Loop

Generative AI systems heavily depend on the quality of their training data. However, as these systems operate continuously, a phenomenon occurs where images previously generated by AI flow back into the training data. This is called a synthetic data feedback loop. Initially, learning is based on actual photographs and human-designed data, but over time, images generated by AI are distributed on the web and are then used again as training data for new models, creating a recurring cycle.

In the case of eco-friendly advertising images, this feedback loop is particularly problematic. Data from human designers who photograph or design actual eco-friendly products or sites reflects the physical conditions of the real world. For example, the texture of actual recycled paper, the real optical properties of solar panels, and the actual colors of plants under natural light are captured within physical limits and conditions. However, images generated by AI are free from these physical constraints. 'More eco-friendly' images with perfect lighting, ideal colors, and exaggerated textures are born. When these generated images flow back into the training data, the model gradually learns 'hyper-realistic eco-friendly' representations that are increasingly disconnected from reality.

### 3.2. Model Collapse and the Simplification of Eco-Friendly Representations

If this feedback loop continues, the phenomenon of model collapse eventually occurs. Model collapse refers to the gradual degradation of data diversity and quality through repeated learning on AI-generated data. Initially, eco-friendly images from various angles, lighting conditions, and compositions can be generated, but as the feedback loop repeats, the distribution of the training data converges on specific patterns. Only the most typical and probable 'eco-friendly' image patterns are selectively reinforced, while subtle differences and new variations disappear.

This leads to an important contrast with Simondon's theory of technical objects. Simondon viewed that technical objects evolve, becoming more precisely 'concretized' in their functions. It is a process where an initially abstract technology develops into a concrete functional structure through interaction with its environment. For example, the evolution of an early car engine from an abstract stage of simply generating power to a concrete stage integrating cooling, exhaust, and fuel supply is a typical example of concretization. This concretization proceeds in a direction where the technical object better adapts to environmental conditions and increases efficiency.

However, the synthetic data feedback loop and model collapse of generative AI show a movement in the opposite direction. Instead of evolving through interaction with the environment, the technical object recursively consumes its own output, resulting in a degenerative movement of internal closure. In other words, a phenomenon occurs that can be called a 'closed loop' or 'degenerative closure,' rather than 'concretization.' The technical object increasingly loses connection with the physical and ecological conditions of the real world, becoming trapped in a loop of self-reproduction according to its own internal logic (statistical probability).

This interpretation connects to the analysis in the provided literature that "the reason generative AI, beyond being a simple editing tool, can directly engage with our thinking is because the knowledge structuring of artificial nature internalizes the order of artificial nature" (Document 4). The internalization of the order of artificial nature can be read as meaning that the statistical order within the AI system is prioritized over the ecological order of real nature. Eco-friendly representations are replaced by the order of 'artificial nature,' determined by the probability distribution inside the AI, rather than reflecting the ecological conditions of real nature.

## 4. Criteria for Value Judgment in Media Aesthetics: Between Evolution and Degeneration of the Technical Object

### 4.1. Evolutionary Interpretation: Generative AI as Concretization of the Technical Object

The view of generative AI's development as the evolution of a technical object remains powerful. According to this view, the AI's ability to generate more sophisticated images more efficiently is a typical case of technical concretization. While early GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks) were limited to simple pattern generation, the latest diffusion models can generate images that accurately reflect the meaning of text while possessing artistic value. This can be interpreted as a process where the technology increasingly acquires more specific and integrated functions.

This perspective offers room for a positive evaluation of the increase in eco-friendly advertising images. The advancement of AI technology allows more companies to convey eco-friendly messages at a lower cost, which could contribute to raising environmental awareness. It is also possible to argue that efficient production reduces the material resources consumed in advertising production itself. While traditional advertising production required numerous personnel and resources, AI generates images in the digital space, so no physical waste is produced.

However, this evolutionary interpretation has critical limitations. First, the concretization of generative AI is directed not towards the ecological conditions of the real world but towards its own internal efficiency. While Simondon's concept of concretization emphasizes the mutual adaptation of technology and environment, the development of generative AI operates in a direction that rather blocks connection with the environment. Second, the evolutionary interpretation systematically overlooks the energy consumption and carbon emissions of the production process. It conceals the material basis of the energy consumed in AI model training and inference under the concept of 'virtuality.'

### 4.2. Degenerative Interpretation: Generative AI as a Closed Loop

Conversely, the view that interprets the development of generative AI as a process of degenerative closure is based on the previously discussed synthetic data feedback loop and model collapse phenomena. This view argues that AI systems fail to absorb the diversity and complexity of the real world, recursively consuming their own output and producing increasingly monotonous and distorted representations.

In the case of eco-friendly advertising images, this degenerative interpretation is particularly persuasive. Initially, various real-world eco-friendly examples (various recycling technologies, eco-friendly architecture from different regions, actual nature reserves, etc.) were referenced, but as the feedback loop repeats, eco-friendly representations converge on increasingly typical and abstract patterns. Images simplify to universally recognized 'green forests and clear skies,' 'clean beaches and solar panels,' 'green leaves and recycling symbols.' While this simplification may increase the appeal of the advertisement, it conceals the actual complexity and regional specificity of environmental problems.

A more serious problem is that this degenerative closure fails to make the carbon costs of the production process transparent. Every time an image is generated, the data center consumes power, servers emit heat, and this ultimately leads to fossil fuel consumption. However, the advertising image itself leaves no trace of these material conditions. It is distributed as a perfectly cleaned digital image. This is the core challenge facing media aesthetics: how to critically analyze the structure where the beauty and efficiency of an image systematically conceal the material conditions of its production?

### 4.3. Synthesis of the Two Interpretations: A Triple Criterion for Media Aesthetics

Between the evolutionary and degenerative interpretations, media aesthetics must seek new criteria for judgment. These two interpretations are not mutually exclusive but reveal two opposing characteristics that the technical object of generative AI simultaneously possesses. In other words, while AI technology is being concretized in terms of technical efficiency, it is experiencing degenerative closure in ecological and ethical terms. Therefore, the value judgment of media aesthetics should be based not on a single criterion but on the following triple criteria.

First, the **criterion of making the materiality of the production process visible**. Media aesthetics must evaluate the material conditions of the production process that exist beyond the aesthetic surface of the image. No matter how beautiful and efficient a generative AI-produced eco-friendly advertising image is, if it fails to make visible the energy, resources, and carbon emissions consumed in its production, its aesthetic value cannot be fully recognized. Unlike platform logic, which treats efficiency and scale as evidence of value, media aesthetics must critically evaluate the material foundation that conditions efficiency.

Second, the **criterion of relational authenticity between artificial nature and nature**. The perspective in the provided literature that posits artificial nature as a knowledge ecosystem (Documents 3, 5) raises the need to evaluate the relationship the image world built by generative AI has with real nature. An important criterion is whether eco-friendly advertising images follow only the internal order of artificial nature (statistical probability) or reflect the ecological conditions of real nature (actual carbon emissions, limitations of actual eco-friendly technologies, etc.). This is a criterion for evaluating whether the image maintains relational authenticity with real nature, beyond simply whether the image 'looks eco-friendly.'

Third, the **criterion of critical distance from the recursivity of technology**. Generative AI has a recursive structure where it uses its own generated images again as training data. Media aesthetics must evaluate whether this recursivity operates as an open loop or a closed loop. An open loop is a direction that continuously absorbs new data from the real world (actual environmental changes, new eco-friendly technologies, regional specificities, etc.), while a closed loop is a direction of increasing simplification within the circulation of existing data. The value judgment of media aesthetics should take the diversity of images and the ability to reflect reality as important criteria.
