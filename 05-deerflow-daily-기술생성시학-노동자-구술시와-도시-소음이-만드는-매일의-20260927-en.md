# Identity and Interpretability of the Work in Techno-Generative Poetics — Analysis of a Complex Media Event

## 0. Introduction: The Ontological Turn of the Question

The fourfold mediation chain—1980s worker oral poetry, real-time urban noise, generative language model, and exhibition output (voice, subtitles, vibrations)—fundamentally transforms the mode of existence of poetry itself. In this case, 'poetry' is no longer a fixed text, nor is it the inner expression of an author. It is a data-media event that is newly produced each day. Below, from the perspective of techno-generative poetics, I analyze how this case should redefine the identity and interpretability of the work along three axes.

## 1. Redefining the Identity of the Work: The Work as a 'Generative Genealogy'

### 1.1. From Substantial Identity to Procedural Invariant

In traditional poetics, the identity of a work is based on the **substantial fixity of the text**. The principle of identity—'this poem is this poem'—guaranteed by identical word arrangements, identical lines and stanzas, and an identical speaker, serves as the criterion for judging a work's identity.

However, in the proposed complex media case, this criterion does not function:

- Since a new recited poem is produced each day, the text differs daily
- The 'author' is distributed among humans (1980s workers) and non-humans (language model, urban noise sensors)
- The sensory modality changes depending on the output medium (voice, subtitles, vibrations)

In this situation, the identity of the work must be redefined not as **substantial identity** but as a **procedural invariant**. That is, the criterion for judging a work's identity becomes not 'what is the same' but 'what process is maintained.'

In techno-generative poetics, the identity of the work is secured on three procedural levels:

| Level | Invariant | Content |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| **Data source** | Dual input structure: archive + real-time | 1980s oral archive + real-time urban noise stream |
| **Transformation rules** | Language model's generation parameters and prompt structure | The same generation rules applied to new input each day |
| **Sensory transformation** | Output mapping rules (correspondence between voice, subtitles, vibrations) | The rules by which the same text is transformed into three sensory channels |

As long as these three procedures are maintained, even if the text changes daily, it can be regarded as 'one work.' This is similar to the way improvisation in music is recognized as 'the same piece,' but differs in that the generative procedure is specified much more rigorously.

### 1.2. Points of Contact with Benjamin's Aura Discussion

Among the Khoj search results, documents related to Benjamin (Documents 3, 4, 5) provide important theoretical resources at this point. As Document 5 notes, Benjamin, in "The Author as Producer" and in his discussion of the age of technical reproducibility, traced "the changes that technology has brought to human perception and structures of experience" and discussed the transformation of the forms of artistic production and reception.

However, this case **goes one step further** than Benjamin's discussion of reproduction. While Benjamin problematized the deconstruction of the hierarchy between original and copy, here the situation is that **the 'original' itself is generated daily**. A paradox of 'original reproduction' or 'generative reproduction' arises.

The concept of "pseudo-aura" in Document 3 is suggestive at this point. The logic that "through exhibition value, it further solidifies its cult value" can be applied to the way that the newly generated recited poem each day is experienced as a 'unique event' within the institutional space of the exhibition. However, techno-generative poetics does not view this 'pseudo-aura' simply as an effect of the exhibition system, but reinterprets it as **a condition of uniqueness inherent in the generative procedure itself**. That is, the daily recited poem is a product of the same procedure, but because that procedure feeds on different real-time data each day, it produces a unique result each time. This 'procedural uniqueness' is the new condition for aura unique to techno-generative poetics.

### 1.3. The Distribution of 'Originality'

Document 4 notes "the point where technology is narrated as a subject" and quotes Benjamin's discussion of technology. The phrase "technology flows into the trenches in the form of armies instead of power plants" suggests that technology no longer functions as a tool but as an agent.

This perspective directly connects to the issue of the identity of the work in techno-generative poetics. In this system where the language model, urban noise sensors, and output devices each function as 'subjects,' the originality of the work cannot be fixed anywhere. Originality belongs:

- partly to the archive (past worker utterances)
- partly to the real-time data (current urban noise)
- partly to the generative model (the algorithm with transformation rules)
- partly to the output media (the materiality of voice, subtitles, vibrations)

The identity of the work can only be grasped as a **superimposition effect** of these distributed originalities. This can be linked to Friedrich Kittler's framework—media determinism, where media determine the message—but unlike Kittler, techno-generative poetics focuses on the **generation that occurs in the gap** between the materiality of media and human semiotic processes.

## 2. Redefining Interpretability: From Meaning Interpretation to the Tracing of Triggering Effects

### 2.1. Limits of Traditional Hermeneutics

Traditional hermeneutics presupposes 'immanent meaning' in the text and posits a process of 'fusion' (H. G. Gadamer) between the interpreter's horizon of understanding and the work's horizon of meaning. Furthermore, traditional literary criticism takes the linguistic subtlety of the poetic text—rhetoric, imagery, meter, rhythm—as the core object of interpretation.

In the proposed case, this approach encounters obstacles on three levels:

1. **Daily text change**: Performing 'deep interpretation' on a different text each day is practically impossible. If the exhibition lasts three months, about 90 different poems would be generated, making it physically and conceptually impossible to apply traditional literary criticism to each one.

2. **Non-human origin of meaning**: If the 'meaning' of the poetic text is determined not by human intentionality but by algorithmic probabilistic calculation, that meaning cannot be the object of traditional semantics. Language models handle only the statistical distribution of signifiers, not the signified.

3. **Sensory heterogeneity**: When the same text is simultaneously output through voice, subtitles, and vibrations—heterogeneous sensory modalities—'interpretation' can no longer be confined to grasping linguistic meaning. Each sensory channel generates 'meaning' in a different way.

### 2.2. Three Directions for Redefining Interpretability

Techno-generative poetics redefines interpretability in three directions:

**① Procedural interpretation**

The object of interpretation shifts from the 'meaning' of the text to the 'logic' of the generative procedure. The critic's task is not to analyze the meaning of individual texts but to ask **why this text was generated here today**:

- Which urban noise data was selected as today's input?
- What phrases from the archive did the language model connect with the urban noise?
- What information was lost or amplified in the transformation between output media?

In this interpretive mode, individual texts are not 'evidence' but 'traces.' The true object of interpretation is the overall operation of the generative system revealed through the text.

**② Sensory-ecological interpretation**

The unit of interpretation expands from 'text' to 'sensory event.' The complex sensory field co-generated by voice (auditory), subtitles (visual), and vibrations (tactile) becomes the object of interpretation.

What is important here is that each sensory channel does not simply 'translate' the same information into a different modality, but **produces different sensory effects**. The auditory rhythm and the tactile vibration rhythm interact but are not identical. This 'inter-sensory gap' becomes a key point of interpretation.

**③ Event-trace interpretation**

Each daily generated recited poem comes from the same procedure but is a different 'event.' Interpretation is carried out by tracing the **genealogy** of these events:

- What is the relationship between the poem on D-1 and the poem on D-2?
- What patterns repeat and what patterns emerge suddenly?
- What variations arise when 1980s worker utterances meet today's urban noise?

This interpretive mode appears similar to traditional literary history's 'influence studies,' but it is fundamentally different in that its object is not the influence among human authors but the complex network of data, media, and humans.

### 2.3. The Changing Role of the Interpreter

This redefinition of interpretation also changes the role of the interpreter (critic, audience, researcher):

- **From meaning discoverer → condition analyst**: The interpreter does not 'discover' the deep meaning of the text but analyzes the 'conditions' under which a particular text could be generated
- **From appreciator of a finished work → participant in an ongoing process**: Tracks the text changing daily during the exhibition period, observing and recording the patterns of change
- **From tracer of a single author → cartographer of a distributed actor network**: Maps the relationships among humans, algorithms, sensors, and output devices

### 3.2. Theoretical Issues of the Question Itself

The question itself implies three unresolved issues that go beyond the above limitations:

**Issue 1: Temporal boundaries of procedural identity**

When the procedural invariant becomes the criterion for the identity of the work, the problem arises of **when the procedure can be considered the 'same' procedure**. If the language model is updated? If the prompt is modified? If the collection range of urban noise data changes? This question is a boundary condition that techno-generative poetics will inevitably face when it meets the practical operation of art institutions.

**Issue 2: Tension between democratization and specialization of interpretation**

The more open and transparent the generative procedure, the more people can participate in 'interpretation,' but at the same time, the need for specialized expertise to overview the entire generative system also increases. The 'procedural interpretation' demanded by techno-generative poetics requires far more technical and media knowledge than traditional literary criticism, creating a new tension between specialization and democratization of interpretation.

**Issue 3: The political weight of 1980s worker oral poetry**

The most sensitive issue in this case is the risk that 1980s worker oral poetry is reduced to mere 'data.' These texts contain historical struggles and suffering, and were uttered in a specific political and social context. When these utterances become 'training data' for a language model, are 'combined' with real-time urban noise, and transformed into new texts, how is the political weight of the historical utterances handled?

This is not simply a problem of 'data ethics' but a question about **the ontological limits of techno-generative poetics**. Under what media conditions is the political nature of historical utterances preserved, and under what conditions is it erased? And who should make this choice, and how?

## 4. Conclusion: Toward a Work Concept for Techno-Generative Poetics

Through the proposed complex media case, the concept of the work in techno-generative poetics should be redefined as follows:

| Traditional Poetics | Techno-Generative Poetics |
|---------------------|---------------------------|
| Work = fixed text | Work = temporal unfolding of generative procedure |
| Identity = substantial identity of text | Identity = maintenance of procedural invariant |
| Interpretation = discovery of meaning | Interpretation = analysis of generative conditions + tracing of sensory events |
| Author = individual human | Author = distributed network of humans, technology, data |
| Original = first text | Original = unique event occurring each time |

The core of this redefinition is a **shift from the ontology of 'is' to the conditional logic of 'enables.'** Instead of asking 'Is this poetry or not?', techno-generative poetics asks, 'What are the conditions under which this can function as poetry?' Identity of the work is not a property of the text but the condition under which the entire generative system is maintained, and interpretability is not the discovery of meaning but the tracing of generative conditions and sensory effects.

This approach inherits the Benjaminian tradition suggested by the Khoj documents—the analysis of how technology transforms the forms of artistic production and reception—while evolving that analysis one step further under the new media conditions of generative algorithms. However, this theoretical framework has not yet provided a sufficient answer to the question of how to handle the political weight of 1980s worker oral poetry, and this remains a core task for techno-generative poetics to resolve in the future.
