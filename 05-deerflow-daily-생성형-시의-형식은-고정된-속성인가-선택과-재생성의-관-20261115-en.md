# The Emergence of Poetic Self-Referentiality: The Intersection of Flusser and Jakobson in Generative Poetry

## Reformulating the Problem

The question asks whether formal self-referentiality in the iterative process of generative-AI poetry—prompt → output → selection → revision → regeneration → comparison—is a **fixed property of the final text** or a **relational effect constituted belatedly** within that process. This problem goes beyond a simple aesthetic judgment: it requires a theoretical clarification of the order and media apparatuses through which authorship, contingency, and formal unity, as presupposed by generative poetry, come into being.

Below, I will reconstruct Flusser’s concepts of the “technical image” and the “apparatus,” as well as Jakobson’s “poetic function,” in their respective original contexts, and then apply them to a step-by-step analysis of a concrete interactive poetry-writing case. Through this analysis, I will argue that formal self-referentiality is constituted through the **retroactive grounding** of the iterative process.

## 1. Flusser’s Technical Image and the Logic of the Apparatus

### 1.1 Three Media Stages and the Specificity of the Technical Image

In Flusser’s media theory, human beings have invented media in order to understand the world. He distinguishes three media stages (Vilém Flusser, *Towards a Philosophy of Photography*, 1984/2000; *Into the Universe of Technical Images*, 1985/2011).

1. **Traditional images**: These are produced directly by the human hand and intention, as in painting or drawing. They give meaning to the world, but when the fact that their mode of signification is conditioned by the medium itself is forgotten, they fall into “idolatry.”
2. **Texts**: These dismantle the superficial and magical world of images and introduce linear and historical consciousness. Linear writing made temporal consciousness and history possible. Yet when the abstraction proper to texts is forgotten, they fall into “textolatry.”
3. **Technical images**: These are produced by an apparatus, as in photographs, films, and computer images. They transform the world into images on the basis of texts—optical and chemical equations—but present themselves as if they were nonsymbolic. “Technical images pretend not to be symbolic, like traditional images”—this is the danger Flusser warns against.

The key point is that technical images are “produced not by humans but by apparatuses.” Whereas a traditional painter directly places signs on a canvas by hand, the producer of a technical image is merely a **functionary** who performs the function of the apparatus as a “black box.”

### 1.2 Apparatus, Program, and Black Box

For Flusser, an apparatus is fundamentally different from a machine.

> “An apparatus is not a tool with which humans change the world, but a tool with which they change the meaning of the world.”
> (*Towards a Philosophy of Photography*, p. 24)

A **program** is the total set of combinations possible within an apparatus. A camera contains a program based on optical and chemical laws, and every image a photographer can choose exists within the range permitted by that program. “The number of these possibilities is large, but nevertheless finite” (ibid., p. 26).

The **black box** refers to the fact that the internal operations of an apparatus are opaque to the user. The photographer presses the shutter, but does not fully know how the image is generated. This opacity strengthens the apparatus’s determining power.

The **functionary** is the human who operates the apparatus. For Flusser, the functionary is not an author. The functionary is moved by the apparatus’s program and can choose only within the range of possibilities established by the apparatus.

> “The photographer is no longer a painter who freely draws toward the world. The photographer is a functionary who must choose among the possibilities presented by the black box of the apparatus.”

Yet Flusser’s analysis does not end here. He proposes the possibility of **experimental photography**. The experimental photographer produces informative images by discovering combinations within the apparatus’s program that have not yet been tried. The task of experimental photography is to reveal the “cracks in the program.” The problem lies in the circularity whereby the apparatus automatically absorbs even attempts at liberation and enriches its own program through them.

### 1.3 Applying the Concepts to Generative AI

When Flusser’s concepts are applied to generative AI, the following correspondences can be established:

| Flusser’s concept | Corresponding element in generative AI |
|---|---|
| Apparatus | LLM model, as a neural network functioning as a black box |
| Program | Training-data distribution + architectural conditions + stochastic generation rules |
| Functionary | User who enters prompts and selects outputs |
| Experimental image | Output that transforms the user’s intention in a completely unpredictable way |
| Information vs. redundancy | Unpredictable combinations vs. statistically predictable patterns |

However, in the case of generative AI, Flusser’s analysis must be modified in one important respect. Flusser’s photographic apparatus produces a single output in response to the functionary’s input—shutter, aperture, and so on—whereas generative AI produces **stochastic variation** in response to the same prompt. This variation makes possible a **recursive loop** in which the prompt can be re-entered and a selected output can be used as the condition for the next stage. This point is directly connected to the problem of how form emerges in generative poetry.

## 2. Jakobson’s Poetic Function: The Relation between the Axis of Selection and the Axis of Combination

### 2.1 The Model of Language Functions

Roman Jakobson’s 1960 essay “Closing Statement: Linguistics and Poetics” presents six functions of language:

1. **Referential function**: oriented toward the context
2. **Emotive function**: oriented toward the addresser
3. **Conative function**: oriented toward the addressee
4. **Phatic function**: oriented toward contact
5. **Metalingual function**: oriented toward the code
6. **Poetic function**: oriented toward the message itself

The poetic function is a “focus on the message for its own sake” and “promotes the palpability of signs.” Jakobson emphasizes that the poetic function is not limited to poetry. In poetry, the poetic function is dominant, but in other linguistic activities it operates as an accessory component.

### 2.2 The Two Axes of Selection and Combination

Jakobson’s most important insight concerns the structural mechanism of the poetic function:

> “The poetic function projects the principle of equivalence from the axis of selection into the axis of combination.”
> (Jakobson, “Closing Statement,” 1960, p. 358)

The meaning of this statement is as follows. Every linguistic act involves two basic operations:

- **Selection**: One item is chosen from among the available items on the basis of similarity or difference. For example, one may choose “lie down,” “doze,” or “nap” instead of “sleep.” On this dimension, the items stand in relations of equivalence, such as synonymy and antonymy.
- **Combination**: The selected items are arranged into a chain on the basis of contiguity.

In ordinary language, selection takes place along the axis of equivalence, while combination takes place along the axis of contiguity. However, **when the poetic function is operative, the principle of equivalence is projected from the axis of selection onto the axis of combination**. In other words, patterns of equivalence involving sound, rhythm, intonation, and stress regulate the arrangement of the linguistic chain. Meter, alliteration, rhyme, and parallel syntax are results of this projection.

For example, in the political slogan “I like Ike”:

- “Ike” has a referential function, pointing to Eisenhower, while
- the vowel similarity between “I” and “like” (/aɪ/ repetition), together with the arrangement of consonants between “like” and “Ike,” activates the poetic function.

### 2.3 Conditions of the Poetic Function

The minimum conditions for the poetic function to operate can be summarized as follows:

1. The message must draw attention to its own form—its sounds, rhythm, or syntactic structure.
2. Relations of equivalence must organize the axis of combination rather than remain confined to the dimension of selection.
3. The materiality of signs—sound and rhythm—must be inseparably intertwined with the communication of meaning.

It is important that Jakobson’s poetic function is **always retrospective**: it is analytically identified in an utterance that has already been completed. Jakobson does not systematically address the process through which the poetic function is generated—that is, how an author performs this projection during the creative process. His model itself cannot determine whether the poetic function is a **property of the completed text** or a **property of the generative process**.

## 3. Case Analysis: The Emergence of Form through Repeated Prompting, Selection, Revision, and Regeneration

We can now make the analysis concrete through a hypothetical but realistic case. Let us assume the following poetry-writing process.

> **User A** asks generative AI to produce a short poem combining three images: “an autumn library, loneliness, and dust by a window.” The first prompt generates three variations. A chooses the second variation, revises its line breaks and one word (“dust” → “speck”), and then asks the AI to regenerate it with “the same atmosphere but a more regular syllable count.” After repeating this process three times, A finalizes the last version.

### 3.1 Stage 1: Stochastic Variation—The Model’s Generation, or the Operation of the Apparatus’s Program

In response to the first prompt, the AI generates three variations. These variations are produced on the basis of poetic patterns distributed throughout the model’s training data: formal rules of poetry, lexical choices, and statistical preferences for rhythm.

At this stage, **the poetic function has not yet emerged**. The generated texts are merely stochastic events. Whether they appear to possess the poetic function is determined not by a structural relation between selection and combination, but by statistical correlations in the training data. In Flusser’s terms, this is simply the result of the apparatus’s program operating. Just as the model has no “intention,” it also has no formal self-referentiality. The fact that the text looks like poetry is only a condition for the **possibility** that a user, as observer, will recognize poetic patterns within it.

**An important theoretical distinction**: The output at this stage is not yet the “poetry” analyzed by Jakobson. The condition Jakobson requires—the projection of equivalence from the axis of selection onto the axis of combination—does not presuppose the creative subject’s intention, but it does presuppose at least a **structural relation between equivalence and combination**. In the model’s output, this relation is merely the accidental product of a probabilistic distribution. Thus, what exists here is only an “illusion of the poetic function.”

**→ This is the moment when the model’s stochastic variations expand the creative possibilities of form.** The differences among the variations present formal possibilities the user could not have predicted in advance: unexpected metaphors, irregular rhythms, and unusual lexical combinations. In Flusser’s terms, this is a field of possibilities in which “cracks in the program” may become visible.

### 3.2 Stage 2: Curatorial Selection—The User’s First Intervention, or the Functionary’s Choice

User A selects one of the three variations. This is the **first authorial act**. Yet, according to Flusser’s analysis, this choice is already structured by the apparatus’s program. All selectable variations exist within the range generated by the model, and the user cannot choose a form outside that range.

Nevertheless, selection performs two important functions.

**First, selection transforms stochastic variation into something meaningful.** By choosing one variation from among three, the user pushes the others into the realm of the “unchosen” and elevates the selected text to the status of a “work candidate.” At this moment, the text’s formal properties—line length, rhythm, and lexical choices—become objects of attention. Selection is a performative act that transforms formal properties from “what could have been otherwise” into “this is what it was.”

**Second, selection establishes the basis for retroactive intentionality.** Selection becomes the starting point for constructing a later narrative: “This is what I intended.” The user may not possess a complete intention at the moment of selection, but afterward may experience the form as if it had corresponded to their intention from the beginning.

**→ This is the moment when selection begins to seal the possibilities of form.** The texture of possibilities narrows into a single selected text. Yet this sealing remains incomplete: the formal unity of the selected text has not yet been fully constituted.

### 3.3 Stage 3: Revision—The Constitution of Formal Unity

User A revises the selected text’s line breaks and changes one word (“dust” → “speck”). This revision is **the moment when Jakobson’s poetic function begins to operate concretely**.

When making the revision, the user attends to the text’s formal properties—sound, rhythm, and nuances of meaning. “Dust” and “speck” are semantically similar, but their phonological properties and contextual connotations differ. In making this substitution, the user considers items that stand in an equivalence relation along the axis of selection—dust, speck, powder, and so on—and judges how the choice will affect the axis of combination: the overall sound pattern and the flow of meaning throughout the poem.

This is precisely the **concrete manifestation of the poetic function** described by Jakobson. What must be noted here is that the poetic function is not a property of the final text; it is a **relational event that occurs during revision**. The poetic function did not exist at Stage 1, during model generation; at Stage 2, during selection, it existed only potentially; and it becomes concrete at Stage 3, during revision.

Revision also has the following theoretically significant characteristics:

- Revision has the character of **retroactive reconstruction**. By partially changing an already existing text, the user constitutes the formal unity of the whole “after the fact.” The user revises parts in order to make the whole appear as if it had been written with that intention from the beginning. This resembles Jacques Derrida’s logic of the “supplement”: revision appears to be added to the original, but in fact reveals that the original—the selected text—was never complete.
- Revision involves a **relocation of authorship**. The user revises in order to appropriate the first-generation output as their own creative work. The more extensive the revision, the stronger the user’s authorship becomes; at the same time, formal properties originating in the model become naturalized as if they had arisen organically from the user.

### 3.4 Stage 4: Regeneration and Comparison—The Visualization of Formal Self-Referentiality

After revising the text, User A asks the AI to regenerate it with the additional condition: “the same atmosphere but a more regular syllable count.” This request functions as a **meta-prompt**: it inputs a formal criterion—syllabic regularity acquired through the previous processes of generation, selection, and revision—as a condition for the next generation.

This stage has several theoretical implications.

**First, the formal criterion enters a feedback loop.** In Flusser’s model, the relation between the functionary and the apparatus was one-directional. In the repeated use of generative AI, however, the user’s selection and revision become conditions for the next generation. This recursive loop is a new phenomenon that exceeds Flusser’s model. A formal criterion consolidated through selection and revision becomes input to the apparatus again and produces new variations.

**Second, formal self-referentiality becomes visible.** When the user compares earlier versions with newly generated versions, formal properties—syllable count, line length, and sound patterns—are foregrounded for the first time. The act of comparison transforms formal properties from objects that are merely “referred to” into standards of comparison. This is the concrete condition for the “focus on the message itself” described by Jakobson. Comparison transforms the form of the message from a “transparent” medium into an opaque object.

**→ This stage is the decisive moment at which formal self-referentiality is constituted belatedly (*post festum*).** Without comparison, formal properties would remain simple properties of the text. Through comparison, form comes to exist as an object of observation.

### 3.5 Final Stage: Selection as Closure—The Determination of Formal Unity

After several rounds of regeneration and comparison, the user finalizes one version. This final selection is **the moment when formal unity is completed retroactively**.

The performative characteristics of this final selection are as follows:

- The selected text acquires the status of a “work.”
- The unselected variations are classified as “drafts” or “failed attempts.” This classification is entirely retrospective: the possibility that those variations were formally superior is not thereby eliminated.
- The process of generation is forgotten. Only the text remains, while the history of prompts, selections, revisions, and regenerations remains outside the text or is deliberately concealed.
- “Intention” is constructed retroactively. When the user explains the final text, they produce a narrative such as “What I intended was…” Yet this narrative is the result of reconstructing the contingency and unintentional discoveries of the actual generative process.

At this point, **formal self-referentiality appears as a “fixed property” of the final text**. But this fixation is the result of concealing the traces of the generative process. A critic analyzing the completed text can describe how its sounds, rhythms, and meter point toward themselves—its self-referentiality. Yet this description does not speak the truth of how the text was generated; it merely describes the surface of the result left after the process has been concealed.

## 4. The Mode of Existence of Formal Self-Referentiality: Fixed Property or Relational Effect?

On the basis of the analysis above, we can now answer the central question.

### 4.1 The View that It Is a Fixed Property of the Final Text—A Normative Illusion

Jakobson’s poetic function has traditionally been used as an analytical tool for completed poetic texts. From this perspective, formal self-referentiality is an immanent property of the text. Meter, rhyme, alliteration, and parallel syntax objectively exist in the structure of a poem and can be identified by an analyst.

Yet this perspective is **the result of forgetting the generative process**. Even in traditional poetry writing, poets produce multiple drafts, replace words, and revise line breaks. The fact that a completed poem conceals the contingency of this process and the traces of selection is an old insight of poetics. In the case of generative AI, the degree of concealment is considerably intensified. From the text alone, it is impossible to trace at which stage of the repeated process of prompting and selection a particular formal decision was made.

### 4.2 The View that It Is a Relational Effect—A Generative Approach

The position supported by this analysis is as follows:

> **Formal self-referentiality is not a fixed property of the final text, but a relational effect constituted belatedly (*post festum*) through the repeated process of prompting, selection, revision, and regeneration.**

This position rests on five arguments:

1. **Temporal division**: In generative poetry, the poetic function does not arise from a single creative act, but from a series of temporally divided events—model generation, selection, revision, and comparison. Each event activates a different aspect of formal properties.

2. **Distributed authorship**: The “subject” of the poetic function is not singular. The model’s stochastic generation, the user’s selection, the user’s revision, and the AI’s regeneration each make different “authorial contributions.” The poetic function emerges within this network of distributed authorship.

3. **Retroactive grounding**: Revision is always retroactive. By altering an already existing text, the user subsequently constitutes the formal unity of the whole. This produces the effect that the author intended the form from the beginning, but in reality intentionality is constituted at the end of the generative process.

4. **The foregrounding effect of comparison**: Comparison itself foregrounds formal properties. When differences among variations—syllable count, line length, and rhythmic patterns—become objects of comparison, those properties shift from referential function to poetic function.

5. **Rediscovery of the materiality of signs**: Jakobson’s “palpability of signs” appears differently at each stage of generative poetry. At the model-generation stage, the materiality of probabilistic distribution comes to the fore; at the selection stage, the materiality of decision; at the revision stage, the sonic materiality of vocabulary; and at the regeneration stage, the materiality of conditions. Formal self-referentiality emerges at the point where these layers of materiality overlap.

### 4.3 The Dialectic of Opening and Closure

The distinction requested by the question—between the moment when the model’s stochastic variations expand the creative possibilities of form and the moment when the user’s selection seals those possibilities as literary intention—can be clarified as follows.

**The moment of opening—model generation:**

- It occurs when the model generates unexpected metaphors, irregular rhythms, or unusual combinations of words.
- This is the moment when Flusser’s “cracks in the program” become visible. The model’s program operates according to the statistical distribution of its training data, but unpredictable variation arises at the boundaries of that distribution.
- This opening is purely contingent and is not yet identical with formal self-referentiality. However, when the user recognizes and adopts it, it exists as a **potential formal possibility**.

**The moment of closure—user selection and revision:**

- It occurs when the user selects one variation from several or revises the selected text.
- This is the moment when the field of possibilities is **reduced** to a particular form.
- Through the declaration “this is what it was,” selection closes the bundle of possibilities. Revision further refines this closed form.
- Yet this closure is never complete. A request for regeneration reopens the already closed form and introduces new possibilities. Finalization occurs when this repetition of opening and closure comes to an end.

This dialectic of opening and closure is the central dynamic of generative poetry. Even when a human writes poetry alone, opening—association and accidental discovery—and closure—selection and decision—take place. In generative AI, however, the decisive difference is that these two processes are **distributed between the human and the nonhuman model**. The model primarily performs opening, while the human primarily performs closure, but this distribution is not absolute. The user can reopen the process by rejecting earlier choices, and when the model’s output strongly imposes a particular form, the model can also perform a kind of closure.

## 5. The Order of Emergence of Authorship, Contingency, and Formal Unity, and the Media Apparatuses Involved

### 5.1 Order of Emergence

The analysis shows that authorship, contingency, and formal unity emerge in generative poetry in the following order:

1. **Introduction of contingency** (model generation): The first thing to emerge is uncontrolled stochastic variation—that is, contingency. At this stage, there is neither authorship nor formal unity.
2. **Initial establishment of retrospective authorship** (user selection): By selecting one output from the field of contingency, the user performs the first authorial act. Yet this authorship is negative—it excludes other possibilities—and has not yet become positive creation.
3. **Gradual constitution of formal unity** (revision and regeneration): Revisions to the selected text and conditional regeneration constitute formal unity. This constitution is retroactive and incremental.
4. **Completion of authorship and concealment of the process** (finalization): Final selection completes authorship while simultaneously concealing the contingency of the generative process. The completed text gives the impression of having been “intended from the beginning.”

This order is not linear. Several cycles may occur between stages 3 and 4, and each cycle reconstructs authorship and formal unity.

### 5.2 Core Media Apparatuses

The media apparatuses that make this process possible are as follows:

| Media apparatus | Function | Theoretical implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Prompt input field** | Interface through which human intention enters the apparatus’s program | Initial apparatus that conditions authorship |
| **Regenerate button** | Repeatedly generates stochastic variations under the same conditions | Makes the apparatus’s program visible; materializes the field of selectable possibilities |
| **History/version management** | Makes the processes of selection and revision traceable | Makes the process visible; creates the condition under which formal decisions become aesthetic judgments |
| **Comparison/parallel view** | Allows multiple variations to be compared simultaneously and foregrounds formal properties | Condition for the poetic function; activates orientation toward the message |
| **Editing/revision window** | Enables partial changes to the text | Apparatus of authorial appropriation; tool for making the model’s output “one’s own” |
| **Final-save function** | Brings the process to a close and fixes the text | Concealment of the generative process and emergence of the work |

These apparatuses are not merely tools. In Flusser’s sense, they are **media environments that reconstruct the conditions of authorship**. The prompt input field translates human creative intention into the language of the apparatus’s program; the regenerate button blurs the boundary between “intention” and “contingency”; and the editing window enables a ritual through which the model’s output is appropriated as the human author’s creative work.

## 6. Overall Conclusion: Toward a Genesis of Technological Form

The most important conclusion of this analysis is the following:

**In generative poetry, formal self-referentiality is not an essential property fixed in the final text, but a relational effect constituted belatedly through repeated human–apparatus interaction.** This relational effect emerges through five stages: (1) the model’s stochastic variation, or the field of contingency; (2) the user’s selection, or the initial establishment of retrospective authorship; (3) the user’s revision, or the retroactive constitution of formal unity; (4) regeneration and comparison, or the foregrounding of formal properties; and (5) finalization, or the concealment of the process and the emergence of the work.

This conclusion suggests that applying Flusser’s and Jakobson’s concepts to the environment of generative AI requires the following theoretical extensions:

**First, Flusser’s model of the “functionary” cannot capture the recursive use of generative AI.** For Flusser, the functionary merely selects within the apparatus’s program; the cycle in which that selection is re-entered as a condition for the next generation lies outside his model. Generative poetry introduces a relation of **mutual conditioning** in which the functionary’s selection conditions the next state of the apparatus. This calls for an expansion of Flusser’s one-directional theory of the apparatus into a **theory of the cyclical apparatus**.

**Second, Jakobson’s poetic function, as an analytical tool for completed messages, cannot explain the dynamics of its emergence during the generative process.** Jakobson presents the structural proposition that “the principle of equivalence is projected from the axis of selection onto the axis of combination,” but does not discuss how this projection occurs temporally, by which subject or subjects it is performed, or under what media conditions it becomes possible. Generative poetry requires an analysis of the **mediated genesis** of this projection.

**Third, authorship is no longer the exclusive property of the human subject, but neither has it been transferred entirely to the nonhuman apparatus.** In generative poetry, authorship is **distributed** between human selection, revision, and finalization and the apparatus’s stochastic generation and reflection of conditions. Yet this distribution is not symmetrical. The authority to finalize still belongs to the human, but the object and conditions of that finalization have already been structured by the apparatus. This is the dilemma of authorship in the technological generative age.
