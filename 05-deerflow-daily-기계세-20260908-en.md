## 2. Theoretical Structure of the Problem: Representation vs. Ontological Condition

The core of the question is compressed into the following options:

- **Position A (Instrumental Medium/Misrepresentation)**: The facial correction and emotion recognition of the smartphone camera are merely technical errors that distort and flatten 'real grief.' The tool is 'wrongly' representing emotion, and the meaning of mourning and conditions of visibility remain attributable to human subjective experience.
- **Position B (Machine World Reconstruction)**: The correction system 'environmentally' reconstructs the meaning of mourning and the conditions of social visibility itself. It can no longer be said that the corrected expression is 'a distortion of real grief'; instead, the 'expression corrected and made visible on the platform' itself becomes the mode of mourning's presence.

**My Judgment**: Position B is theoretically coherent but must internalize the critical tension of Position A.

## 3. Three Layers Reconstructed by the Machine World

### 3.1 Reconstruction of Meaning: The New Criterion of 'Readability' of Grief

Facial correction algorithms do not make a binary judgment of 'frowning/not frowning' but, through **continuous micro-modulation**, transform the expression to fit a 'template of grief.' In this process:

- **The angle of tears, trembling of lips, height of eyebrows** are adjusted to statistical averages.
- 'Atypical mourning' outside the standard deviation (a hybrid expression of smiling while crying, a blank void, a rigid face) is processed by the system as 'noise' and corrected, or if uncorrectable, **loses visibility itself** (pushed out of the screen or blurred).

This is not simply 'misrepresentation.' It is a structure where the **a priori condition for mourning to exist socially is established by the algorithm**. To borrow Hegelian terms, the algorithm performs 'the rational transformation of the actual'—if the reality of grief can only exist rationally (readably) through correction, correction is no longer distortion but a **condition of existence**.

### 3.2 Reconstruction of Visibility: The Power Structure of 'Visible Mourning'

The core of the Machine World is that a non-human system determines the conditions of 'what is visible.' In funeral live streaming:

- **Previously**: Mourning was made visible through physical co-presence. Attendance itself was proof of mourning.
- **Machine World**: Mourning only socially exists if it meets the platform's conditions of visibility (passing facial correction, maintaining a stable expression, sustaining 'appropriate' emotional expression). Expressions that fail to pass correction become **'invisible mourning'**.

This radicalizes Benjamin's discussion of exhibition value. In the ritual space, the issue for mourning was 'sincerity' before God and the community. In the exhibition space, the issue for mourning becomes 'readability' before the audience. The Machine World's facial correction **technically enforces** this readability—expressions can only remain on screen if they conform to the platform's emotion classification system.

### 3.3 Reconstruction of Subject Position: The Relationship between the Non-human Sensing/Correction System and Humans

This is the most important layer. The facial correction algorithm is not a tool that 'passively records' human emotion but **actively generates the outward form of emotion**.

- The subject being filmed is either unaware that their expression is being corrected, or even if aware, cannot control it.
- The system converts even the human's 'unintended expression' into 'intended grief.'
- This is a structure where **the sovereignty over human emotional expression is partially transferred to a non-human system**.

This goes beyond simple 'mediation' and is closer to an **ontological shift**. The human face is no longer the 'natural expression of inner emotion' but a **data surface** for the algorithm to read and correct. At this point, the Machine World exactly aligns with its definition: 'a civilizational shift where human-centered interpretations of emotion and ritual are restructured by non-human sensing and correction systems.'

## 4. Counterarguments and Internalization: The Validity Zone of Position A

However, the following counterarguments remain valid:

1. **Possibility of Error**: Facial correction systems still 'correct wrongly.' They might add tears to someone who isn't crying, or judge real grief as 'too excessive an expression' and flatten it into a blank look. This is evidence that the system is still in the realm of 'representational failure.'
2. **Possibility of Human Resistance**: Mourners can deliberately avoid the camera or choose lighting/angles that neutralize the correction. It is not a complete environmentalization.
3. **Continuity of Convention**: The standardization of mourning expressions existed before the algorithm. Black clothes, solemn expressions, restrained behavior—these are the 'standardization of mourning' that cultural customs have already performed. The algorithm merely technically reinforced this custom, it did not create it.

These counterarguments are important but **do not refute Position B**. Rather, they are reinterpreted as follows:

- **Error is an inherent property of the system**: The Machine World's environmentalization is not complete, and errors reveal the boundaries of the system. However, the existence of errors does not mean the system does not work. Rather, errors inversely reveal 'what the system takes as its standard.'
- **Resistance presupposes the system**: Avoiding the camera is a reaction that presupposes the system has already determined the conditions of 'being seen.' Resistance only confirms the system's existence.
- **Qualitative difference between custom and algorithm**: Cultural customs are transmitted and transformed through human conscious performance and interpretation. Algorithmic correction is based on bio-statistical models as **non-conscious, non-interpretive coercion**. These show discontinuity, not continuity.

## 5. Comprehensive Judgment: The Machine World Reconstructs, but this Reconstruction is an 'Environmental Condition,' not a 'Total Determination'

Returning to the question:

**Can the non-human technical subjects of the Machine World be seen as reconstructing the meaning of mourning and the conditions of social visibility?**

→ **Yes. However, this reconstruction should not be grasped within the epistemological framework of 'distortion vs. truth,' but within the ontological framework of 'establishment of conditions.'**

The facial correction algorithm is not a tool that 'misrepresents' human emotion. It is an **environment that (partially) establishes the very conditions under which mourning can exist in a platform society**. The corrected expression is not 'fake grief' but 'grief under the conditions of Machine World visibility.' This is not an 'error' to be corrected with a better tool, but a symptom of the following **ontological shift**:

> _Grief is no longer a relationship between 'what is felt' and 'what is expressed,' but becomes a datal event occurring within the cycle of 'sensing-correction-exhibition.'_

Simultaneously, this reconstruction is not a total determination but an **environmental condition (Umwelt)** . User resistance, system errors, and clashes with cultural customs enable various deviations and variations within this environment. This is why the Machine World should be conceptualized as an 'environment,' not a 'totalitarian system.'

**Core Tension**: The Machine World enables the visibility of mourning (inclusion) but flattens its complexity by standardizing the conditions of that visibility (exclusion). The non-human subject of the Machine World performs a **dual environmentalization that simultaneously expands and contracts the conditions of visibility**. Within this tension, the question 'what is true mourning' no longer remains in the metaphysical realm but becomes an issue of **critical practice** under specific technical conditions.

## 6. Theoretical Implications and Future Tasks

The theoretical tasks revealed by the above analysis can be summarized in three points:

1. **How far does the Machine World's environmentalization reach?**: This case (funeral live streaming) corresponds to a 'weak' version of environmentalization. Comparison with 'strong' environmentalization cases, such as autonomous vehicle accidents, AI medical diagnosis, and automated trials, is necessary.
2. **The relationship between the environmental reconstruction of emotion and the commodification of emotion**: Is the Adorno-Benjamin critique of the culture industry a valid tool for analyzing the Machine World, or does the Machine World demand a completely different analytical framework? (This is directly connected to the potential link with Document 4.)
3. **The possibility of constructing an 'alternative environment' to non-human sensing/correction systems**: Given the environmental conditions of the Machine World, is it possible to technically design other kinds of conditions (acceptance of slowness, deficiency, noise)?

These questions suggest that the Machine World is not simply an object of critique but **an inquiry into conditions and a task for design**. The Machine World is an environment we must live within, and understanding its conditions is the first step in rewriting the politics of mourning.
