### 2. The Explanatory Power and Limitations of the Romantic Theory of Authorship

The Romantic theory of authorship is a modern concept established from the late 18th through the 19th centuries. This theory posits that the author recreates the world through original expression emanating from their inner self. Therefore, the work is considered the total outcome of the author's personality, intention, and the physical and mental labor invested in the creative process. From this perspective, the author is the absolute origin of the text and the final arbiter of meaning.

However, the fact that AI intervened in translation and sentence polishing in the 2024 domestic publishing case directly violates the core premise of this theory. Translation is not merely a linguistic conversion but a creative act of interpreting and reconstructing the meaning of the source text. The translation performed by AI is an output based on statistical patterns in the training data, independent of the human writer's intention. Similarly, sentence polishing, under the pretext of "improving" the writer's unique style and expressive intention, transforms the text according to the probabilistic predictions of the AI model. In this case, the human writer's "labor" is reduced to drafting the initial manuscript and then reviewing and approving the AI-generated output. "Intention" becomes limited to designing prompts and finalizing the proofread version.

In this situation, the Romantic theory of authorship can respond in two ways. First, by considering AI as a mere "tool." In this view, AI is no different from a past pencil or typewriter, and all creative intention still belongs to the human writer. However, this seriously misunderstands how AI works. A pencil is a passive medium that simply transfers the writer's intention to paper, whereas generative AI is closer to an active subject that "generates" new text based on training data. The translation produced by AI is not a simple substitute for the source text but an interpretation and transformation of it. Second, one can take the human writer's "final approval" as the basis for authorship. That is, regardless of how much text an AI generates, the final decision to confirm it as a publication lies with the human, so the author is still human.

Yet such responses dramatically reveal a problem that the traditional Romantic theory of authorship had already concealed. In fact, human editors have long been deeply involved in shaping the meaning of a work by revising manuscripts, rearranging structures, and polishing style. Publishing platforms have also determined the reception of works through distribution strategies and marketing. Nevertheless, the Romantic theory of authorship has relegated this collaborative creative process to a "supportive role" and highlighted only the author's genius. The intervention of generative AI makes this hidden collaborative creation structure visible. In other words, from the very beginning, the author was not really a "single creative subject" but a network-like entity involving the human writer, editor, publishing platform, and now also the AI model and training data. By adding a new node to this network, AI exposes the fictionality of the Romantic theory of authorship.

### 3. Editorial/Mediation Theory and Platform Studies: Conditions for Distributed Authorship

To supplement what the Romantic theory of authorship fails to explain, editorial/mediation theory and platform studies offer useful alternative perspectives. According to editorial/mediation theory, the editor is not a mere proofreader but an active mediator who intermediates and reconstructs meaning between the author and the reader. One document from the Khoj references notes that with the emergence of generative AI, "reading" is being replaced by "editing." This means that as AI performs the primary knowledge structuring of reading and writing on behalf of the human subject, humans move to a position of "meta-author" who edits and restructures the AI-generated outputs.

From this perspective, the human writer in the 2024 domestic publishing case is no longer a direct producer of the text but a hybrid subject—an "editor-author"—who reviews the translation and polishing results produced by AI, corrects errors, and rearranges them according to the overall narrative structure. This does not simply mean that the author's role has diminished; it shows that the nature of authorship itself has changed. The author has become a subject who "selects and arranges" rather than a subject who "writes."

Platform studies explain this change even more structurally. According to the Khoj references, digital publishing platforms collect metadata such as user reviews, bookmarks, highlights, and sharing records, which are reflected in real time in strategies for restructuring and redistributing content. The emergence of generative AI accelerates this role mixing. Users do not simply consume existing content; they become integrated creator-editor-distributors, producing new content or transforming existing materials using AI. Furthermore, the AI player acts as a complex mediator connecting technology and context between algorithms and platforms. By repeating the processes of questioning, editing, curation, and publishing, the AI player designs their own knowledge structures, understands the platform's operational principles and algorithmic response patterns, and controls them.

Applying this to the 2024 case, the human writer performs the role of an "AI player" who understands the response patterns of the AI model, designs appropriate prompts, and adjusts the AI-generated output to fit the platform's distribution strategy. At this point, authorship is no longer an attribute of a single individual but the product of a "distributed network" in which the human writer's prompt design ability, the AI model's training data and generative algorithm, the publishing platform's metadata collection and distribution strategy, and the editor's coordinating ability all act in combination. Therefore, this case creates severe tension with existing theories that define the author as a single creative subject. While traditional theory asks the singular attribution question "Who wrote this?", this case demands a processual question: "How is meaning produced and circulated?"

### 4. Simondon's Concept of Concretization and Model Collapse: The Crossroads of Evolution and Degeneration

To understand the technical nature of generative AI, Simondon's concept of concretization is useful. According to Simondon, technical objects evolve through a process of progressive structuring that satisfies the demands of both the internal and external environments. That is, a technical object begins in an abstract form and develops into a more concrete and integrated structure as various constraints and functional requirements combine. Generative AI is a typical example of this concretization process. An AI model is concretized into a technical object with specific generative capabilities through the interaction of vast training data (external environment) and neural network architecture (internal environment). When a human writer uses AI for translation or polishing, they inject a new occasion for individuation into this concretized technical object. The human's prompt acts as an external occasion that activates the AI model's potential generative capacity in a specific direction.

However, the concretization process of generative AI does not guarantee pure evolution alone. Here, the problems of the synthetic data feedback loop and model collapse arise. Let us assume that AI-generated translations or polished sentences are fed back into the training data. The AI model retrains on its own outputs, and in this process, the tails of the original data distribution—that is, unique expressions or stylistic features that appear less frequently—gradually disappear. Eventually, the model, by repeatedly learning its own outputs, loses diversity and reaches a state of "model collapse," generating only average, homogenized text.

At this point, we can derive two contrasting interpretations. The first is an evolutionary interpretation of the technical object. According to this view, generative AI is concretized as a "technical object" that creates new literary forms and expressions through collaboration with humans. The human writer's prompt design and editorial intervention inject creative variation into the concretization process of the AI model, acting as an evolutionary occasion that expands the horizon of literary expression. If the translation and polishing performed by AI in the 2024 domestic case created new styles that existing human translators or editors had not attempted, this could support the evolutionary interpretation.

The second is an interpretation of a degenerative closed loop. This view warns that as the feedback loop of AI-generated data re-entering the training data repeats, literary diversity decreases, eventually leading to model collapse. This signifies not the concretization of a technical object but rather a degenerative closure of the creative ecosystem. If AI-generated translations and polished sentences are finalized as publications, and these publications are collected again as AI training data, the next generation of AI models will retrain on the statistical patterns of text generated by the previous generation of AI. If this process repeats, literature will gradually lose original expression and be filled only with average, safe sentences generated by AI. This presages an ontological crisis of literature that goes beyond the problem of authorship.

Since the 2024 domestic case is still only a partial intervention, it is difficult to determine which interpretation—evolutionary or degenerative—predominates. However, what this case demonstrates is that the use of generative AI is not confined to the question "Who is the author?" but extends to the more macro issue of "the sustainability of the creative ecosystem." Simondon's concept of concretization explains the evolution of technical objects, but the synthetic data feedback loop shows that this evolution entails an inherent risk of turning into degeneration. Therefore, the problem of authorship can be fully understood only when considering both the concretization process of technical objects and the directionality of their feedback loop.

### 5. Implications from Eco's Research on Digital and Artificial Nature

Umberto Eco's research provides theoretical resources to understand the problem of authorship in the age of generative AI from the dimension of changes in the ontological conditions of literature. Through the concept of the "open work," Eco argued that the meaning of a work is not fixed by the author's intention but is completed by the interpretation of the receiver. From this perspective, the text is inherently open, and the reader is an active participant in constructing the work's meaning. Generative AI radically extends this condition of "openness." Because AI does not fix the meaning of a text but can infinitely generate new, varied texts according to the probabilistic distribution of its training data.

However, Eco's concept of "artificial nature" suggests that this openness simultaneously harbors the risk of closure. Artificial nature refers to the phenomenon where a technical environment created by humans operates as a second nature. Generative AI is an extreme form of artificial nature. AI simulates the human creative process and presents its outputs as if they were natural literary expressions. Yet the texts AI generates are actually only products of statistical patterns, and those patterns are determined by the scope and quality of the training data. Therefore, the "naturalness" produced by AI is not actual nature but an artificially constructed second nature.

From this perspective of artificial nature, the 2024 domestic publishing case shows that the conditions of literary creation have been fundamentally restructured. The author is no longer a being who creates texts through natural inspiration and personal labor, but becomes a "technical mediator" who collaborates with an AI model operating as part of artificial nature, edits its outputs, and adjusts them to the platform's distribution structure. This shifts the problem of authorship from the attribution question "Who wrote it?" to the process question "How is meaning produced and circulated?"

The implications of Eco's research for this issue are as follows. First, authorship is no longer a fixed attribute based on individual originality but a product of a network in which humans and technology are intertwined. Therefore, literary theory must move beyond the Romantic paradigm that defines the author as a single creative subject and build a new conceptual framework that can explain distributed authorship, networked creation, and the complexity of technical mediation. Second, the artificial nature created by generative AI expands the "openness" of literature while simultaneously harboring the risk of "closing" literary diversity through model collapse. Thus, literary theory requires a dialectical approach that considers both the optimistic perspective of technical evolution and the pessimistic perspective of degenerative closure. Third, all these discussions demand that literature rethink the relationship between humans, technology, and nature. Generative AI should no longer be regarded as a simple auxiliary tool for human creation but as a key element that restructures the ontological conditions of literature.
