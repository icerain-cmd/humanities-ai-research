## Slow Information and Sensory Ethics: Eco-Digital's Response to the Affective Machine of Platform Capitalism

### 1. Diagnosis: The Affect-Reactivity Machine of Platform Capitalism

Platform capitalism is not simply a data extraction economy. It is an **infrastructure that reshapes the temporality of affect itself**. While Srnicek (2017) defined 'platform capitalism' as the commodification of data as raw material, at a more fundamental level, the platform is a **media environment that controls the speed and direction of affect circulation**.

As Kittler's media theory points out, media do not merely transmit content; they constitute **the very conditions of perception** (media materiality). The core power exercised by the platform is **control over reaction time** — that is, the infrastructural conditions that determine what, when, and how quickly users perceive and respond.

This is the structure of the **'demand for immediacy of affect'**:
- **Notification systems (scroll/notification)**: Fragment the temporality of affect through uninterrupted attention capture.
- **Infinite scroll/short-form feeds**: Compress reaction time to milliseconds, shortening the cycle of perception-judgment-action.
- **Affective economy of like/share buttons**: Reduce complex emotions to simple binary reactions.

In this structure, the user functions not as a 'knowing subject' but as a **response node**. Affect is already coded before it flows, and the platform architecture pushes the circulation time of this coded affect to its extreme limit.

### 2. The Response Structure of Slow Information and Sensory Ethics

The Eco-Digital framework's Slow Information and Sensory Ethics respond to this affective machine along two strategic axes.

**(a) Slow Information: Reappropriating Temporality**

Slow Information is not simply 'reading slowly.' It is a **deliberate disruption of the speed of affect flow**. It operates through three practical mechanisms:

1. **Introduction of Wait Time**: By inserting intentional delays between the information consumption-response cycle, it neutralizes the platform's command to 'respond now, immediately.'

2. **Recovery of Sensory Literacy**: Just as Kittler focused on the 'material conditions' of media, Slow Information restores sensory awareness of the material foundations of digital information (pixels, sampling rates, data packets) — resisting the 'transparency' of information.

3. **Reintroduction of Context**: Against fragmented information flows, it repositions information within biographical, social, and ecological contexts, restoring the narrative depth of affect.

**(b) Sensory Ethics: The Politics of Affect**

Sensory Ethics extends the issue of individual sensation to a **common sensory condition (sensus communis)**. The core thesis is: **the way we feel constitutes the world we inhabit**.

1. **Judgment on the Quality of Affect**: Rather than the 'binary affect of pleasure-displeasure' demanded by the platform, it re-evaluates more complex sensory judgments — the value of 'inefficient' affects such as surprise, discomfort, ambiguity, and boredom.

2. **Sensory-Ecological Connection**: It recognizes that individual sensory experience is conditioned by specific perceptual infrastructures (algorithms, interfaces, databases) and transforms this recognition into ethical practices that alter these conditions. This connects to the concept of 'environmentality' in Artificial Nature theory — the user-platform relationship is not an interaction between objects but an environment-organism relationship.

3. **Media Discernment**: Not simply blocking information (digital detox), but the ability to **simultaneously regulate the temporality and materiality of perceived information**. Examples: adjusting notification settings, managing information contact time, blocking information during specific periods — but this does not stop at the individual level.

### 3. From Individual Sensory Management to Collective Infrastructural Intervention: Three Conditions

The critical question is whether Slow Information and Sensory Ethics can avoid being trapped in the neoliberal framework of **individual self-optimization** and instead lead to collective interventions that change the platform's operating conditions themselves.

Here, I propose three conditions for this transition:

**(1) Formation of a Common Infrastructural Sensibility**

When individual practices of Sensory Ethics are shared, the way platform temporal structures are experienced becomes collectively recognized. This goes beyond sharing a diagnosis (e.g., "This app intentionally shortens my reaction time") to a process where **common sensory patterns are elevated to social facts**. This connects to Latour's concept of the 'collective' and signifies that resistance to a specific media environment transforms into an awareness of common sensory conditions.

**(2) An Alternative Model for the Affective Economy of Slow Information**

For Slow Information to become not just an 'individual choice' but an **infrastructure for an alternative affective economy**:
- Introduce response speed limitation mechanisms in public conversation platforms.
- Institutionalize the 'right to think time' as a user right.
- Enable collective monitoring of information consumption patterns and negotiable platform design.

An interesting example is **reading groups/slow reading circles** — not just hobby gatherings, but collective sensory practices where participants read the same text at the same pace and share the experience. This demonstrates how individual slow reading expands into a collective perceptual condition through sensorial simultaneity.

**(3) Legal/Political Intervention in Platform Temporality**

The collectivization of Slow Information can ultimately lead to political demands that **make the temporality demanded by platforms itself a subject of regulation**:
- Regulation of infinite scroll and autoplay (in the context of the European Digital Services Act).
- A 'right to digital latency' — the right not to respond immediately.
- Demands for transparency of algorithmic temporality — the right to know the temporal patterns in which information is arranged.

### 4. Repositioning within the Eco-Digital Framework

The theoretical depth of this discussion is supported by each of the four axes of Eco-Digital:

| Axis | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Kittler (Media Materiality)** | Understands the platform as a transcendental condition of perception — analyzes the operating conditions of the affective machine. |
| **Artificial Nature** | Understands the platform as a 'naturalized artificial environment' — the mutual constitution of environmentality and the user. |
| **Media Aesthetics** | The conditions of sensory judgment and the political dimension of Sensory Ethics. |
| **Eco-Digital** | Positions Slow Information as an ecological practice within the cyclical relationship of individual-environment-infrastructure. |

In conclusion: For the practice of Slow Information to move beyond mere **individual sensory management** and become a **collective infrastructural intervention**, the key is to create **conditions under which sensory commonality can emerge as a political actor**. This is not a simple refusal of the immediacy of affect, but a **positive practice of collectively forging a different temporality** — and that collective potential is the core practical task of the Eco-Digital framework.
