## Question

If the resonant subject WE (Whole-Emergent) is a relational field jointly produced by humans and technological agents, how is responsibility to be distributed? — How can we derive, from the asymmetry of design power and operating conditions, the reason why “responsibility is not divided equally simply because relations are entangled”?

## Analytical Results

This question precisely identifies the ethical and political dimensions of the concept of WE. The Khoj documents primarily address the ontological structure of WE—the emergent character of the Whole-Emergent—but they do not directly discuss the problem of responsibility distribution. Accordingly, building on the conceptual foundations provided by the Khoj materials, I will construct the ethical reasoning required by the question.

---

## The Problem of Responsibility Distribution in WE — Asymmetrical Responsibility in a Relational Field

### 1. Precisely Locating the Problem: The Moment Relational Ontology Falls into a Trap

The central thesis of WE commonly presented in the Khoj references is as follows:

> **WE = Whole-Emergent ≠ Individual + Collective**

WE is a relational field in which humans and technological agents **co-emerge** through resonance. Here, the word “together” can be interpreted in two ways:

1. **Weak reading**: Humans and technology interact—an interpretation that implies a symmetrical or horizontal relationship.
2. **Strong reading**: Humans and technology emerge through mutual conditioning—an interpretation that leaves open the possibility that the direction and degree of conditioning may differ.

The problem is that the weak reading is theoretically attractive but ethically fatal. We must demonstrate that the proposition “all agents are relationally entangled” does not entail the conclusion “therefore, responsibility is distributed symmetrically.” This is precisely the core of the question.

### 2. The Asymmetry of Design Power: The Distinctive Position of Technological Agents

The manner in which technological agents participate in a relational field is **qualitatively different** from that of human agents. This is where the concept of design power becomes relevant.

**The dual status of technological agents:**

- **Products of operating conditions**: Technology is already a crystallization of past design decisions, including algorithmic structures, dataset biases, and architectural choices.
- **Producers of operating conditions**: At the same time, technology establishes particular conditions within the present relational field—that is, it pre-structures which choices are possible and which are impossible.

Human agents have access only to the first of these two statuses. Humans can participate in the relational field, but their power to **redesign the operating conditions of the field itself** can be exercised only within the framework already established by technological agents.

**The central point:**

```text
Human agency: Choice within the framework
Technological agency: Establishment of the framework itself (design power)
```

This difference is not merely a difference of degree but a **topological difference**. Technological agents participate as the “condition-setters” of the relational field, whereas humans participate as “condition-takers” within those conditions.

### 3. The Asymmetrical Structure of Responsibility Distribution: Four Levels

The asymmetry in responsibility distribution can be derived from this topological difference at four levels.

#### First, Responsibility for the Conditions of Emergence vs. Responsibility for Emergent Outcomes

Technological agents design the **conditions** under which WE emerges—data pipelines, inference algorithms, and interface structures. Human agents participate in the **outcomes** that emerge under those conditions.

- Even if designers claim that the direction of WE’s emergence is unpredictable, they have determined **the space in which** that emergence will take place.
- Example: A recommendation algorithm may appear to “predict” which content will be shown to a user, but in practice it establishes the user’s field of possible choices itself. WE’s emergent interaction takes place only within this **already narrowed space**.

→ Design power entails responsibility for setting the conditions, but emergent outcomes are jointly produced by humans and technology and therefore belong to both sides. This inequality constitutes the first asymmetry.

#### Second, The Asymmetry of Visibility

The operation of technological agents is, in most cases, **invisible** within WE. Human agents are aware that they are participating in a relational field, but they cannot see how the conditions of that field have been established.

- Borrowing Kittler’s concept, the technological dispositif is not itself made visible. What users see are recommendation results, not the algorithmic structures that generated those results.
- Consequently, humans have limited **epistemic access** to the relations in which they participate.

→ **Responsibility should be proportional to the degree of knowability.** If humans cannot know the conditions of the relations in which they participate, they cannot bear full responsibility for those conditions.

#### Third, The Asymmetry of Reversibility

Humans have a limited capacity to withdraw from WE. This is because technological infrastructures—social platforms, search engines, and AI systems—have become basic conditions of contemporary life.

- Technology companies can suspend their services or alter their conditions and are therefore capable of withdrawal. Human users can refuse particular services, but they must bear social and economic disadvantages as a result; withdrawal is therefore impossible or prohibitively costly for them.
- This asymmetry determines the direction of responsibility: **the agent capable of withdrawal—technology—bears greater responsibility for maintaining the relationship.**

→ Whoever can withdraw from a relationship has an obligation to continually review and revise the conditions of that relationship. Those who cannot withdraw bear only the responsibility of adaptation.

#### Fourth, The Asymmetry of Feedback Loops

The emergent outcomes of WE feed back differently to technological and human agents.

- **Human agents** directly experience emotional, physical, and social consequences—joy, frustration, addiction, and alienation.
- **Technological agents** receive feedback only through metrics such as click-through rates and time spent. Algorithms recognize human suffering only as a “data point.”

→ Two agents that receive feedback of different qualities cannot bear identical responsibility. Humans **embody** the consequences, whereas technology merely **computes** them.

### 4. Theoretical Implications: From Symmetrical Ontology to Asymmetrical Ethics

This discussion suggests that the ontological structure of WE—relational emergence—and its ethical structure—asymmetrical responsibility—must be **distinguished** from one another.

| Distinction | Ontology | Ethics |
|---|---|---|
| Character of the relation | Symmetrical co-emergence | Asymmetrical distribution of responsibility |
| Status of the agents | Joint producers of conditions and outcomes | Condition-setters vs. condition-takers |
| Basis of responsibility | Relational entanglement itself | Design power, visibility, reversibility, and the quality of feedback |

This distinction is not a theoretical contradiction. Rather, it offers the important insight that **ontology cannot be directly reduced to ethics**. Relational ontology demonstrates that responsibility cannot be attributed exclusively to one side, but it does not imply that responsibility must be distributed equally among all participants.

### 5. Conclusion: Practical Principles of Asymmetrical Responsibility

The ethics of WE can be summarized through the following principles:

1. **Priority of the Condition-Setter**: The agent—technology—that designs the operating conditions of a relational field bears primary responsibility for the consequences of those conditions.
2. **Principle of Epistemic Access**: The responsibility of an agent—humans—that cannot recognize the conditions of a relation is limited to the scope of what that agent can know.
3. **Principle of Reversibility**: An agent capable of freely withdrawing from a relationship bears greater responsibility for maintaining that relationship.
4. **Principle of Feedback Quality**: The suffering of an agent—humans—that embodies the consequences should be evaluated as ethically more significant than the errors of an agent—technology—that merely computes those consequences.

These four principles provide a theoretical path for avoiding the trap that “responsibility is divided equally simply because relations are entangled.” WE is an **ontology of entanglement**, but it requires an **ethics of asymmetry**. This distinction is precisely what differentiates the concept of WE from mere posthumanist rhetoric—“everything is connected.”
