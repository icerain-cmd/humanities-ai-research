# Media Aesthetics in the Age of Technological Generation: A Study on the Ontological Status of AI-Restored Family Photos

## 1. Introduction: Concealment of Flaws and Reconstruction of Memory

The phenomenon of family photos retouched by smartphone one-click AI restoration and colorization apps circulating on Instagram raises fundamental questions about the ontological status of memory in the digital age, beyond mere technical convenience. These images claim to 'restore' the material traces of the past, but in reality, they algorithmically 'fill in' the original's flaws, thereby generating a new visual entity. This study conceptually examines whether such images represent a restoration of Walter Benjamin's aura or a new past produced by the recording technologies and apparatus analyzed by Friedrich Kittler and Vilém Flusser. Furthermore, it connects Gilbert Simondon's concept of concretization with the synthetic data feedback loop of generative AI, comparing two interpretations: the evolution of technical objects and regressive closed loops.

## 2. Benjamin's Aura and Technical Reproduction: The Paradox of Restored Images

### 2.1 Conditions and Dissolution of Aura

In "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction," Benjamin defines aura as "the unique phenomenon of a distance, however close it may be." Aura is based on the 'authenticity' and 'authority' that a work of art acquires by existing in a specific place and time. Technical reproduction media like photography and film destroy this aura because reproductions are detached from the original's unique spatiotemporal context and mass-distributed.

However, Benjamin did not view the dissolution of aura as a mere loss. Rather, technical reproduction transforms art's 'cult value' into 'exhibition value,' creating democratic conditions for mass access to art. Photography preserves the physical traces of the original while making the image infinitely reproducible. At this point, photography possesses the paradoxical characteristic of 'the aura of the dead.' As Benjamin noted, "in photography, we encounter ourselves in an unfamiliar way," photography preserves a moment of the past but simultaneously makes the 'here and now' of that moment eternally lost.

### 2.2 Attempts to Restore Aura in Restored Images

The act of AI restoration apps filling in flaws in family photos may superficially appear as an attempt to restore aura. If the original photo has been damaged over time, and those damaged areas are traces revealing the photo's 'historicity,' AI restoration seeks to remove these historical traces and return the photo to its 'original state.' This could be interpreted as an attempt to recreate the 'unique phenomenon' of the moment the photo was taken.

However, this interpretation contains a fundamental misunderstanding. Benjamin's aura is based not on mere visual completeness but on the 'uniqueness' that the object possesses within a specific historical context. AI-restored images fill in the original's flaws, but the process of filling relies entirely on statistical probability and algorithmic inference. In other words, the restored areas are not 'traces' of the original but 'averages' of the training data. This advances the logic of Benjamin's 'technical reproduction' one step further: reproduction now not only copies the original but also 'generates' the original's 'flaws.'

## 3. Kittler's Media Theory of Recording: Material Traces and Technical Conditions

### 3.1 Materiality and Selective Storage of Recording Media

In "Gramophone, Film, Typewriter," Kittler analyzes that recording media are not neutral tools reflecting reality but 'selective systems' that can only store information within specific material conditions and technical limitations. The gramophone records only within the frequency range of sound, film only within the visible light spectrum, and the typewriter only within the alphabet writing system. Each medium stores only information that meets its technical conditions and systematically excludes other information.

In the case of photography, analog photography records optical information of reality through the chemical changes of silver halide crystals that react to light. In this process, photography possesses an 'indexical' characteristic: the image in the photo is a physical trace left by the light at the time of shooting. Roland Barthes' "ça-a-été" (that-has-been) in "Camera Lucida" is based on this indexical characteristic. Photography proves that "the object was actually there, and the light emitted from that object reached my eyes."

### 3.2 Transformation of Recording Status in Digital Restoration

However, the moment an AI restoration app intervenes, this indexical relationship is fundamentally transformed. Digital photography, unlike analog photography, is already converted into discrete data in pixel units, and in this process, the continuous optical information of the original is quantized. AI restoration goes further by 'generating' the missing parts, adding information beyond the material traces of the original.

From Kittler's perspective, AI-restored images are no longer the result of 'recording' but of 'synthesis.' While recording media preserve traces of reality, synthetic media generate information that never existed in reality. This signifies a transformation of what Kittler called 'recording systems' (Aufschreibesysteme). If the recording systems around 1900 recorded sound, image, and text separately through three media—gramophone, film, and typewriter—the AI restoration technology of the 2020s forms a meta-recording system that 'learns' from this recorded data to generate new data.

## 4. Flusser's Concept of Apparatus: Programmed Possibilities

### 4.1 Dialectics of Apparatus and Program

In "Towards a Philosophy of Photography," Flusser analyzes photography as a product of 'apparatus' and 'program.' An apparatus is not a simple machine but a 'black box' designed to perform specific functions, and a program is the set of all possible operations the apparatus can perform. The photographer manipulates the apparatus, but the scope of manipulation is predetermined by the apparatus's program. In other words, the photographer can only exercise creativity within the limits allowed by the apparatus.

Flusser explains this apparatus-program relationship as 'functional interdependence.' The apparatus is a tool for realizing human intentions, but at the same time, it subordinates human actions to the logic of the apparatus. Just as a photographer can adjust the aperture and shutter speed of a camera, but only within the technical limits of the camera, the user of an AI restoration app can only choose within the 'restoration' options provided by the app.

### 4.2 Programmed Selectivity of AI Restoration Apps

AI restoration apps radicalize Flusser's concept of apparatus. These apps provide users with a simple 'one-click' interface, but internally, a neural network trained on millions of photos operates. The moment the user clicks the 'restore' button, the app performs a series of programmed operations:

1. **Flaw Detection**: Identifies damaged areas in the image.
2. **Context Analysis**: Analyzes pixels, colors, textures, and patterns around the flawed area.
3. **Generation Model Application**: Generates the most probable pixel values to fill the flawed area based on learned data.
4. **Consistency Verification**: Checks if the generated area maintains visual consistency with the surrounding area.

In this process, the user only issues the command 'restore' and has no decision-making power over the specific restoration method. In Flusser's terms, the user degenerates into a 'functionary' of the apparatus. The user believes they are using the apparatus, but in reality, the apparatus's program pre-structures the user's choices.

More importantly, while AI restoration apps claim to recreate 'the appearance of the past,' that 'past' is merely a statistical average constructed by the app's training data. When the app claims to restore 'original colors,' those colors do not reflect the chemical state of the original photo or the lighting conditions at the time of shooting but are the result of applying the most frequently occurring color combinations in the training data. This radicalizes the characteristic of what Flusser called 'technical images': technical images no longer refer to reality but to other images (training data).

## 5. Foucault's Archive: Rules Determining What Can Be Remembered

### 5.1 Power and Selective Preservation of the Archive

In "The Archaeology of Knowledge," Michel Foucault defines the Archive as "the system of all statements that can be uttered in a given period." The Archive is not a simple document repository but a set of rules that determine "what can be said and what can be remembered." These rules are not visible but structure the discourse of a particular era and enable the formation of specific subjects and knowledge.

Applying Foucault's concept of the Archive to the issue of AI restoration of family photos raises the following question: "Which flaws are deemed worthy of restoration, and which flaws are ignored?" AI restoration apps do not treat all flaws equally. Facial flaws are prioritized for restoration, while background flaws are treated as relatively less important. This means the app's algorithm embeds specific value judgments about 'what is important.'

### 5.2 Transformation of the Family Photo Archive

AI-restored family photos circulating on Instagram demonstrate the transformation of what Foucault called the Archive. The traditional family photo archive consisted of printed photos stored in physical albums, and their preservation and viewing depended on family members' physical access. The circulation of family photos on digital platforms fundamentally changes the nature of this archive:

1. **Selective Visibility**: Not all family photos are digitized; only those meeting specific emotional value or aesthetic standards are selected for upload.
2. **Algorithmic Curation**: The platform's algorithm exposes certain photos more and others less.
3. **Participatory Archiving**: Not only family members but also friends, acquaintances, and even strangers participate in constructing the archive through comments, likes, and shares.

AI-restored images are at the core of this archival transformation. By presenting a 'better past,' restored images conceal the 'incomplete past' implied by the original's flaws. This is a digital implementation of what Foucault called the 'politics of memory,' where technical devices determine which past is worth remembering.

## 6. Simondon's Concept of Concretization and the Feedback Loop of Generative AI

### 6.1 Concretization and Evolution of Technical Objects

In "On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects," Gilbert Simondon argues that technical objects are not mere tools but 'concretized' beings with internal consistency. Technical objects initially consist of abstract, separate components, but through development, they undergo 'concretization' where each component becomes functionally integrated. For example, early automobiles were abstract objects with separate engines, transmissions, and bodies, but through development, they evolved into concrete objects where each part complements and optimizes the functions of others.

Applying Simondon's concept of concretization to AI restoration technology, this technology can be seen not as a simple image processing tool but as a concrete technical object where various technical elements (neural networks, training data, user interfaces, cloud computing) are integrated. However, Simondon noted that the evolution of technical objects is not always positive. Technical objects can fall into 'over-concretization,' leading to system closure and loss of adaptability.

### 6.2 Synthetic Data Feedback Loop of Generative AI

The synthetic data feedback loop of generative AI illuminates Simondon's concept of concretization in a new way. The cyclical process where an AI model generates new images based on learned data, and these generated images are then used as training data, can be seen as 'auto-concretization' of the technical object. However, this process entails the following risks:

1. **Data Homogenization**: When AI-generated images re-enter the training data, data diversity decreases, and specific patterns are amplified.
2. **Model Collapse**: Repeated training on synthetic data degrades model performance and gradually reduces the quality of generated images.
3. **Loss of Creativity**: By learning from its own outputs, the model loses the ability to discover new patterns and becomes trapped in repeating existing patterns.

In the case of AI restoration apps, this feedback loop works as follows: Restored family photos circulate on Instagram, and these images are collected through web crawling as training data for AI. The updated AI then learns these synthetic images as patterns of 'real' family photos and applies these patterns in subsequent restoration tasks. This cycle gradually reduces the diversity of original family photos and reinforces a standardized image of 'what AI thinks a typical family photo looks like.'

## 7. Evolution and Regressive Closed Loop of Technical Objects: A Comparison of Two Interpretations

### 7.1 Evolutionary Interpretation: Creative Concretization of Technical Objects

The first interpretation views AI restoration technology as the creative evolution of technical objects. From this perspective, AI restoration has the following positive aspects:

1. **Expansion of Memory Preservation**: By restoring damaged photos, it preserves family memories that might have been lost.
2. **New Aesthetic Experience**: It provides an opportunity to experience the past anew through clear images and colors that the original could not offer.
3. **Participatory Memory Formation**: Family members participate in the collective construction of memory by jointly restoring and sharing photos.

This interpretation positively applies Simondon's concept of concretization. AI restoration technology evolves from a simple image processing tool to a concrete technical object that becomes increasingly sophisticated through user feedback and data learning. In this process, technology develops beyond a tool for reproducing the past into a medium that creates new relationships between the past and the present.

### 7.2 Regressive Interpretation: Closed Loop and Model Collapse

The second interpretation views AI restoration technology as a regressive closed loop. This perspective points out the following problems:

1. **Standardization of Memory**: AI-generated 'typical' family images replace actual family memories.
2. **Loss of Meaning in Flaws**: The historical meaning of photo flaws (the passage of time, material aging) is eliminated.
3. **Crisis of Authenticity**: As restored images become indistinguishable from originals, the distinction between 'real' and 'fake' becomes meaningless.

This interpretation emphasizes the risk of model collapse. If AI restoration apps become trapped in a synthetic data feedback loop, they will gradually restore all family photos in the same style. This is 'over-concretization' of the technical object, where the system loses the ability to adapt to changes in the external environment and becomes trapped in a self-reproducing closed loop.

### 7.3 Dialectical Synthesis of the Two Interpretations

The two interpretations are not mutually exclusive but form a dialectical relationship. AI restoration technology simultaneously contains the potential for both evolution and regression. The direction of technological development is determined not only by the internal logic of the technology itself but also by the social, cultural, and economic conditions surrounding it.

Importantly, AI-restored images are not simply 'reproductions of the past' but 'generations of the past.' These images fill in the original's flaws, but the filling always occurs under specific technical and cultural conditions. Therefore, AI-restored images should be understood as neither Benjamin's restoration of aura nor Kittler's new past produced by recording technologies, but as a dialectical synthesis of the two.

4. **Power of the Archive**: Foucault's concept of the Archive reveals that AI restoration technology is a power apparatus that determines 'which past is worth remembering.'

5. **Dialectics of Technical Objects**: Simondon's concept of concretization and the feedback loop of generative AI show that AI restoration technology simultaneously contains the potential for both evolution and regression.

From the perspective of eco-digital/artificial nature research, AI-restored family photos can be understood as an example of 'artificial nature.' They are hybrid beings combining natural processes (the aging of photos over time) and artificial processes (restoration by AI). These images are no longer purely natural or purely artificial but form a 'third nature' that crosses the boundary between the two.

In conclusion, AI-restored family photos are neither Benjamin's restoration of aura nor Kittler's new past but indicators revealing new media-aesthetic conditions in the age of technological generation. Under these conditions, 'the past' is no longer a fixed entity but a processual being continuously reconstructed by technical devices. What is important is cultivating critical media literacy that recognizes the conditions and limitations of this reconstruction and prepares for the risk of technology standardizing and homogenizing memory.
