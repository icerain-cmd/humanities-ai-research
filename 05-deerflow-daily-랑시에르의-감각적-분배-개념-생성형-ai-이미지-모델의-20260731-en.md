# Algorithmic Distribution of the Sensible: Generative AI and the Extension of Rancière's Concept

## Introduction: Setting the Problem

Jacques Rancière's concept of the 'distribution of the sensible' (partage du sensible) is an original philosophical tool for analyzing the a priori conditions of aesthetic experience. According to Rancière, the distribution of the sensible refers to "a system of sensory experiences that determines what is seen and heard, what can be said, and what can be done." The core of this concept lies in the dual meaning of 'distribution' (partage): it simultaneously signifies 'sharing' and 'exclusion.' This system, which determines both which sensory experiences belong to the common domain and which are systematically excluded, is, for Rancière, political beyond a mere aesthetic category.

This paper explores the theoretical tensions and possibilities for extension that arise when applying this concept to the operation of generative AI image models, particularly text-to-image models like Stable Diffusion, DALL-E, and Midjourney. Specifically, it aims to answer three questions. First, does the fact that generative AI can theoretically produce 'infinite' visual outputs fundamentally violate the fixity of the sensory order posited by Rancière? Second, given that the model's training data distribution and fine-tuning process form a new type of 'statistical sensibility,' should Rancière's concept be applied more precisely in the age of generative AI? Third, when explaining the phenomenon of Stable Diffusion reproducing certain cultural and aesthetic biases through the statistical distribution of training data, must Rancière's concept be extended beyond a 'politics of representation' to a 'politics of data'?

These questions are not merely about the applicability of a concept; they seek a theoretical response to the ontological and political challenges posed by the algorithmic conditions of representation in the 21st century. The analysis will reference Gilbert Simondon's ontology of technical objects, Michel Foucault's concept of regimes of visibility, and recent discussions on data aesthetics.

## 1. The Structure and Limits of Rancière's Concept of the Distribution of the Sensible

### 1.1. The Dual Nature of the Distribution of the Sensible

In Rancière's *The Distribution of the Sensible*, the key point is that 'distribution' is not simply a division but simultaneously signifies 'participation' and 'exclusion.' This operates as a political act that establishes the boundary between 'the visible and the invisible, the sayable and the unsayable,' from Plato's *Republic* where the artist is banished from the community to the curation policies of modern art museums.

For Rancière, the distribution of the sensible operates on three dimensions. First, the 'regime of visibility' determines what can be seen, which objects are included in the realm of visual representation. Second, the 'regime of the sayable' defines which utterances are recognized as meaningful discourse. Third, the 'regime of doing' determines which actions are considered political acts. These three regimes are interconnected and form a specific 'sensible common sense' (sens commun sensible).

### 1.2. The Aesthetic Regime and Political Dissensus

Rancière distinguishes three historical regimes of art: the ethical regime, the representative regime, and the aesthetic regime. The aesthetic regime dismantles the hierarchical order of the representative regime and opens up the very question of "what is art." In this regime, art is no longer subordinated to specific rules or genres; the boundary between the everyday and the artistic is dissolved.

However, the important point is that Rancière does not view these regimes as mere historical stages. Rather, each regime represents a specific mode of distributing the sensible, and the tensions and conflicts between them create moments of political 'dissensus.' Dissensus is the moment when a crack appears in the existing sensory order, allowing excluded voices and visibilities to emerge into the public sphere.

### 1.3. The Ontological Presuppositions of the Concept

Rancière's concept rests on several ontological presuppositions. First, sensory experience is socially constructed and mediated by specific historical conditions. Second, this construction is not a matter of conscious decision or personal taste but operates as an a priori condition of the community. Third, this condition is not fixed but can be changed through political struggle and aesthetic practice.

However, these presuppositions reveal significant tensions when they come into contact with the operation of generative AI. Rancière's concept is centered on the sensory experience and political agency of human subjects and does not adequately account for the way non-human technical objects actively shape the conditions of the distribution of the sensible. In particular, the way generative AI's training data distribution forms a sensory order through statistical regularity introduces a dimension qualitatively different from the 'human' sensibility presupposed by Rancière's concept.

## 2. The Operation of Generative AI Image Models and Sensory Conditions

### 2.1. The Ontology of Statistical Representation

Generative AI image models, especially diffusion models like Stable Diffusion, learn the statistical distribution of training data to generate new images. This process is fundamentally different from traditional models of representation. While traditional representation presupposes a relationship between original and copy, reality and representation, the 'representation' of generative AI is generation based on statistical probability distributions.

Vilém Flusser's concept of the 'techno-image' is useful for understanding this difference. Flusser argues that while traditional images 'represent' the world, technical images 'construct' the world through computational processes. Generative AI images are, in this sense, doubly technical. First, they are generated by computational algorithms. Second, they do not imitate human visual experience but recombine statistical patterns from training data.

This ontological characteristic poses a significant challenge to Rancière's concept of the distribution of the sensible. While Rancière's concept focuses on the question of 'what is visible,' generative AI raises the question of 'what is generable.' If the former analyzes the conditions of visibility, the latter requires an analysis of the conditions of generability.

### 2.2. Training Data Distribution and Statistical Normativity

Stable Diffusion's training data primarily consists of large-scale internet image datasets like LAION-5B. These datasets are collected under specific cultural and historical conditions and reflect the predominance of Western visual culture. The crucial point is that this bias is not merely an accidental result of the data collection process; the statistical distribution of the training data itself forms a specific 'normativity.'

'Statistical normativity' refers to the phenomenon where the statistical average functions as a normative standard. In generative AI, the fact that certain image types (e.g., faces conforming to Western aesthetic standards) are generated more frequently is not mere statistical chance; it is because the distribution of the training data 'naturalizes' specific visual norms. This is similar to the 'naturalization' process of the distribution of the sensible described by Rancière, but with an important difference. For Rancière, naturalization occurs through social customs and institutions, whereas in generative AI, naturalization occurs through statistical probability distributions.

### 2.3. The Politics of Prompts and Generation

Image generation through text prompts reconstructs the relationship between 'the sayable' and 'the visible' in a new way. The prompt is a medium that transforms linguistic input into visual output, and a specific politics of translation operates in this process.

For example, the tendency of a prompt like "beautiful woman" to generate images conforming to Western aesthetic standards is not merely a technical limitation but a result of cultural biases embedded in the training data. More importantly, this bias operates despite the linguistic neutrality of the prompt. That is, even if the user does not intend a specific cultural bias, the model's statistical distribution automatically reproduces specific visual norms.

This poses a significant challenge to Rancière's concept of 'dissensus.' For Rancière, dissensus is a political act that cracks the existing sensory order. However, the statistical normativity of generative AI can systematically limit the possibility of such dissensus. Because the model's generation process is based on statistical probability, 'abnormal' or 'minority' images are systematically excluded.

## 3. Simondon's Concept of Concretization and the Technical Evolution of Generative AI

### 3.1. The Concretization of Technical Objects and the Distribution of the Sensible

Gilbert Simondon's ontology of technical objects provides a theoretical framework for understanding generative AI not as a mere tool but as a 'concretized technical object' that evolves through interaction with its environment. According to Simondon, a technical object initially consists of abstract and separate elements but gradually acquires internal coherence and adaptability to its environment through a process of concretization.

Generative AI image models can be seen as an instance of this concretization process. Early models (e.g., DALL-E 1) had relatively simple structures, but as they evolved into Stable Diffusion v2, DALL-E 3, and Midjourney v6, they acquired more complex internal structures and adaptability to their environment (user prompts, fine-tuning data). In this process, the model functions not merely as a tool but as a 'technical actor' that has internalized a specific mode of distributing the sensible.

However, Simondon's concept of concretization does not fully capture the specificity of generative AI. Simondon's technical object evolves gradually through interaction with its environment, whereas the 'evolution' of generative AI is abruptly determined by the statistical distribution of training data. More importantly, the 'environment' of generative AI is not the physical world but datasets and user prompts. This means that the concretization of the technical object is mediated not by a material environment in the traditional sense but by statistical distributions and algorithmic processing.

### 3.2. Synthetic Data Feedback Loops and Model Collapse

An important phenomenon in the evolution of generative AI is the 'synthetic data feedback loop' and 'model collapse.' The synthetic data feedback loop refers to the process where generative AI uses images it has generated as training data again. When this process is repeated, the model gradually loses the diversity of the original data distribution and converges to specific patterns, a phenomenon known as 'model collapse.'

This phenomenon provides important implications for Rancière's concept of the distribution of the sensible. Model collapse is an extreme example of the 'closed loop' of the distribution of the sensible. The process where the statistical distribution of initial training data forms specific visual norms, these norms are reproduced through generated images, and these images are then used as new training data, reinforcing the norms, replicates at the algorithmic level the 'naturalization' process of the distribution of the sensible described by Rancière.

However, there is an important difference. Rancière's distribution of the sensible can be changed through human political action and aesthetic practice, whereas the synthetic data feedback loop systematically blocks the possibility of such change. Because the feedback loop operates in a direction that reinforces the existing statistical distribution. This suggests that generative AI can create a new form of 'closed sensibility' beyond merely reproducing the existing distribution of the sensible.

### 3.3. The Evolution of Technical Objects and Regressive Closed Loops

When connecting Simondon's concept of concretization with the synthetic data feedback loop of generative AI, two opposing interpretations are possible.

The first interpretation is an evolutionary perspective on technical objects. According to this view, the concretization process of generative AI is an evolutionary process that creates new modes of distributing the sensible. As the model learns from more data and user feedback, it can overcome the biases of the initial training data and generate a wider variety of visual possibilities. This interpretation sees generative AI as creating new conditions that enable Rancière's 'dissensus.'

The second interpretation is a perspective of regressive closed loops. According to this view, the synthetic data feedback loop reinforces the closure of the distribution of the sensible through model collapse. As the model uses its own outputs as training data again, initial biases become increasingly reinforced and diversity is lost. This interpretation sees generative AI as a 'regressive' technology that makes the existing distribution of the sensible more rigid.

These two interpretations are not mutually exclusive; in reality, the development of generative AI shows a complex pattern where both tendencies coexist. The important point is not which interpretation is more valid, but that these two interpretations reveal the theoretical tensions that arise when applying Rancière's concept to the age of generative AI.

## 4. The Politics of Data: Extending the Distribution of the Sensible

### 4.1. From the Politics of Representation to the Politics of Data

Rancière's concept of the distribution of the sensible has traditionally been discussed within the realm of the 'politics of representation.' That is, questions about who has the right to represent what, which images are included in the public sphere, and which voices are excluded have been central. However, the emergence of generative AI demands that these questions be extended to the realm of the 'politics of data.'

The politics of data raises the following questions. First, what data is included in the training dataset? This is not merely a technical decision but a political decision that determines which visual experiences are considered 'normal.' Second, how does the statistical distribution of data form specific visual norms? This requires analyzing the process by which quantitative characteristics of data are transformed into qualitative norms. Third, how are data biases amplified in the algorithmic processing? This requires a critical examination of the myth of technical neutrality.

These questions suggest that Rancière's concept must be extended beyond the 'politics of representation' to a 'politics of data.' However, this extension requires not merely the application of the concept but a reconstruction of the concept itself. Because the politics of data must consider not only the agency of human subjects but also the agency of non-human technical objects, the normativity of statistical distributions, and the political nature of algorithmic processing.

### 4.2. Conceptualizing Statistical Sensibility

'Statistical sensibility' is an attempt to conceptualize the new sensory order formed by generative AI. This concept has the following characteristics.

First, statistical sensibility is probabilistic. While traditional sensibility is based on specific rules or principles, statistical sensibility is based on probability distributions. This means that the distinction between 'normal' and 'abnormal' images is determined by statistical frequency.

Second, statistical sensibility is distributive. It does not have a specific center or origin; the distribution of data itself forms normativity. This contrasts with Rancière's distribution of the sensible, which is mediated by specific social positions or institutions.

Third, statistical sensibility is cyclical. It is continuously reproduced through the cyclical relationship between training data, generated images, and user feedback. This cycle can operate in a closed manner through model collapse or in an open manner through the introduction of new data.

The conceptualization of statistical sensibility provides a theoretical tool for applying Rancière's concept of the distribution of the sensible to the age of generative AI. However, this concept is still in its early stages and requires more systematic theorization.

### 4.3. The Possibility of Algorithmic Dissensus

Rancière's concept of 'dissensus' is useful for seeking a critical response to the statistical normativity of generative AI. Dissensus is the moment when a crack appears in the existing sensory order, allowing excluded voices and visibilities to emerge into the public sphere. In the context of generative AI, algorithmic dissensus can take the following forms.

First, dissensus through diversification of the dataset. This is an attempt to crack the existing statistical normativity by including more diverse cultural and aesthetic standards in the training dataset. However, this approach must recognize that the composition of the dataset itself is a political decision.

Second, dissensus through prompt engineering. This is an attempt to intentionally violate the model's statistical normativity through specific prompts. For example, a prompt like "beautiful woman conforming to non-Western aesthetic standards" can reveal and crack the model's underlying biases.

Third, dissensus through model fine-tuning. This is an attempt to change the existing statistical distribution by retraining the model according to specific cultural and aesthetic standards. However, this approach must recognize that the composition of the fine-tuning data and the retraining process itself can form new types of normativity.

These possibilities of algorithmic dissensus suggest that generative AI can be not merely a tool for reproducing the existing distribution of the sensible but a field for creating new sensory orders. However, at the same time, it must be recognized that such dissensus can be systematically limited by the closed loop of statistical normativity.
