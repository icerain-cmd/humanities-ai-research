## Question

Can Maurice Merleau-Ponty’s concept of embodied perception explain the phenomenon of generative AI mediating language and vision? — What challenge does the absence of bodily sensation between prompts and outputs pose to theories of perception?

## Analytical Results

# Merleau-Ponty’s Embodied Perception and Generative AI: The Possibility and Structure of the Challenge

## Introduction: Decomposing the Levels of the Question

This question actually contains three distinct levels: (1) whether Merleau-Ponty’s concepts can explain **the operation of generative AI as it mediates language and vision**, (2) what the **absence of bodily sensation** between prompts and outputs consists in, and (3) what challenge that absence **poses to theories of perception themselves**. These three levels require different answers. In short: **Merleau-Ponty’s framework explains the phenomenon only partially; it cannot explain it at the primordial level; and precisely this failure becomes a challenge that calls for an expansion of perceptual theory**—that is the central claim of this answer.

---

## 1. What Can Be Explained: Prompting as “Speaking Language”

Merleau-Ponty’s theory of language is exceptionally well suited to explaining generative AI’s mediation of language. In *Phenomenology of Perception*, he distinguishes between two levels of language: **speaking language** (*parole parlante*, the generative use of language) and **spoken language** (*parole parlée*, already-established language and cultural sedimentation). The former is “language at the moment when thought emerges, in the advent of meaning,” while the latter is a secondary form of expression that depends on “the speaker’s linguistic resources and cultural inheritance” (to use the formulation of the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*).

This distinction is structurally consistent with a phenomenological description of the experience of prompting. Designing a prompt is not simply a matter of combining pre-existing tokens; it is a gesture toward a meaning that has not yet fully taken shape. Users may not yet know what meaning they want, or may know it only incompletely, and the prompt forms part of an intentional arc directed toward that indeterminate intention. The moment users read the output, they come to know *for the first time* what it was that they wanted. This has precisely the same structure as Merleau-Ponty’s paradox of expression: “Expression creates the thought that makes it possible.”

It is also important that prompting constitutes an extension of a **fully linguistic mode of embodied being-in-the-world**. Recent research supports this point. The argument of *Algorithms, Language, and Poetry* (*AI and Ethics*, 2025) is that computationally modeled language should not be regarded as “a neutral medium for the transmission of information,” but rather as “a historical crystallization of a more primordial field of embodied expression.” This is an application to AI of Merleau-Ponty’s thesis in *The Prose of the World* that “language is one of the fundamental ways in which we inhabit the world.” At this level, generative AI mediation as linguistic interaction falls **within** the scope of an embodied theory of perception.

---

## 2. What Cannot Be Explained: Disconnection from the Primordial Structure of Perception

However, when Merleau-Ponty’s theory of perception addresses perception itself beyond language, generative AI’s “absence of bodily sensation” becomes an unavoidable barrier. This can be specified in four respects.

**First, the absence of the intentional arc.** For Merleau-Ponty, perception is a sensorimotor achievement that operates through the intentional arc. Visual perception results from the entanglement of eye movements, changes in the direction of the head, vestibular sensations, and tactile expectations within a single circuit. It is not the **brain** but the **whole body** that perceives, and the body is “my means of having a world.” LLMs possess none of this sensorimotor loop: they have no physical sensors, no actuators that transform the world, and no sensory changes generated through such a circuit. As *Transforming Agency* (*Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences*, 2025) accurately points out, these systems “cannot autonomously recognize failure” and lack the conditions of normativity and individuality. Perception is connected to embodied affective states such as regret and surprise, but LLMs possess no such internal states.

**Second, the absence of pre-reflective self-awareness.** Merleau-Ponty’s account of perception rests on the body’s implicit self-awareness: the body perceives without “perceiving that it is perceiving.” This pre-reflective dimension is fundamentally different from any post-processing self-check. As argued in Volume 6 of *AI Meets Philosophy*, the essential difference between human perception and the “perception” of LLMs lies precisely in the presence or absence of this pre-reflective self-awareness—and this gap may be impossible to close in principle. When an LLM responds to an instruction by “identifying” its own error, this is retrospective processing, not the self-feeling of an embodied lifeworld.

**Third, the rupture of flesh and chiasm.** In late Merleau-Ponty, perception rests upon the chiasmatic structure of the intertwining (*entrelacs*) in which the world and the body touch and are touched by one another. *Technological Intentionality and the Concept of Flesh* (*Philosophy & Technology*, 2026) applies this concept to generative AI and proposes reinterpreting technology’s autonomous activity as flesh that is “intrinsically connected” to the human pole. Yet this argument establishes technology as a “pole”; it does not claim that technology *perceives*. Flesh is the ontological condition of felt corporeality—the experiencing body—not the condition of information processing. Generative AI does not inhabit the world’s texture or felt quality; what exists is only a computational mode of being within the statistical structures of text corpora.

**Fourth, the problem of deprivation-based perception or negative capability.** For Merleau-Ponty, the body’s vulnerability and finitude are conditions of perception. Because the body can suffer, become tired, age, and fail, it possesses a particular horizon toward the world. Human beings cannot process everything simultaneously; this sequential mode of reading is a limitation, but at the same time it is a condition for the sedimentation of meaning. The LLM’s mode of processing text globally and all at once lacks this finitude, and we may therefore question whether the result of such processing should be called “perception” at all.

## 3. Is the “Absence” a Challenge or an Opportunity for Reconstruction?

Here we arrive at the third level of the question: the challenge to theories of perception. The central point is this: **the absence of bodily sensation is less a counterexample to perceptual theory than a device that reveals the theory’s boundary conditions.** Paradoxically, absence does not simply indicate non-presence; it illuminates the fact that absence may be a minimum requirement of perception.

There is, however, a more interesting reconstruction. Borrowing a concept proposed in *Transforming Agency*, we can say that LLMs, as a “library-that-talks,” lack sensorimotor embodiment but possess three distinctive forms of embodiment: **textual embodiment**, **embodiment through digitally extended interfaces**, and **resource-intensive computational embodiment**. The coupling of human beings and LLMs produces **midtended agency**, which is neither a conventional instrumental extension nor a fully intentional agent. This concept enables us to understand the user–prompt–output circuit as a distributed perceptual system. Like a cane—a component of the extended body schema—the AI interface becomes incorporated into the user’s perceptual circuit. The difference is that, rather than “transmitting” the world’s texture in the way a cane transmits the feel of the world, it **transforms and conveys the world’s statistical structures through a linguistic surface**.

A theoretical refinement is important here. The difference between a tool such as a cane and generative AI is that the former operates *within* a sensorimotor circuit, whereas the latter generates linguistic outputs *outside* that circuit and intervenes in the user’s sensorimotor circuit. AI therefore cannot be reduced to the phenomenological theory of tools in HCI after Dourish—particularly the transparency and backgrounding of tools. AI is **an anomalous tool that does not recede into the background**; rather, it is a medium that reorganizes the user’s perceptual background itself.

---

## 4. The Final Form of the Challenge to Perceptual Theory

Taken together, the challenge can be stated in three forms.

1. **An ontological challenge**: Does perception necessarily presuppose flesh? If so, the concept of LLM “perception” is a category mistake, and the problem lies not with perceptual theory but with the metaphorical use of language. If flesh is optional, then a new category of “disembodied perception” is required. — The latter is the more productive direction.

2. **A mediological challenge**: Unlike the media addressed by perceptual theory thus far—tools, art, and language—generative AI is a **medium that creates outputs**. This destabilizes the distinction between perception and expression itself. For Merleau-Ponty, perception and expression were circularly related, whereas AI media synthesize expression without perception. The question posed to perceptual theory by “expression without anything to express” is a new one.

3. **A normative challenge**: The absence of embodied perception leads directly to an absence of responsibility. Embodied perception is the capacity to respond to the world. (This point is directly connected to the asymmetrical structure of responsibility addressed in your paper on AI and the environment: namely, the question of how an environment that lacks agency can nevertheless become an object of responsibility.)

## Conclusion

Merleau-Ponty’s concept of embodied perception can explain generative AI’s mediation of language **at the level of a theory of expression**. The three axes of prompting as “speaking language,” the cycle of expression and discovery, and the embodied origins of language provide effective tools for describing the phenomenology of interaction with generative AI. However, **at the primordial level of perception—the intentional arc, the sensorimotor circuit, pre-reflective self-awareness, and flesh—it cannot provide an explanation**. Precisely this failure reveals the boundaries of perceptual theory. The “absence of bodily sensation” does not refute perceptual theory; rather, it is an event that makes historically visible the question of what the minimum conditions of perception are. Ultimately, the most productive way to interpret contact with generative AI is as a challenge requiring perceptual theory to expand from a “phenomenology of the human body” into a “phenomenology of distributed, medially mediated, and disembodied perceptual systems.” At this point of expansion, the ontological turn you are pursuing—understanding AI not as an object but as an environment—may make an important theoretical contribution.
