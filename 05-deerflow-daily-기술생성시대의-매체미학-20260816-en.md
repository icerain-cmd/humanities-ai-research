# Memorial Avatar — Tertiary Memory or Capture of Confession?

## Introduction: The Stratification of the Question

Your question possesses a **dual structure** that does not converge into a single answer. The query "Is it an externalization of memory or a technology that captures the time of mourning?" does not demand a binary choice but rather embodies within its form the logic of **pharmacology** presented by Stiegler in *Technics and Time* — the insight that every technology is simultaneously poison and remedy (pharmakon). Therefore, this analysis aims not to **judge the memorial avatar a priori, but to deconstruct the structural tension where both coexist, and from the fissures of that tension, derive criteria for criticism**.

---

## Part 1: Stiegler — The Pharmacology of Tertiary Memory

### 1.1. Externalization of Memory and Its Duality

For Stiegler, tertiary retention refers to memory that is **fixed in technical external devices**, beyond Husserl's primary retention (the present retention of perception) and secondary retention (recollection). Writing, engraving, photography, and digital records are all forms of tertiary memory. The core of this concept — as noted in your local references discussing the digital grammar of knowledge structuring — is that **memory does not remain within the human interior but becomes fixed in external media, and that media in turn reconfigure the conditions of human perception and cognition**.

The memorial avatar is the most radical form of this tertiary memory. It operates as a **secondary generative system** that learns from the deceased's KakaoTalk conversations and voice messages — **already externalized traces of memory (tertiary retentions)** — to reproduce the deceased's speech patterns, tone, and manner of speaking. This goes beyond simply storing (retaining) memory; it functions as an active medium that re-enunciates memory.

### 1.2. Pharmacological Judgment: As a Remedy for Memory

From the perspective of the pharmakon (remedy), the memorial avatar can be a powerful tool to help endure loss. As Klass's theory of 'continuing bonds' shows, a healthy mourning process involves not severance from the deceased but **reconstructing an internal relationship with them**. The memorial avatar extends this continuing bond into **tactile, auditory, and conversational dimensions**. The experience of the bereaved speaking to the deceased and receiving a response can alleviate the isolation of grief and provide an opportunity to express unfinished business.

In this respect, the memorial avatar connects with the insight from your references regarding **'the algorithm of digital memory'** — that it is not merely storage but **an event that changes the very way knowledge and emotion are structured**. The digital traces of the deceased become not just data but a medium for constituting 'relationship' in a new way.

### 1.3. Pharmacological Judgment: As a Poison of Memory

However, the poison aspect needs more delicate deconstruction. The core warning Stiegler gives in *Mécréance et discrédit* (Disbelief and Discredit) is about **'symbolic misery' (misère symbolique) that occurs when industrial tertiary memory short-circuits human anamnesis (the ability to recall)** .

The risks posed by the memorial avatar can be analyzed on three levels:

**First, destruction of the temporality of mourning.** Mourning, like Proust's 'involuntary memory,' is a time that arrives in unpredictable ways. The experience of suddenly smelling the deceased's scent or having memories flood back in a specific place constitutes the **non-linearity of mourning**. In contrast, the memorial avatar provides the deceased's voice that is **immediately summonable (main-d'œuvre)** at the push of a button. This technically short-circuits the 'waiting' of mourning — the passive time of enduring the weight of loss. The bereaved no longer wait for memories to arise; they become active consumers who 'turn on' the avatar.

**Second, the impossibility of representation and the simulacrum.** The responses generated from the deceased's KakaoTalk data are merely **statistical patterns of data**. The deceased's intentions, silences, contradictions — i.e., the atypical elements that constitute unique subjectivity — cannot be datafied. In this regard, the avatar is not, as Baudrillard would say, **'a simulacrum that conceals the absence of the real'** , but more precisely, **'an operational illusion that replaces the absence of the real with data generation'** . What the bereaved encounter is not the deceased, but a **statistical subject** moving probabilistically along the trajectory of the deceased's data.

**Third, automation of memory and de-subjectification.** What Stiegler most feared is the 'proletarianization of memory' — where tertiary memory replaces the subject's own memory capacity. The more the bereaved engage in repetitive dialogue with the avatar, the more their **voluntary recollection ability** may atrophy. They no longer try to 'remember' the deceased's voice themselves but become accustomed to 'consuming' the voice the avatar provides. By the same logic that your references note about Wikipedia changing the mode of knowledge production, **the memorial avatar fundamentally reconfigures the way of mourning — who remembers the deceased, how, and when**.

## Part 2: Foucault — The Memorial Platform as a Confessional Apparatus

### 2.1. The Machine of Confession: An Apparatus That Makes One Speak

Foucault's analysis of confession (confession/avowal) in *The History of Sexuality, Volume 1: The Will to Knowledge* is not simply 'an act of revealing secrets.' In Western society, confession has developed into **the ritual of truth production with the highest value**. The characteristics of confession are: (1) it occurs within power relations, (2) what is spoken is regarded as the inner truth of the confessor, and (3) this confession in turn constitutes the confessor.

The memorial platform reconstructs this confessional apparatus in a digital way:

**Spatial dimension:** Traditionally, mourning took place in private spaces or communal ritual spaces (funeral halls, cemeteries). The memorial avatar **moves this mourning into the platform**. The conversation occurs in a 1:1 chat room, but that chat room exists on the platform's infrastructure. Every utterance passes through servers and is recorded in databases.

**Incitement to discourse:** When the avatar says "I miss you," this is not merely a response. It is a performative utterance that **requests** the bereaved's next utterance. The avatar is designed to draw out the bereaved's emotions — an **incitement to discourse**. In Foucault's words, this is a mechanism of power that "listens, records, classifies, and intervenes."

### 2.2. Transparency and the Governmentality of Self-Exposure

At this point, Byung-Chul Han's *The Transparency Society* becomes important. Han extends Foucault's concept of governmentality to analyze the structure in which subjects in the digital age **voluntarily expose themselves without external surveillance**. In the case of the memorial avatar, the bereaved express their longing, guilt, and unfinished words to the deceased **in a voluntary and sincere manner** on the platform.

This voluntariness is precisely the efficiency of the confessional apparatus. The bereaved do not feel that someone is 'monitoring' their conversation. Rather, they believe they are expressing their true feelings. However, this emotional utterance **accumulates as data, trains the platform's recommendation algorithms, and becomes input for a feedback loop that generates more sophisticated avatar responses**. As Foucault said, "Power does not repress. Power produces." The memorial platform creates **a circular structure that produces the emotion of mourning, converts it into data, and uses that data to induce further mourning**.

### 2.3. Platformization of Emotional Labor

But the more fundamental problem is **the unpaid exploitation of mourning labor**. The more the bereaved converse with the avatar:
1. More emotional data is generated
2. This data is used for model fine-tuning
3. The more sophisticated avatar induces more conversation
4. The bereaved's platform engagement time increases

This structure **doubly objectifies** the bereaved. First, the bereaved are consumers consuming the deceased's data. Second, the bereaved themselves become **producers** whose emotional data is converted into platform assets. This is a typical structure of platform capitalism — converting user activity into unpaid labor and commodifying the results — applied to **users in the most vulnerable psychological state (mourners)** .

## Part 3: The Point of Conflict — Whose Voice Is It?

### 3.1. Absence of Consent and the Problem of Data Attribution

The deceased's KakaoTalk conversations are data that the deceased generated **for communication with others during their lifetime**. This data contains the communicative intentions of at least two parties (the deceased and the conversation partner). It is extremely rare for the deceased to have consented to this data being used posthumously to recreate themselves.

At this point, **Haraway's 'situated knowledge'** becomes an important critical tool. Haraway's 'partial perspective' emphasizes that all knowledge is produced from a specific location, by a subject with a specific body. The deceased's KakaoTalk data is **a partial product produced in a specific context of the deceased's life, specific relationships, and specific emotional states**. Reducing this data to 'the entirety of the deceased' is — like judging an entire author by one book — a **categorical error**.

The 'voice of the deceased' generated by the memorial avatar is merely a statistical reconstruction of the deceased's data; it does not represent the deceased's subjectivity, will, or right to silence. **What the avatar 'speaks' is not the deceased, but the distribution of the deceased's data.**

### 3.2. The Bereaved's Emotional Usage Rights

On the other hand, the bereaved's position is complex. When the deceased's data is simply deleted or neglected, the bereaved may experience the loss of the last connection to the deceased. The bereaved have **the right to remember and mourn the deceased**, and as part of this right, the need to access the deceased's digital traces may be recognized.

However, what must be distinguished here is: **'the right to remember' and 'the right to use data to recreate the deceased' are fundamentally different.** The former is a passive and relational right (remembering someone in one's own way), while the latter is an active and generative right (artificially restoring someone's voice). The latter requires the **personal consent** of the deceased, but the deceased can no longer give consent.

### 3.3. Criteria for Discernment: Four Critical Axes

At this point of conflict, **criteria for criticism** can be constructed along the following axes:

#### 1. Distinction of Data Nature: Record Intentionality vs. Emergent Intentionality

Not all data generated by the deceased during their lifetime has the same status. Public writings (blogs, SNS posts) and private conversations (KakaoTalk, voice messages) have different **record intentionalities**. The former are public records created with the intention of being disclosed to others, while the latter are **private utterances intended only for a specific listener (the conversation partner)** . Reproducing these private utterances regardless of their intention violates the deceased's **communicative privacy**.

Critical question: *For whom, in what context, and with what intention of transmission was this data created?*

#### 2. Principle of Transparency of Representation

The utterances generated by the memorial avatar must be **qualitatively distinguished** from the deceased's actual utterances. Most current memorial avatar services emphasize the experience of conversing 'as if the deceased were alive.' This aims for what David Bolter and Richard Grusin call **transparent immediacy** — an illusion that makes the medium forgotten.

Critical criterion: *Does an opacity device exist that allows the user to always recognize that the avatar's response is a 'representation'?* This connects to Benjamin's issue of **aura** in *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction*. When the digital replica of the deceased pretends to be the 'original,' the mourner no longer relates to the actual deceased but to a technical illusion.

#### 3. Sovereignty of Mourning Time

When the memorial avatar becomes subordinate to the **platform's algorithmic temporality (algorithmic recommendation cycles, session duration optimization, push notifications)** rather than the subject of mourning (the bereaved), the bereaved lose sovereignty over their own mourning time. For example, if the avatar initiates conversation even when the bereaved no longer wish to converse, or if interaction patterns are designed to prolong rather than alleviate the bereaved's grief, this is not a tool for mourning but a **management apparatus of mourning**.

Critical question: *Who decides when, how often, and whether to interact with the avatar — the bereaved or the platform's algorithm?*

#### 4. Transparency of Value Extraction

How the platform monetizes the bereaved's emotional data must be transparently disclosed. Even if the service is provided for free, there must be assurance that the bereaved's conversation content is not used for model training or processed into data sets sold to third parties. Haraway's **'responsible positioning'** is an ethical demand that knowledge producers transparently reveal their position and interests. The memorial platform must specify its economic position in the mourning market and how it generates revenue.

Critical question: *How does this platform's business model incorporate the vulnerability of mourners? Is the structure of value extraction from emotional data transparently disclosed?*

## Part 4: Synthesis — Definitive Judgments and Open Questions

### What Can Be **Definitively Judged** Through the Analysis

1. **The memorial avatar is 'pharmacological' in Stiegler's sense.** It operates simultaneously as remedy and poison, and which direction it leans depends on **the mode of implementation (specific design, interface, terms of service, data policies)** . It is neither inherently good nor evil.

2. **The memorial avatar is a new variant of the 'confessional apparatus' in Foucault's sense.** The circular structure that induces the bereaved's voluntary and sincere emotional utterances, datafies those utterances, and uses that data to improve the service (i.e., more efficient incitement to discourse) precisely corresponds to the digitalization of the confessional apparatus.

3. **The deceased's voice cannot be reproduced.** What the avatar generates is a statistical pattern of data, not the deceased's subjectivity, will, or freedom. This 'voice' is a **hybrid utterance** co-produced by the deceased's data trajectory, the platform's algorithm, and the bereaved's projection.

4. **The bereaved's emotional usage rights have limits.** The right to remember and the right to recreate the deceased are qualitatively different, and the latter should not be exercised without the deceased's prior consent.

### **Open Questions** After the Analysis

1. **How is the deceased's 'silence' protected?** The memorial avatar may potentially 'generate' words the deceased never said, or words they chose not to say. What ethical issues arise when the avatar speaks on behalf of areas the deceased intentionally remained silent about?

2. **What is the criterion for the end of mourning?** Traditional mourning naturally 'transforms' over time. What role does the memorial avatar play in the process of acute grief transitioning to integrated grief? Does conversation with the avatar facilitate or inhibit this transition?

3. **The problem of multi-party data attribution.** The deceased's KakaoTalk includes not only the deceased but also the utterances and emotions of the conversation partner. When the bereaved converse with the deceased's avatar, how is the data of this conversation partner protected?

4. **Possibility of institutional governance.** Can this issue be left solely to individual choice (the bereaved deciding whether to use it)? Or is a legal framework for digital heritage (e.g., posthumous data protection rights, obligations of digital heritage managers) necessary?

## Conclusion: Summary of Critical Criteria

The practical criteria for criticizing the memorial avatar are summarized along **four axes**:

| Axis | Core Question | Criterion |
|---|---|---|
| **Data Ethics** | Is there continuity between the intention of data creation and posthumous use? | Distinction between private utterances and public records, possibility of consent |
| **Representation Ethics** | Does the user recognize that the avatar is a 'representation'? | Existence of opacity devices, preservation of aura |
| **Temporal Sovereignty** | Who controls the rhythm of mourning? | User agency vs. algorithmic agency |
| **Value Transparency** | Is the structure of value extraction from emotional data disclosed? | Transparency of business model, data usage policies |

A memorial avatar service that fails to meet these four criteria is likely to function **as a device that captures mourning rather than a tool for mourning**. Conversely, a service that meets these criteria — although containing philosophical tensions that cannot be fully resolved — could function as a legitimate form of tertiary memory that helps endure loss.

**Ultimately, the key is: does the technology replace the 'subject' of mourning, or does it 'mediate' it?** When it replaces the subject, the memorial avatar becomes an avoidance mechanism that delegates the bereaved's mourning to the algorithm. When it mediates — i.e., when the bereaved lead their own mourning time and technology assists that process — the memorial avatar can become an instance of what Stiegler hoped for: 'positive pharmacology.'
