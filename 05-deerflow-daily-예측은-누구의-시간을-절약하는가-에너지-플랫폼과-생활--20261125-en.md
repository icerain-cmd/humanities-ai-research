## Question

Suppose an energy management platform in a rural village combines solar power generation, household electricity use, battery charge levels, and weather forecasts to automatically adjust electricity allocation and rates by time period. Although this system may appear to manage the variability of renewable energy efficiently, it can shift the cost burden onto the everyday rhythms of older adults, care workers, and seasonal laborers who cannot change their consumption schedules to match predictions. Drawing together Donna Haraway’s concept of “situated knowledges” and Anna Tsing’s discussions of multispecies coexistence, analyze what should count as evidence of sustainability when the energy savings calculated by the dashboard conflict with the times of waiting, care, and seasonality borne by residents and nonhuman life. In particular, focusing on the process through which residents bypass automatic allocation or redistribute a community battery, and through which those choices alter the criteria of subsequent prediction models, argue whether digital sustainability can be designed not as a reduction in consumption but as the capacity to publicly renegotiate the authority of prediction and the procedures for coordinating everyday rhythms.

# The Conflict Between Predictive Power and Everyday Rhythms: Rethinking the Evidence of Digital Sustainability

## 1. The Layers of the Problem — What the Dashboard Does Not See

An energy management platform integrating solar power generation, household electricity use, battery charge levels, and weather forecasts has been introduced in a rural village. The system appears to solve the technical challenge of renewable energy variability. It lowers rates when generation is high and raises them when it is cloudy or nighttime, thereby distributing consumption across time. Based on prediction models, the dashboard automatically adjusts real-time electricity allocation and summarizes the result as a numerical figure called “energy savings.”

Yet the problem depends on whose time serves as the basis for calculating these “savings.” The periods of consumption that the prediction model regards as optimal conflict with the everyday rhythms of older adults, care workers, and seasonal laborers. The system may recommend running the washing machine on a clear morning, but a resident caring for a family member with dementia may have no control over that time. The prediction model records this choice as “inefficiency,” and the accumulated inefficiency data leads the next day’s allocation algorithm to become more stringent. Households unable to adapt their consumption patterns to the system are gradually pushed into “penalty periods.”

The central point is this: what the platform manages is not simply “energy” but **the rhythm of time**. When the authority to define that rhythm is monopolized by the prediction model behind the dashboard, the evidence of sustainability becomes distorted.

## 2. Situated Knowledges: The Positionality Concealed by the Predictive Viewpoint

The “god trick”—the disembodied viewpoint criticized by Donna Haraway in “Situated Knowledges” (1988)—corresponds precisely to the way this platform’s prediction model operates. The prediction model presents itself as though it calculates the “optimal allocation” from a neutral viewpoint located nowhere. Yet this neutrality is an illusion. The prediction model is based on a particular temporality—thirty-minute consumption patterns for each household and twenty-four-hour weather forecasts—and on particular value criteria, such as minimizing costs and maximizing renewable-energy utilization. These criteria were designed independently of residents’ everyday rhythms.

Haraway rejects the god trick and argues that **partial perspective** is instead a condition for a more powerful form of objectivity. All knowledge is produced from a particular location, and it becomes responsible knowledge only when that location is acknowledged. Applied to the energy platform, the “energy savings” displayed on the dashboard are partial knowledge produced from particular locations: platform designers, electricity markets, and weather prediction models. When this knowledge presents itself as an “objectively optimal solution,” knowledge from other locations is excluded from the system’s calculations: the “possible time” experienced by care workers, the “irregular time of returning home” experienced by seasonal laborers, and the “slow-moving morning” experienced by older adults.

As the local reference *A Structural Examination of the Differences Between the Anthropocene and the Machinic Era* points out, the machinic era is “an era in which machines, algorithms, sensors, networks, data, and artificial intelligence organize human perception and judgment, labor, production, and consumption.” In this era, “machines function not simply as tools but as active agents that interpret, distribute, and control the world.” The prediction model does not merely “provide information”; it defines which times count as “normal” times for consumption and treats rhythms that depart from that definition as “noise.” This is an instance of the power of perspective, against which Haraway warned, becoming embedded in digital infrastructure.

## 3. Conflicting Multispecies Temporalities — What the Rhythms of Waiting, Care, and Seasonality Tell Us

Anna Lowenhaupt Tsing’s discussion of multispecies coexistence extends this issue beyond human society. In *Friction* (2005) and *The Mushroom at the End of the World* (2015), Tsing traces how capitalism’s singular temporality collides with the rhythms of diverse living beings. Her multispecies ethnography sees the possibility of new relationships emerging at “friction points,” where human economic activity encounters the distinct temporalities of nonhuman beings—mushrooms, forests, and soil.

In the case of the energy platform, these friction points appear at three levels.

**First, the time of the atmosphere.** Short-term weather forecasts divide the sky into discrete categories such as “clear,” “cloudy,” and “rainy.” Rural atmospheres, however, move according to much finer rhythms: fog that lingers late in a valley, afternoon gusts that suddenly intensify across a field, and the rate at which dust during harvest season covers solar panels. These temporal variations in the atmosphere, which prediction models fail to capture, produce errors in forecasts of renewable-energy generation. The cost of those errors is passed on to residents as unpredictable spikes in their electricity bills.

**Second, the time of care.** As Tsing’s work demonstrates, rhythms of care cannot be reduced to the logic of productivity. The sleep patterns of an older adult with dementia, the mealtimes of a family member with a disability, and the feeding rhythms of an infant do not coincide with the schedules of the labor market and therefore do not coincide with the “optimal consumption periods” established by the energy platform. When the system records this mismatch as “inefficiency,” care workers bear a double burden: in addition to the labor of care itself, they incur a penalty because that labor takes place outside the system’s preferred schedule.

**Third, seasonal time.** Agricultural workers during harvest, seasonal workers in orchards, and temporary workers employed during the kimchi-making season appear as extreme outliers in statistical averages of electricity consumption. The system treats them as unpredictable variables, but this variability is not a defect in the system’s design; it is a fundamental rhythm of agriculture and ecosystems. To borrow Tsing’s terminology, this is “friction” between the extractive logic of capitalism and the regenerative rhythms of ecosystems. When the system treats this friction only as “error,” sustainability loses its ecological foundation.

The local reference “Part IV, Chapter 1: Cleaning Up Pollution—Principles of Digital Ecology” offers an important insight here. If artificial nature, or the digital ecosystem, is a field in which “numerous nonhuman agents—from data, algorithms, platforms, and servers to energy infrastructure and the physical natural world—are entangled,” then the question of “how humans and nonhumans can form ecological solidarities” becomes a question of whether an energy platform can accept the time of the atmosphere, the time of care, and seasonal time as conditions of coexistence rather than as noise.

## 4. Bypass, Redistribution, and Feedback — The Possibility of Renegotiating Predictive Power

Residents, however, are not passive victims. Alternative evidence of digital sustainability emerges through their practical responses.

**Bypass:** Some residents manually override automatic allocation. If they need to run the washing machine during an expensive period, they do so. This choice is recorded by the system as an “anomaly.” What matters is that the anomaly does not end with the inefficiency of a single household. When similar acts of bypass occur across multiple households, the pattern suggests the existence of a social temporality that the system fails to recognize.

**Redistribution:** Residents may renegotiate the location and charging strategy of the community battery. If a village-scale battery installed with government subsidies was initially centrally controlled by the platform’s algorithm, residents may develop their own rules for collectively determining when the battery is used. For example, they might create local rules such as “from 9:00 to 11:00 a.m., priority is given to households providing care” or “during harvest season, battery discharge is permitted in the evening.”

**Feedback loops:** When data from these acts of bypass and redistribution is fed back into the prediction model—especially when the system is redesigned to process the data as “learning” rather than as “error correction”—the criteria of prediction themselves change. A model that initially had the single objective of “minimizing costs” may begin learning patterns such as “demand among care households is concentrated at 10:00 a.m. on Mondays” or “the 7:00 p.m. peak during harvest season is unavoidable.” This means more than a simple algorithmic update. It is a process of renegotiating the fundamental question of **who has the authority to predict**.

From Haraway’s perspective, this process represents the democratization of knowledge production: the “god trick” is dismantled and multiple positionalities are recognized. From Tsing’s perspective, it is a moment in which new forms of relationship emerge at the friction point where capitalism’s—or the machinic era’s—singular temporality encounters the rhythms of diverse living beings.

## 5. A Thesis on the Evidence of Digital Sustainability

What, then, should count as evidence of this platform’s sustainability?

The “carbon savings” calculated by the dashboard—such as reductions in CO₂ per kilowatt-hour—or the amount of money saved are not sufficient evidence of sustainability. These indicators conceal the structural problem of how predictive power is distributed and how it conflicts with everyday rhythms. A new system of evidence is needed.

**First, the existence of procedures through which predictive authority can be publicly negotiated.** It must be transparent which criteria the prediction model prioritizes—cost, time, season, or other factors—and who has the authority to adjust those criteria when they conflict with residents’ everyday rhythms. Evidence of sustainability lies not in the accuracy of prediction but in the extent to which predictive power is distributed.

**Second, the existence of feedback pathways through which bypass and redistribution are learned by the system.** Do residents’ resistance and adjustments lead to structural learning that changes the system’s next predictive criteria, or are they treated merely as exceptions or errors to be corrected? Sustainability is not the degree to which residents adapt to the system; it is the system’s flexibility in adapting to residents’ rhythms.

**Third, the way the temporalities of nonhuman beings—the atmosphere, seasons, and ecosystems—are incorporated not as model variables but as “partners in coordination.”** Renewable-energy management ultimately involves coordinating human energy consumption with the energy flows of nature. When this coordination takes the form of reciprocal tuning rather than one-sided prediction—for example, when the prediction model treats uncertainty in weather forecasts not as “error” but as “room for negotiation”—the possibility of multispecies sustainability opens up.

The local reference “From Technical Editing to Technogenesis” states that technology represents “a paradigm shift that goes beyond a simple instrumental extension and redefines the very concept of human creativity.” Applied to the energy platform, genuine digital sustainability depends not on technology’s capacity to “calculate” consumption efficiency, but on its procedural capacity to newly “coordinate” the relationship between predictive power and everyday rhythms. In other words, it depends on whether technology can transform itself into a space in which the temporalities of residents and nonhuman agents coexist, rather than converging into a single prediction.

## 6. Conclusion — Redefining the Evidence of Sustainability

The “energy savings” calculated by a dashboard may be the result of converting someone’s time into a cost. The evidence of genuine sustainability lies not in a reduction in consumption but in whether **the predictive power that determines who must live according to which times can be renegotiated through public procedures**. This renegotiation must include not only the everyday rhythms of human residents but also the rhythms of nonhuman entities such as the atmosphere and the seasons.

Haraway’s concept of “situated knowledges” reminds us that the viewpoint of prediction is never neutral, while Tsing’s discussion of multispecies coexistence calls for coordination with the rhythms of ecosystems beyond human temporality. Seen through these two lenses, digital sustainability is redefined not as a technical task of improving the accuracy of prediction algorithms, but as a political and ecological task of democratizing procedures of coordination at the “friction points” where multiple temporalities collide.

The central point of this redefinition is simple. Evidence that a system is sustainable is not the figure displayed in green on the dashboard. It is whether the system contains a channel through which someone can say, “That won’t work right now,” and whether a feedback loop exists through which that statement changes the criteria of the next day’s predictions.
