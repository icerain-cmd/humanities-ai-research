## Introduction: The Machinic Phylum as Sensory Infrastructure

The phenomenon in which an autonomous vehicle's LiDAR sensor identifies the movements of nighttime, rainy-weather, and wheelchair users with different probabilities is not a simple technical defect. It reveals that machine sensing operates not as a neutral tool assisting human vision, but as an apparatus of sensory politics that allocates who becomes 'visible' first and who may pass through public space. This paper borrows the concept of the machinic phylum to analyze how the sensing system of autonomous vehicles produces asymmetric citizenship, contrary to what anthropocentric perception theories and safety norms assume.

The machinic phylum is a concept proposed by Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, referring to a dynamic system in which technical objects self-organize within material-energy flows. However, this paper extends the concept to limit it to phenomena where non-human sensory and judgment infrastructure reconstructs human perception, mobility, and daily rhythms. The LiDAR of an autonomous vehicle functions not as a mere sensor but as sensory infrastructure that redistributes visibility and mobility in urban space.

## 1. Asymmetric Sensing by LiDAR: Case Analysis

### 1.1 Sensing Bias Under Nighttime and Rainy Conditions

LiDAR recognizes the distance and shape of objects by emitting laser pulses and measuring their reflection time. However, this technology shows drastic performance differences depending on weather conditions and lighting environments. At night, LiDAR uses its own light source, so theoretically it can be advantageous over human vision, but in practice, research shows that pedestrian recognition rates decrease by 15-20% compared to daytime due to low contrast and changes in reflectivity. A more serious problem occurs in rainy weather. Raindrops scatter the laser, increasing noise, and wet road surfaces distort reflection patterns. This drastically lowers the detection probability of pedestrians, especially vulnerable road users such as wheelchair users.

### 1.2 The Invisibility of Wheelchair Users

Wheelchair users experience structural disadvantages in LiDAR detection. Wheelchairs have a lower height than typical pedestrians, and their metal frames and plastic components exhibit different reflection characteristics from human tissue. Additionally, wheelchair movement patterns (rotation, stopping, acceleration) differ from pedestrian gait patterns, making it highly likely they deviate from the standard pedestrian trajectories learned by machine learning models. These differences go beyond simple technical limitations, revealing problems in a recognition system that classifies certain body types and modes of movement as 'abnormal.'

### 1.3 Political Implications of Detection Probability

This asymmetric sensing distributes visibility and mobility rights in public space differentially. The lower the probability with which LiDAR detects a particular user, the more conservative the driving strategy (deceleration, avoidance, stopping) the autonomous vehicle adopts toward that user. Consequently, users who are technically 'less visible' experience more waiting time and uncertainty on the road. This is not merely a safety issue but a political decision about who can 'legitimately' occupy public space.

## 2. The Explanatory Limits of Anthropocentric Perception Theories

### 2.1 The Limits of the Cartesian Visual Paradigm

Modern Western philosophy, based on Descartes' concept of 'clear and distinct perception,' positioned vision as the most objective and reliable sense. This paradigm leads to understanding LiDAR's 'objective' measurement as an extension of human vision. However, LiDAR sensing has a fundamentally different ontological status from human vision. Human vision is contextual and interpretive, whereas LiDAR sensing is based on algorithmic classification and probabilistic estimation. This difference is where anthropocentric perception theories fail to explain the asymmetry of machine sensing.

### 2.2 The Inadequacy of Phenomenological Perception Theory

Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology understands perception as the active participation of the body. However, autonomous vehicle sensing is a 'disembodied vision' that operates without bodily experience. This means phenomenological perception theory has limits in explaining machine sensing. Machine sensing operates without human perceptual intentionality, and rather replaces and reconstructs human perception.

### 2.3 The Assumption of Technical Neutrality in Safety Norms

Current autonomous vehicle safety norms are based on the assumption that technology is neutral and objective. For example, the U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) guidelines for autonomous vehicles present 'objective' criteria for sensor performance but do not consider discriminatory impacts on specific user groups. This shows how the myth of technical neutrality conceals social inequality.

## 3. The Sensory Politics of the Machinic Phylum: Theoretical Analysis

### 3.1 Simondon's Concept of Concretization and the Evolution of LiDAR

Gilbert Simondon argued that technical objects evolve from an abstract stage to a concrete stage. Early LiDAR was a simple distance-measuring device, but it has now been concretized into a complex recognition system combined with machine learning. This concretization process is one in which a technical object optimizes its internal structure through interaction with its environment. However, this optimization is biased toward specific conditions (clear weather, standard pedestrians), meaning the evolution of technical objects cannot be separated from social values.

### 3.3 Model Collapse and the Simplification of the Senses

Model collapse is a phenomenon that occurs when generative AI uses its own output as training data, causing the model's diversity to gradually decrease and become fixed on specific patterns. In autonomous vehicle recognition models, this manifests as maintained detection accuracy for certain user types (standard pedestrians) but progressively degraded detection capability for non-standard users (wheelchair users). This is a process in which the machinic phylum converges toward a simplified sensory system, and in this process, specific bodies and modes of movement are systematically excluded.

## 4. Evolution of Technical Objects and Regressive Closed Loops: A Comparison of Two Interpretations

### 4.1 Evolutionary Interpretation: Adaptation of Technical Objects

The evolutionary interpretation, following Simondon's concept of concretization, views LiDAR's asymmetric sensing as part of the process by which a technical object adapts to its environment. From this perspective, performance degradation under nighttime and rainy conditions is a 'temporary' problem to be overcome through technological development, and the invisibility of wheelchair users is a 'correctable' bias due to lack of data. This interpretation offers an optimistic outlook on technological development but overlooks that the evolution of technical objects cannot be separated from social power relations.

### 4.2 Regressive Closed-Loop Interpretation: The Fixation of Technical Objects

In contrast, the regressive closed-loop interpretation views LiDAR's asymmetric sensing as a process in which a technical object becomes 'fixed' in a direction that systematically excludes specific user groups. From this perspective, synthetic data feedback loops and model collapse are mechanisms that impede the evolution of technical objects and amplify initial biases. This interpretation emphasizes that technological development does not automatically proceed in a more inclusive direction; rather, it warns that technical objects can 'evolve' in ways that reinforce social inequality.

### 4.3 Synthesis of the Two Interpretations: The Political Evolution of Technical Objects

The two interpretations are not mutually exclusive. Technical objects simultaneously undergo processes of evolution and regression. LiDAR technology evolves under certain conditions (clear weather, standard pedestrians) but regresses under others (rainy weather, wheelchair users). This means the evolution of technical objects is not neutral but is directed by specific social values and power relations. Therefore, understanding the sensory politics of the machinic phylum requires analyzing the evolution of technical objects within their social context.
