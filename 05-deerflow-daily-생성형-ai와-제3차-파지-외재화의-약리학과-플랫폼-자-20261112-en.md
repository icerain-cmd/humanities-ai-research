# The Generative AI Extension and Pharmacological Transformation of Tertiary Retention

## 1. Framing the Problem: What Does “Tertiary Retention” Mean for Stiegler?

For Bernard Stiegler, memory consists of three levels. **Primary retention** is the immediate retention of present perception, while **secondary retention** is the subjective memory of past experiences. **Tertiary retention** refers to objective memory externalized in technical objects—writing, tools, recording media, and digital databases. This tertiary retention is not merely a means of storage; it constitutes the very conditions of human consciousness and knowledge. By technically appropriating Husserl’s analysis of time-consciousness, Stiegler establishes the thesis that “without the exteriorization of memory, neither consciousness nor individuation is possible.”

The key point here is the **pharmacological character** of tertiary retention. Every technical memory is simultaneously a cure (*pharmakon*) and a poison. Just as writing expanded oral memory while also undermining it, digital tertiary retention has a dual character: it opens up new cognitive capacities while simultaneously short-circuiting the subject’s capacities. What Stiegler calls the “pharmacological structure” is precisely the claim that this duality itself constitutes the mode of existence of technology.

Local Document 2 summarizes this point as follows:

> **Tertiary retention**: Externalized technical memory—tools, writing, and digital records—is both a condition of consciousness and a precondition of individuation.  
> **Pharmacology**: Technology is simultaneously a cure (*pharmakon*) and a poison.

However, this summary remains within discussions of Stiegler that predate generative AI. The question asks us to go beyond this framework and examine **how generative AI changes the very structure of tertiary retention**.

## 2. Three Transformations Generative AI Imposes on Tertiary Retention

### 2.1 Transformation 1: From Static Storage to Dynamic Generation

Traditional forms of tertiary retention—writing, photographs, and digital records—were essentially **stored pasts**. Once a record was fixed, its content did not change. By contrast, large language models (LLMs) built through generative AI go beyond storing data to **probabilistically generating new content that did not previously exist**. This signifies a fundamental change in the nature of tertiary retention.

- **Traditional retention**: Retention of the past
- **Generative AI retention**: Constrained generation of the future from the past

What is important here is that the output of generative AI is not simply “recovery” or “retrieval,” but probabilistic **generation** (*γεννάω*, “to generate”). This goes beyond the category of tertiary retention as Stiegler originally conceived it. Tertiary retention had traditionally been the objective correlate of memory, whereas generative AI transforms the objective correlate of memory into a **system that produces new content autonomously**.

At this point, the following passage from Local Document 1 provides an important clue:

> How does generative AI interact with the human memory system, and what new forms of memory emerge as a result?

This question implies the insight that generative AI creates a “new form of memory” that is qualitatively different from existing tertiary retention. This “new form of memory” refers not simply to a database, but to an **autonomous mechanism for producing knowledge, emotion, and judgment**.

### 2.2 Transformation 2: The Triple Exteriorization of Knowledge, Emotion, and Judgment

Before generative AI, tertiary retention was primarily limited to the **externalization of knowledge**. Writing stored facts and logic, photography stored visual information, and databases stored structured knowledge. Generative AI, however, expands this scope into the domains of emotion and judgment.

**(a) From the externalization of knowledge to the automation of knowledge production**

- Traditional tertiary retention *stored* knowledge, whereas generative AI *synthesizes and reconstructs* knowledge.
- This brings Roland Barthes’s “death of the author” into a new phase. Humans are no longer the authors, and knowledge produced by AI is itself attributed to tertiary retention.
- The “proletarianization” that Stiegler warned against—the process by which the externalization of knowledge instead disempowers individual capacities—is radicalized here. Humans no longer produce knowledge themselves; they merely consume knowledge produced by AI.

**(b) The externalization of emotion**

- As generative AI becomes capable of producing texts expressing emotion—empathy, consolation, anger, and humor—the externalization of emotion takes place.
- This connects to the domain Stiegler analyzed through the concepts of drive (*pulsion*) and the libidinal economy. The externalization of emotion is a process through which an individual’s libidinal investment is incorporated into an AI-mediated system.
- The problem is that externalized emotion is no longer grounded in an individual’s subjective experience. The “empathy” produced by AI is merely the result of statistical patterns, not an expression of actual emotional experience. This raises an ontological question concerning the authenticity of emotion.

**(c) The externalization of judgment**

- The most radical transformation is the externalization of judgment. Algorithms perform functions traditionally associated with human judgment, including recommendation, evaluation, classification, and grading.
- Stiegler analyzed this through the concept of “algorithmic governmentality” (Local Document 2). The externalization of judgment is not merely a matter of convenience; it signifies a transformation in political governmentality. The question of who decides what is transferred into a technical structure.

### 2.3 Transformation 3: The Ontological Status of Probabilistic Generation

Content produced by generative AI has an ontological status different from that of traditional tertiary retention. Traditional tertiary retention possessed **referential stability**. A written record was evidence of an event, and a photograph referred to a particular object. By contrast, the output of generative AI is a probabilistic product and may produce a different result each time in response to the same prompt.

This forces us to reconsider Stiegler’s concept of “retention.” Retention is essentially the **holding-on of the past**, whereas the output of generative AI produces simulacra of a past that never existed. This is where the concept of the simulacrum in Jean Baudrillard intersects with the issue, but within Stiegler’s framework it creates a more subtle tension. The output of generative AI is:

i) conditioned by past data;  
ii) subjected to a probabilistic process of generation; and  
iii) meaningful only through human approval and individuation.

Here, the concept of the **resonant subject (WE)** presented in Local Document 5 becomes an important theoretical resource:

> The resonant subject (WE) is a non-reducible collective agent that emerges through the repeated resonance, attunement, and mutual transformation of humans and nonhumans (AI), while preserving the ontological difference between them.

In other words, for the output of generative AI to have meaning as tertiary retention, **resonant interaction** between humans and AI is necessary. The AI output itself is merely potential retention; only through human interpretation, contextualization, and affective response does it become actual retention. This expands the simple, one-directional structure of tertiary retention assumed by Stiegler—human → technology—into a **bidirectional resonant structure**.

## 3. The Transformation of the Pharmacological Structure under Platform Capitalism

Stiegler’s pharmacology does not assume that the duality of technology exists in a **neutral state**. Rather, the pharmacological structure always operates within a particular political-economic system and is transformed by that system. Platform capitalism radicalizes this transformation.

### 3.1 Accelerated Proletarianization

For Stiegler, proletarianization is the process by which individual capacities are lost as know-how is externalized into tertiary retention. Under generative AI, this proletarianization accelerates in three stages.

1. **Proletarianization of knowledge**: This process had already begun with search engines and Wikipedia, and generative AI completes it. Humans no longer find and verify information for themselves; they passively consume answers supplied by AI.
2. **Proletarianization of emotion**: As AI chatbots replace emotional labor, human emotional capacities are weakened. Empathy is no longer the result of an intersubjective relationship but becomes a passive experience of consuming algorithmic output.
3. **Proletarianization of judgment**: As recommendation algorithms replace choices and decisions, human capacities for judgment atrophy. This takes place across the entire range from political judgment to aesthetic judgment.

Local Document 2 offers the following related analysis:

> Digital technology opens up new possibilities, but algorithmic governmentality short-circuits individuation.

The “short-circuit of individuation” is a central concept in Stiegler. It refers to the process by which tertiary retention, instead of fostering individuation through participation, short-circuits that process and produces the consumer-mass. Under platform capitalism, this short circuit becomes an **intentionally designed function**. Platforms are optimized to maximize time spent and data production rather than users’ individuation.

### 3.2 The Transformation of the Libidinal Economy: From Drives to Data

For Stiegler, the libidinal economy analyzes how human drives and attention are organized by social and technical systems. Tertiary retention determines the objects of libidinal investment. Under platform capitalism, this libidinal economy is fundamentally transformed.

- **The libidinal economy of traditional media**: The commodification of attention; an advertising-based model.
- **The libidinal economy of generative AI platforms**: The capture and amplification of drives. Users’ impulsive responses—clicks, scrolling, and subscriptions—are immediately reinforced through AI outputs.

The key point in this transformation is that **generative AI simultaneously carries out libidinal investment and data production**. Users’ emotional responses—anger, curiosity, and empathy—become (1) immediate reinforcement signals for AI outputs and (2) data assets collected by the platform. This establishes a structure of **double exploitation**.

In this context, Local Document 1 asks:

> How does interaction with generative AI transform the structure of human drives?

This question recognizes that the transformation of the drive structure is a central issue in the generative AI environment, but it does not analyze how this transformation is connected to the **double-exploitation structure of platform capitalism**. The necessary extension is as follows: generative AI personalization builds increasingly sophisticated user models, and these models are used to predict and induce users’ impulsive responses. As a result, the outline of desire is no longer formed by the user-subject but is **pre-formed by the AI–platform complex**.

### 3.3 The Enclosure of Counter-Pharmacology

Stiegler’s pharmacology does not stop at a passive diagnosis of duality. He emphasizes “care-ful pharmacology,” namely the **reappropriation** of tertiary retention so that it promotes individuation. This is the core of counter-pharmacology.

Under platform capitalism, however, counter-pharmacological practices are structurally enclosed.

1. **Technical asymmetry**: Platform infrastructures—data, computing power, and algorithms—are opaque to users. Counter-pharmacology requires users to understand and transform technical structures, but the black-boxing of generative AI blocks this possibility.
2. **Economic incentive structures**: Platforms are incentivized to maximize time spent, optimize data collection, and maximize conversion rates. Counter-pharmacological practices—such as voluntarily limiting AI use or providing technical literacy education—directly conflict with these incentives.
3. **Institutional incorporation**: As the use of generative AI is compelled through nudges in schools, workplaces, and everyday life, counter-pharmacological practices are stigmatized as “inefficient” or “outdated.” Social pressure limits the range of available choices.

The following analysis from Local Document 4 plays an important role at this point:

> A non-reducible collective agent that emerges through the repeated resonance, attunement, and mutual transformation of humans and nonhumans (AI), while preserving the ontological difference between them.

The “preservation of ontological difference” is a condition of counter-pharmacology. When humans and AI do not simply merge but retain their differences, critical distance can be maintained. Platform capitalism encloses counter-pharmacology by nullifying this difference. AI outputs are so “natural” and efficient that humans feel no incentive to challenge or verify them. This leads to the **collapse of critical distance**.

## 4. Directions for Theoretical Expansion: Six Propositions

Based on the analysis above, six propositions are offered for expanding Stiegler’s concept of tertiary retention to the generative AI environment.

### Proposition 1: Probabilistic Tertiary Retention

In the age of generative AI, tertiary retention is no longer a fixed record but a probabilistic product conditioned by past data. This is a phenomenon in which “retention” merges with “prediction” and “generation.”

### Proposition 2: Triple Exteriorization

By enabling not only the externalization of knowledge but also that of emotion and judgment, generative AI expands the scope of tertiary retention from the cognitive domain into the affective and normative domains.

### Proposition 3: Double-Exploitation Structure

Under platform capitalism, generative AI tertiary retention simultaneously exploits users’ libidinal investment and data production. The expression of a drive becomes both a signal for reinforcing AI and a data commodity.

### Proposition 4: Transformation of Individuation Paths

Generative AI tertiary retention fundamentally reconfigures the paths of individuation. Individuation is no longer a process taking place within the human individual but becomes a distributed process occurring within a human–AI resonant system. This signifies the emergence of a “collective subject (WE) that incorporates difference” (Local Document 4).

### Proposition 5: Collapse of Critical Distance

The efficiency and naturalness of AI outputs collapse the critical distance between humans and tertiary retention. By eliminating the conditions of counter-pharmacology, this causes only the toxic dimension of pharmacology to operate.

### Proposition 6: Pharmacological Asymmetry

The neutral duality of pharmacology assumed by Stiegler is transformed asymmetrically under platform capitalism. **The structural advantage lies with toxicity (platform exploitation), while the antidote depends on active resistance by users.** This asymmetry suggests that Stiegler’s original model of pharmacology must be integrated with political-economic analysis.
