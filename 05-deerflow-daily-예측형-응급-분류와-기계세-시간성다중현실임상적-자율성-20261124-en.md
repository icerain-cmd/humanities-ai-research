## Question

Suppose that a predictive triage system in an emergency department combines patients’ vital signs, medical histories, waiting times, and past treatment records to assign treatment priority and observation intensity before clinicians make their own judgments. Does the machinic turn lie in automating physicians’ judgments, or in preemptively capturing as-yet-undisclosed urgency in probabilistic scores, thereby changing the very order in which patients wait and clinicians’ attention is organized? Drawing together Heidegger’s analysis of temporality and Annemarie Mol’s theory of multiple ontologies, analyze how a single patient’s condition is negotiated when the patient’s report of pain, the nurse’s clinical sense, and the model’s risk score conflict. In particular, examine how the moment when clinicians challenge the score, or when patients minimize or exaggerate their symptoms, changes the criteria for subsequent decisions and the records themselves. Then argue whether clinical autonomy can be redefined not merely as an individual professional’s independence, but as a problem of the medical environment in which the powers to predict, wait, challenge, and reclassify are distributed.

## Predictive Emergency-Department Triage Systems and the Machinic Turn

### Locating the Problem: Preemption Beyond Automation

The machinic turn performed by predictive triage systems in emergency departments **does not consist in automating physicians’ judgments**. Rather, it consists in preemptively capturing as-yet-undisclosed urgency in probabilistic scores and thereby changing the **very order** in which patients wait and clinicians’ attention is organized. This recalls Kittler’s claim that a transformation of the machinic world (*Medienwelt*) is not merely a replacement of tools, but an event in which the very conditions of perception and judgment are reconstructed.

The concept of “technogenesis” in Khoj RAG Document 1—its paradigm shift that “goes beyond a simple instrumental extension to redefine the very concept of human creativity”—provides an important insight here. A predictive triage system does not **replace** the physician’s clinical judgment; rather, it reconstructs the space of risk **before** that judgment takes place. At the moment a patient is registered, the system combines vital signs, medical history, waiting time, and past treatment records to produce a score. Before the physician has even seen the patient, that score **assigns the intensity of observation and the order of treatment**.

This is a machinic turn in the Kittlerian sense because the **conditions of decision-making** have shifted from the cognitive subject—the physician—to algorithmic infrastructure. The physician still makes the final judgment, but that judgment occurs within an environment already structured by the score. The score is not merely a reference point; it functions as the baseline to which every subsequent clinical action must either **respond** or **raise an objection**.

### Heidegger’s Analysis of Temporality: Anticipation (*Vorlaufen*) and Waiting (*Warten*)

Heidegger’s analysis of temporality illuminates this problem more precisely. In *Being and Time*, Heidegger analyzes the temporality of Dasein through **anticipation (*Vorlaufen*)**—running ahead toward death as one’s ownmost possibility. Here, anticipation is not simply prediction; it is the way in which an **as-yet-unrealized possibility structures the mode of being in the present**.

Predictive triage systems in emergency departments algorithmize this anticipation. The risk score assigned to a patient by the system brings “as-yet-undisclosed urgency” into the present. A patient may not yet have suffered cardiac arrest, but when the system marks that possibility as having an 87 percent probability, this **future possibility determines the present order of treatment**.

The central points of a Heideggerian analysis are as follows:

1. **The distinction between anticipation and prediction**: Heidegger’s anticipation (*Vorlaufen*) is an existential attitude that entails a transformation in one’s mode of being, whereas the system’s prediction is a probabilistic expression of calculable risk. The system presents the future not as an **open possibility**, but as a **statistical necessity**. This is the ontological tension inherent in the algorithmization of anticipation.

2. **The structuring of waiting**: In the emergency department, patients’ waiting is no longer random. The system’s score causes some patients to **wait less** and others to **wait longer**. It distributes waiting time in proportion to risk. This is a concrete operation of what Heidegger describes as “world-time”: time is organized as an order of waiting.

3. **Anticipation of death and emergency**: Emergency-department prediction preemptively occupies the most extreme possibilities—death and severe deterioration. Before patients have even reported symptoms, the system has already calculated their chances of survival. This is an **operationally concretized** form, within medical infrastructure, of Heidegger’s being-toward-death (*Sein zum Tode*).

### Annemarie Mol’s Theory of Multiple Ontologies: When One Patient Exists in Multiple Ways

Annemarie Mol’s *The Body Multiple: Ontology in Medical Practice* (2002) offers a decisive insight for this analysis. Mol shows that in hospitals, a single disease—atherosclerosis, for example—is **present in different modes of existence across different practices**, including clinical examinations, pathology, patients’ experiences, and medical records. A patient’s condition is not a single object but a **multiple reality** enacted and constituted across various practices.

Applying this framework to predictive triage systems in emergency departments makes the following analysis possible:

**The conflict and negotiation among three realities:**

| Domain of reality | Technical mode | Temporality | Mode of existence |
|-----------|-----------|--------|-----------|
| **The model’s risk score** | Algorithmic calculation, probabilistic estimation | Future-oriented anticipation (prediction) | Statistical risk |
| **The patient’s report of pain** | Linguistic/bodily expression | Present-oriented manifestation | Subjective suffering |
| **The nurse’s clinical sense** | Experience- and intuition-based perception | Continuous observational time | Contextualized bodily condition |

These three realities each possess a different ontology and temporality. **The model’s score** represents risk inferred through comparison with statistical populations—a future possibility. **The patient’s report** represents present suffering—immediate presence. **The nurse’s sense** represents contextual judgment that detects change within temporal continuity—a changing condition.

When these three realities **conflict**—for example, when the model assigns a score of 4, indicating low risk, while the patient reports extreme pain and the nurse feels that “something is wrong”—what occurs is not merely the detection of an error. It is an **ontological negotiation over which reality will take precedence**.

For Mol, the outcome of such negotiations is not the disclosure of a single truth—for example, that “the patient was actually at risk.” Rather, it is the temporary **dominance of one practical order over another**. When a patient is reclassified on the basis of a nurse’s judgment, this does not mean that the “correct” reality has been discovered. Instead, a particular practice—clinical sense—has temporarily **reconfigured** another practice—algorithmic assessment.

### The Mechanisms of Challenge and Reclassification: The Mutability of Records

When clinicians challenge the system’s score, or when patients minimize or exaggerate their symptoms, what matters is not merely the occurrence of a discrepancy. What matters is **how that discrepancy changes the criteria for subsequent decisions and the records themselves**.

**First, a challenge changes the system’s learning environment.** Predictive triage systems can adjust how they generate scores through real-time feedback. If a physician disregards a score and assigns a higher level of observation, after which the patient is in fact confirmed to be in serious condition, this feedback can modify the system’s future predictions. This is the concrete operation of what Khoj Document 2 describes as “a network of relations in which knowledge is jointly constituted by humans and technology.” Knowledge—the risk score—is not fixed; it is reconstructed through the practice of challenging it.

**Second, the patient’s expression of symptoms selectively alters the system’s input data.** The more a patient exaggerates or minimizes their pain, the more subjective bias enters the vital-sign and medical-history data that are input into the system. This creates an interesting paradox: although the system attempts to predict risk on the basis of objective data—numerical values—its **inputs themselves are mediated by the patient’s subjective report and clinicians’ practices of documentation**, and therefore can never be completely objective.

**Third, reclassification is not simply a change in the score, but an ontological transition.** When a patient is reclassified from Category 3 to Category 1, it is not only the score that changes. **The patient’s mode of existence changes.** A Category 1 patient becomes an entity entitled to faster treatment, more intensive observation, and more aggressive intervention. In Mol’s terms, reclassification moves the patient’s reality—or ontology—into a different practical order.

### The Environmental Redefinition of Clinical Autonomy

In its traditional sense, clinical autonomy is understood as **the independence of an individual physician to make treatment decisions according to their professional judgment**. This is an individualistic and cognitivist conception of autonomy. However, in an emergency department that has adopted predictive triage systems, this concept needs to be fundamentally reconsidered.

**If autonomy is redefined as a matter of environmental distribution of authority:**

1. **The authority to predict**: The anticipation performed by the system is an **authority** to define future risk and determine the order of treatment. This authority is exercised by statistical algorithms and **precedes** the individual physician’s judgment.

2. **The authority to make patients wait**: The system’s score determines who must wait and for how long. Waiting is not a passive condition, but the result of an allocation of authority—that is, a structural condition in which some patients must wait for the sake of others.

3. **The authority to challenge**: The ability of clinicians to challenge the system’s score becomes a core component of autonomy. Yet this authority is **not distributed equally among all clinicians**. Nurses and physicians possess different capacities to challenge the system, and the authority of senior physicians differs from that of residents.

4. **The authority to reclassify**: Ultimately, who is permitted to decide on reclassification? This reflects the distribution of authority within the medical environment. Reclassification is not merely a change in a score, but **the authority to determine which reality will take precedence**.

The distribution of these four forms of authority reveals the **environmental conditions** that traditional concepts of autonomy have overlooked. A physician’s “autonomous judgment” may in fact be the outcome of an environment in which particular authorities have been allocated to particular actors. When a physician disregards the system’s score and follows their own clinical judgment, this may be because they occupy a position in which they can **effectively exercise the authority to challenge**, not because their judgment is inherently superior.

The definition in Khoj Document 3 identifies this point precisely: “In the technogenetic era, the structuring of knowledge is an engineering of knowledge organized not around what one knows, but around how things are distinguished, in what form they are transferred, and under what conditions they can be reused.” Clinical autonomy should likewise be redefined: autonomy is not an individual’s inner capacity, but a question of how the authorities to predict, wait, challenge, and reclassify are distributed within the medical environment, and who can exercise those authorities under what conditions.

### Conclusion: The Ontology of Negotiation and Its Political Implications

The machinic turn of predictive triage systems in emergency departments does not consist in automating physicians’ judgments. It consists in **reorganizing the order of waiting and attention through the preemption of urgency**. This transformation can be analyzed on two levels: the algorithmization of Heideggerian anticipation and the conflict among the multiple realities described by Annemarie Mol.

Heidegger enables us to critically illuminate how these systems bring future possibilities into the present—an operational form of *Vorlaufen*. Mol, meanwhile, enables us to analyze concretely how a single patient’s condition is negotiated when three realities—the model’s score, the patient’s report, and the nurse’s sense—come into conflict.

The ontology of this negotiation is ultimately **political**. Whose reality will take precedence? Who has the authority to challenge? Who can decide on reclassification? These questions reveal the power structure of the medical environment. Redefining clinical autonomy as a matter of the environmental distribution of authority makes it possible to analyze this structure and, further, opens the way to normative questions about **which authorities should be distributed, and how**, when predictive technologies are introduced into clinical settings.

**Concluding argument**: In an environment shaped by predictive triage systems, clinical autonomy is no longer an individual psychological attribute or a form of professional independence. It is a problem of **environmental distribution**, organized along four axes: the authority to predict—who defines future risk; the authority to make patients wait—who waits and for how long; the authority to challenge—who can resist the system’s judgment; and the authority to reclassify—who can change a patient’s mode of existence. This redefinition allows autonomy to be understood not as an individual privilege, but as a constitutive principle of a medical environment in which technology, humans, and institutions are entangled.
