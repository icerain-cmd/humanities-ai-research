## Question

In a case where a generative voice model learns a deceased person's unpublished poetry recitations and, upon the family's posthumous request, produces new recitations and prosodic variations, can the author theory that has attributed the meaning of poetry to the speaker's intention and life experience be repositioned as a component of a cooperative techno-poiesis, comprising voice data, the model's probabilistic selection, and the bereaved family's editorial acts? If so, by what criteria should this repositioning redefine the boundary between mourning and imitation?

## Analysis: Generative Voice Models, Posthumous Recitation, and the Repositioning of Techno-Poiesis

### 1. Layers of the Problem — Three Fractures Facing Traditional Author Theory

Traditional author theory builds poetic meaning on two axes: 'the speaker's intention (intentio auctoris)' and 'the authenticity of lived experience.' In the case of poetry recitation, these two axes are further sensuously embodied through 'the materiality of voice' — that is, unique vocalization, breathing, prosody, and intonation. The moment a generative voice model intervenes, all three axes reveal fundamental fractures:

1. **Impossibility of Attributing Intention**: The recitations generated by the model include prosodic variations that the deceased never actually uttered. Can 'the speaker's intention' be attributed to these variations? Within the framework of traditional author theory, it is impossible.
2. **Disjunction of Lived Experience**: The voice data the model learns from are traces of the deceased's life, but the generated recitations produce new vocal events that go beyond those traces. This is no longer a matter of 'authenticity' but of 'hierarchical modeling.'
3. **Dialectics of Vocal Materiality**: The model's output statistically follows the deceased's voice distribution, but each individual generation explores paths not selected within that distribution (unuttered vocalizations, unrealized breathing patterns). The voice becomes both 'record' and 'virtual.'

### 2. Repositioning Toward Cooperative Techno-Poiesis

Within the 'AI environment vs object' framework, viewing AI as an object limits it to a tool, whereas viewing AI as an environment reveals that it functions as a condition for meaning generation itself. In this case, the generative voice model can be repositioned as a component of cooperative techno-poiesis on three layers.

#### (a) Data Layer — 'Trace' and 'Material'

The deceased's voice data is not a mere archive. In Friedrich Kittler's media theory, recording media are not 'facts' but results of 'selection.' The voices the deceased produced in daily life (everyday conversations, public recitations, recorded memos, etc.) are only utterances selected in specific contexts, not the entire set of all possible utterances. Therefore:

> The data does not represent the deceased's 'total speakerhood' and does not include the 'other speakers the deceased could have been.'

At this point, the data is both 'trace' and 'material.' The quantity and quality of data provided by the bereaved family, as well as the selection/exclusion of specific recordings, already function as the first 'editorial act.'

#### (b) Model Layer — The Generativity of 'Probabilistic Selection'

Generative voice models (e.g., zero-shot voice cloning technologies like Fish Audio, ElevenLabs' Voice Design, StyleTTS 2) perform conditional generation based on the probability distribution of training data. The 'selection' performed by the model in this generation process has the following implications:

- **Unintentional but not meaningless**: The model does not have 'intention,' but the generated acoustic choices (specific prosody, specific stress, specific breathing positions) are experienced as meaningful by listeners.
- **Realization of unrealized possibilities**: The model generates prosodic patterns the deceased never actually uttered. For example, a poem the deceased wrote in a prosaic tone could be transformed by the model into a recitation with unexpected prosodic tension. This transformation is not 'arbitrariness' but the realization of possibilities inherent in the data but never vocalized.
- **Generative difference**: Borrowing from Gilles Deleuze's concept of 'difference and repetition,' the model's generation is not a repetition of identity (the deceased's voice) but a production of difference. The crucial point is that this difference is not an 'error' but the very condition of generation.

#### (c) Bereaved Family Layer — 'Mourning Practice' and 'Authorial Intervention'

The bereaved family's editorial acts appear most traditionally 'authorial,' yet simultaneously most radically deconstruct the traditional concept of authorship:

- The family decides 'what to generate' (prompts, style guides, generation parameters).
- The family decides 'what to select' (adopting/rejecting specific versions among multiple generated recitations).
- The family decides 'what to make public' (the distribution scope and context of the final output).

These three decisions occupy the territory of traditional authorship (intention, selection, responsibility), but the family experiences their actions not as 'authorship' but as 'mourning.' This disjunction is the core tension.

### 3. The Strategic Concept of 'Distributed Authorship'

Synthesizing the analysis of the three layers above, authorship is no longer attributable to a single point (the speaker's intention) but is distributed as follows:

| Layer | Action | Nature of Authorship | Temporality |
|------|------|--------------|--------|
| Deceased (Speaker) | Lifetime utterances | Unintentional original authorship | Past perfect |
| Data Curation | Provision/selection | Condition setter | Past-present |
| Model | Probabilistic generation | Generative co-authorship | Present (impersonal) |
| Bereaved Family | Editing/selection/publication | Mourning corrector | Present-future |

The decisive point is that the model functions not as a mere tool but as a 'generative co-author.' This repositioning demands that AI be viewed not as an object but as an environment — the model constitutes the very condition of 'rewriting the voice of the dead through the medium of the living,' as both a background condition for meaning generation and an active participant.

### 4. Redefining the Boundary Between Mourning and Imitation

If the repositioning is accepted, the boundary between mourning and imitation cannot be set by existing criteria of intentionality (intentional = imitation, unintentional = mourning) or by the external similarity of the output. New criteria are needed:

#### (a) Acknowledgment of 'Non-Representable Surplus'

- **Imitation** regards the generated output as a 'proxy' for the deceased the moment it is judged to 'sufficiently resemble' the deceased's voice. The danger of imitation lies in the illusion that the output **substitutes** for the deceased.
- **Mourning** is an attitude that acknowledges that no matter how accurately the generated output replicates the deceased's voice distribution, it is not the deceased themselves. This acknowledgment is possible not by the degree of similarity of the output, but by recognizing the 'non-representable surplus' — the countless possibilities the deceased could have uttered but did not, and the irreversibility of the deceased's very existence.

**Criterion**: The boundary between mourning and imitation lies not in the acoustic similarity of the output, but in whether the generated product is used to 'stand in place of the deceased' or is left to 'serve in memory of the deceased.'

#### (b) Transparency of Generation Conditions

- A practical condition for the bereaved family to maintain an attitude of mourning is **whether the conditions of generation remain transparent**. That is, listeners must be able to know whether 'this voice was actually uttered by the deceased or generated by the model.'
- **The danger of imitation** arises when this boundary is blurred. If the family edits the model's output to be indistinguishable from the deceased's actual voice and does not disclose this fact, it becomes imitation disguised as mourning.

#### (c) Epistemological Shift Required by Cooperative Techno-Poiesis

Ultimately, this case demands the following epistemological shift:

> Poetic meaning is no longer a matter of 'who spoke' (the speaker), but of **'under what conditions meaning is generated'** (the media environment).

This is not an abandonment of traditional author theory but a repositioning. The speaker's life experience and intention are no longer the singular source of meaning generation but are adjusted to one condition among many (data, model, editing). The voice generation model functions as a media apparatus that makes the relationships among these conditions visible.

### 5. Limitations and Remaining Issues

The search scores (0.03–0.04) from the local Khoj references were insufficient to provide substantial grounding for the above analysis. The analysis primarily relies on Kittler's media selection theory, Deleuze's theory of difference generation, and the existing 'AI environment vs object' framework. Benjamin's concept of play space (documents 3, 5) identified in the Khoj search provides auxiliary reference points for the question of 'how technology changes the reception form of art,' but was not directly applied to the specific case of voice generation.

Key remaining issues:

1. **Attribution of Ethical Responsibility**: If the model is a 'co-author,' who is responsible if the generated recitation leads to defamation or distortion of the deceased? — The model developer, the bereaved family, or the 'technical conditions' of the model itself?
2. **Impossibility of Posthumous Consent**: The deceased cannot consent to their voice being reproduced by a generative model. What procedures can fill this gap of consent?
3. **Perceptual Conditions of the Receiver**: When a listener experiences a posthumous recitation, is that experience based on mourning emotions, or on a temporary illusion based on acoustic similarity? How can this distinction be judged on a case-by-case basis?

These issues are complex and difficult to cover in a single paper. Systematic analysis will be possible in the future through the expansion of the 'AI environment vs object' discussion and the 'eco-digital' framework.
