# Media Aesthetics in the Age of Technogenesis: Merleau-Ponty's Embodied Perception and the Bodyless Mediation of Generative AI

## 1. Problem Setting

Generative AI simulates human symbolic activity by receiving text prompts and producing language and images. In this process, there is clearly no human sensory-motor process between prompt and output. The user types on a keyboard and stares at a screen, but the intermediate process by which input is transformed into output consists of probabilistic calculations unrelated to bodily sensation. Can Merleau-Ponty's concept of embodied perception, then, explain this phenomenon? This question is not simply a cognitive-scientific one of "Can AI perceive?" but rather asks how the core phenomenological claim that meaning, language, and image are grounded in the body is put to the test in the process of technical generation.

## 2. Merleau-Ponty's Embodied Perception and the Genesis of Meaning

Merleau-Ponty's concept of perception is a direct critique of Cartesian mind-body dualism. Perception is not the calculation of a representation of an object within the mind, but an event in which the body engages with the world. In *Phenomenology of Perception*, the body is not merely a physical organ but an "intentional object" and the subject of "I can (je peux)." The meaning of things, the depth of space, the texture of colors arise within the body's motor capacities and sensory engagement. For example, a chair does not gain meaning because its form is visually recognized; it gains meaning through its entanglement with the body's possibility of sitting. The body is an "opening" onto the world and the foundation of all symbolic activity.

From this perspective, language is not a medium of clues or representations. Speech is a sounding gesture. The meaning of speech cannot be separated from the intonation, rhythm, and attitude inscribed in the body. In Merleau-Ponty's later ontology, the body is understood as an element of "flesh." Flesh is the medium in which my body, other bodies, and the world intertwine. The "reversibility" of the touching hand and the touched hand shows that the body can be both subject and object. The one who sees the world is at the same time seen by the world; the one who speaks is at the same time spoken by the world. Without this reversibility, the meaning of being cannot be formed.

Thus, for Merleau-Ponty, perception is not a synthesis of sensory data or the result of information processing, but the existential movement of the living body. The perceiving subject first and foremost contacts the world through the body, and in that contact, the world acquires meaning. If a being does not have a body, it cannot be said to perceive. No matter how sophisticated the representations it processes, if it lacks contact with the "living world," its operation is not perception but calculation.

## 3. The Mediating Structure of Generative AI and the Absence of Bodily Sensation

Generative AI is a probabilistic model trained on large corpora of text and image datasets. When a user inputs text, the model predicts the most appropriate tokens based on context and generates output. In the case of image generation, the text prompt is transformed into a vector in latent space, which is then decoded back into an image. This process is structurally different from human perception. Human perception is a motivated, experiential process in which the body's sensory organs, motor plans, and emotional responses are simultaneously intertwined, whereas AI generation is a non-experiential computation that takes place outside the body.

Of course, on the user's side, the body is involved. There is finger movement when writing a prompt, and visual sensation occurs when viewing the output image. However, this embodiment belongs to the human user, not to the AI. The absence of bodily sensation between prompt and output becomes problematic precisely at this point. The AI does not "understand" the relationship the human body has with the world. It merely probabilistically recombines linguistically and visually learned patterns from a distribution. In Merleau-Ponty's terms, the AI does not possess the body's "I can," nor does it have an entanglement of "flesh" with the world. Therefore, the meaning produced by the AI is not existential meaning based on the body's intentionality, but functional significance within a bodyless system.

This absence poses a substantial challenge to theories of perception. We witness the phenomenon of AI appearing to understand text and generate images. Its outputs are often indistinguishable from those made by humans. Is Merleau-Ponty's claim that the essence of perception or meaning lies in the body correct, then? Or is this counterevidence that meaning can be generated without a body? To answer this question, it is necessary to examine the technical structure of generative AI not merely as a tool or medium, but in connection with Simondon's philosophy of technology.

## 4. Two Levels of Explainability

If we explain generative AI using Merleau-Ponty's concepts, it can be divided into two levels. The first is the level of human experience. AI, as a technical object inserted into human perceptual life, can become an extension of the body, like a cane or a car. Merleau-Ponty says that a cane becomes a sensory organ for a blind person, and a car being driven becomes part of the driver's body. Similarly, generative AI can function as a kind of "transparent medium" in the human creative process. The user absorbs the AI's output as their own expression, and prompt thinking becomes like habitual bodily thinking. In this case, the concept of embodied perception is useful for explaining the phenomenology of the AI user experience.

The second is the level of the AI's own operation. At this level, Merleau-Ponty's concepts cannot explain AI. AI is not an experiencing body, nor is it an intentional subject. AI generation does not arise from the "flesh" of a body-subject, but from sampling a distribution. Therefore, Merleau-Ponty's concept of embodied perception is not a theory that explains AI as a "perceiving being," but rather a critical lens that reveals the essential limitations of AI's bodyless mediation. Generative AI can produce a virtual semblance of perception, but that semblance is not the reality of perception. This is the core answer to the question. AI can explain how humans perceive the output, but it cannot explain how AI itself perceives.

Then why does the output of AI appear so "perceptual"? This question is closely related to the evolution of technical objects. Here, Simondon's concept of concretization provides crucial insight.

## 5. Simondon's Concept of Concretization and Two Interpretations of Generative AI

Simondon distinguishes between abstract and concrete structures of technical objects. Early technical objects are abstract objects in which each part functions individually and depends on the external environment. In contrast, a concretized technical object reaches a stage where functions are structurally fused and the object forms its own "associated milieu." For example, the more the internal combustion engine integrates functions such as cooling, fuel supply, and combustion into a single structure, the more it internalizes its dependence on the environment. For Simondon, technical evolution is a process moving from the dispersion of functions to the condensation of functions.

Applying this criterion to generative AI allows for two opposing interpretations.

First, generative AI can be seen as a new stage of concretization. Latest models have multimodal structures that process text and images in a single latent space. The combination of language models and image generation models, where a text prompt directly shapes the image structure, demonstrates functional integration. In particular, the synthetic data feedback loop builds an autonomous cycle in which the model uses its own produced data as training data again. This appears to be a transition from an abstract stage dependent on external data to a concretization stage that uses self-produced data as its associated milieu. From this perspective, the absence of bodily sensation is not a problem. Because a concretized technical object does not become like a body; rather, it internalizes its own environment without the external condition of a body. Generative AI can be interpreted not as a "perception technology without sensation," but as a "meaning-generation technology operating without perception" that can continue its own evolution.

Second, however, the synthetic data feedback loop can be read as a regressive closed loop. As is known, when a model retrains on self-generated data, a "model collapse" phenomenon occurs where the distribution gradually becomes biased and diversity decreases. The rich distribution of initial human data converges to a simple average through repeated reproduction of synthetic data. This is not Simondonian concretization, but a regression in which the technical object's associated milieu loses contact with the world and heads toward self-enclosure. Concretization is a process where functions adapt to the environment and become internally integrated, but the AI's synthetic data loop blocks contact with the natural and social world and repeats only the model's self-reproduction. This is not an evolution that updates structure in response to environmental change, but a cycle that consumes its own outputs and homogenizes.

These two interpretations are not contradictory. On one hand, the technical structure of generative AI shows a tendency toward concretization through the internalization of the associated milieu; on the other hand, when that associated milieu is purely artificial and self-referential, the evolutionary meaning of concretization is lost. Simondon saw that a technical object truly concretizes when it communicates with the world through its associated milieu. But in the synthetic data feedback loop of generative AI, the associated milieu is not the world but the model's own output. Therefore, this loop becomes not an opening to the world, but a disconnection from it. That is, the absence of bodily sensation initially appears as a condition of technical autonomy, but over time becomes the cause of regressive closure.

## 6. The Phenomenological Significance of Model Collapse

Model collapse is both a statistical phenomenon and a phenomenological symptom. Borrowing Merleau-Ponty's later concepts, perception is a reversible game between the sensing and the sensed within the element of "flesh." This reversibility is not mere repetition but a dynamic in which difference and indifference, opening and closing are intertwined. In contrast, the synthetic data feedback loop of generative AI reinforces self-identity without reversibility. The structure where output becomes input again does not allow for the emergence of novelty through the body's world-contact. Consequently, over time, the language and images of generative AI converge to an average, repetitive "style." This is due to the absence of sensory difference—the nourishment of meaning—perspectival variation based on bodily position, and emotional modulation.

This poses a significant challenge to theories of perception. If AI evolves through synthetic data, the claim that bodyless meaning generation is possible could have empirical support. However, model collapse suggests the opposite. Bodyless meaning generation produces remarkable results in the short term, but in a cycle that feeds only on its own products, it eventually degenerates. In other words, "meaning without sensation" is ultimately a self-depleting form, and genuine meaning is renewed only through the body's openness, that is, contact with the world. What Merleau-Ponty called "brute being (être brut)" is precisely this thickness of the sensory world. AI experiences model collapse because it loses contact with brute being and circulates only within a purely fabricated artificial nature.

Of course, this is not a rigorous empirical conclusion but a conceptual inference. The conditions for model collapse can vary depending on learning methods, data ratios, model size, etc. However, the fact that the synthetic data feedback loop theoretically forms a self-referential structure and tends to reduce the entropy of the distribution can be linked to phenomenological analysis. That is, the "absence of bodily sensation" not only indicates that AI does not perceive the world, but also shows that this absence narrows the quality and horizon of meaning in the long term.
