# Authorship in the Age of Generative AI: Tensions Between Distributed Agency and Traditional Authorship Theory

## 1. Introduction: Case Overview and Problem Setting

The case of a university freshman welcome speech—a public writing scenario—in which a generative AI produced a draft and a student council president revised it for official presentation poses a fundamental challenge to the traditional concept of authorship. In this case, the text production process is not the expression of a single human will but the result of complex interactions among the dataset (a large text corpus), the language model (a statistical pattern generator), the editing interface (a medium for revision and selection), and the human agent (the student council president). When traditional authorship theory attempts to explain this process, we face two difficulties. First, a significant portion of the text is not the product of intentional human creation but of probabilistic generation. Second, the final presenter may remain merely a 'selector' or 'editor.'

This article analyzes the tensions created by this case from the perspective of the concept of authorship after Roland Barthes and Actor-Network Theory (ANT). ## 2. The Attribution Model of Traditional Authorship and Its Limitations

### 2.1. The Author as a Subject of Intention and Signature

The modern Western concept of authorship is deeply rooted in the Enlightenment idea of the subject. The author is considered the source who intentionally creates the meaning of a text and the subject who bears legal and moral responsibility for that text. From this perspective, the fact that the student council president revised and presented an AI-generated draft allows for an interpretation that he, as the 'final decision-maker,' acquired authorship over the entire text. In other words, the act of revision itself is evidence of intentional intervention, and the act of signing through public announcement establishes authorship.

However, this interpretation faces several serious problems. First, if the student council president's revisions were limited to simple error correction or stylistic adjustments, the core structure and content of the text still depend on the AI model's statistical generation. In this case, 'intention' operates only within an extremely limited scope, and a substantial part of the text falls outside the category of intentional creation. Second, the operating mechanism of generative AI is fundamentally different from human intentional writing. AI does not understand meaning; it merely probabilistically recombinants patterns from training data. Therefore, attributing 'intention' to AI-generated text is close to a category mistake.

### 2.2. Barthes and Foucault: The Death of the Author and the Author Function

In his 1967 essay "The Death of the Author," Roland Barthes argued that the meaning of a text arises not from the author's intention but from the interaction between the reader's interpretation and the linguistic system. For Barthes, the author is not the 'origin' of the text but merely a temporary 'channel' functioning within the a priori system of language. Applying this perspective to the generative AI case, the student council president is no longer the creative source of the text but rather an 'assembler' who selectively rearranges the outputs of already existing linguistic patterns (the dataset) and algorithmic processing (the model). Barthes' logic deconstructs the traditional concept of authorship but simultaneously raises new problems. Specifically, it fails to explain the power relations and distribution of responsibility among the various actors intervening in the text production process (dataset, model, interface, human editor).

Michel Foucault, in his 1969 lecture "What is an Author?" introduced the concept of the 'author-function.' For Foucault, the author is not a psychological entity behind the text but a social construct that binds, classifies, and authorizes specific texts within discursive practice. The author-function is linked to legal and institutional mechanisms that determine the ownership, authenticity, and punishability of the text. From this perspective, the student council president's signature is not merely personal expression but a performance of the 'author-function' that confers authority and responsibility for public speech within the institutional context of the university. However, Foucault's analysis, by attributing the author-function only to human agents, overlooks the role of non-human actors (models, datasets). The era of generative AI raises the need to consider the possibility of a 'non-human author-function.'

## 3. Distributed Agency and Actor-Network Theory

### 3.1. Basic Premises of ANT: The Agency of Non-Human Actors

Actor-Network Theory, developed by Bruno Latour, Michel Callon, and John Law, explains social phenomena as heterogeneous networks of human and non-human actors. A core thesis of ANT is that agency is not monopolized by humans; technical objects, texts, institutions, etc., can also play active roles within a network. In *Reassembling the Social: An Introduction to Actor-Network Theory* (2005), Latour proposes treating humans and non-humans symmetrically through the concept of the 'actant.'

In this case, the generative AI model functions not as a mere tool but as an actor that 'suggests' the specific style and content of the text. The dataset embodies specific discursive patterns, and the editing interface structures the range of possible modifications. The student council president's choices are made within the interaction of all these actors, and the final text is less the creation of a single subject and more the result of an 'association of actants.'

### 3.2. The Network of Distributed Authorship

From an ANT perspective, the text production process in this case can be reconstructed as follows. First, the dataset acts as a 'silent co-author' containing texts from countless human authors. This dataset internalizes specific cultural and linguistic biases and fundamentally constrains the model's output. Second, the language model is a 'statistical co-author' that recombinants patterns from the dataset through probabilistic inference. The model's hyperparameters, learning algorithm, tokenizer, etc., critically influence the style and content of the output. Third, the editing interface (e.g., ChatGPT's conversational UI) acts as a 'mediating actor' that structures user input and induces specific modification methods. Fourth, the student council president functions as the 'final mediator' within the network of all these actors, but his choices are only possible within the structural constraints of the network.

This distributed agency fundamentally shakes the core premise of traditional authorship theory: 'the expression of a single will.' The meaning and responsibility of the text can no longer be attributed to a single individual but are distributed across the entire network. However, ANT has a limitation in that it does not provide normative criteria for how to evaluate and regulate this distributed responsibility.

### 3.3. Tensions Between the Barthes-Foucault Tradition and ANT

Both Barthes and Foucault deconstruct the privileged status of the human subject, yet they still place the human actor (reader, discourse community) at the center of analysis. In contrast, ANT treats humans and non-humans symmetrically, actively acknowledging the agency of technical objects. The tension between these two approaches emerges at the following points.

First, the problem of attribution of responsibility. From a Barthesian perspective, since the meaning of a text is open to interpretation by readers, the author's responsibility is relativized. However, in the context of a public announcement, the student council president still bears social responsibility for the content of the text. ANT distributes this responsibility across the entire network, but actual legal and institutional responsibility remains concentrated on human actors. This implies a discrepancy between the 'actor-network' and the 'responsibility-network.'

Second, the problem of intentionality. Barthes and Foucault do not take the author's intention as the ultimate criterion for interpreting a text, but they still take human intentional action as the starting point of analysis. ANT acknowledges effects similar to 'intentionality' in non-human actors, but this is close to a metaphorical expression. A generative AI model is a statistical machine lacking intentionality; therefore, traditional authorship theory mediated by 'intention' is difficult to apply to this case.

## 4. Simondon's Concept of Concretization and the Evolution of Generative AI

### 4.1. The Concretization Process of Technical Objects

In *On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects* (1958), Gilbert Simondon analyzes technical objects not as mere tools but as entities possessing internal coherence and self-organizing capacity. According to Simondon, technical objects evolve through a process of 'concretization.' In the early stages, a technical object is merely an abstract collection of separate components, but as concretization progresses, the components become functionally integrated and acquire new properties through interaction with the environment.

Generative AI models can be seen as an instance of this concretization process. Early language models were close to simple n-gram statistical models, but through developments in transformer architecture, attention mechanisms, and large-scale pre-training, they have evolved to increasingly internalize complex language patterns. In particular, models like the GPT series demonstrate abilities beyond simple pattern matching, such as context-dependent reasoning, style imitation, and zero-shot learning. This is close to the emergence of what Simondon calls a 'technical individual.'

### 4.2. Synthetic Data Feedback Loops and Model Collapse

However, the concretization process of generative AI entails a paradoxical risk. Recent studies report a 'model collapse' phenomenon, where a model's performance gradually degrades when it reuses its own generated synthetic data as training data. This is a kind of 'degenerative closed loop,' where the model learns from its own output, losing the diversity of the original data distribution and eventually generating only homogeneous, degraded outputs.

In this case, if the student council president revises and presents the AI-generated welcome speech, and this text is then distributed on the internet and included in future model training data, it could become an instance of the synthetic data feedback loop. That is, a text that has undergone human revision but still retains structural characteristics of AI-generated text is fed back into the dataset, potentially causing the model's outputs to gradually converge toward specific patterns.

### 4.3. Comparison of Two Interpretations: Evolution of the Technical Object vs. Degenerative Closed Loop

Simondon's concept of concretization and the phenomenon of model collapse offer contrasting interpretations of the evolution of generative AI.

According to the first interpretation (evolution of the technical object), generative AI gradually becomes a more sophisticated language model through interaction with humans. The student council president's revisions function as human feedback on the model's output, which can be utilized in the model's fine-tuning process. From this perspective, AI is not a mere tool but a technical individual that co-evolves with humans. Simondon argued that technical objects remain 'open' through human use, acquiring new functions and adaptive capacities.

According to the second interpretation (degenerative closed loop), the development of generative AI may instead lead to a self-closing cycle. When the model reuses its own output as training data, the diversity of the original data gradually disappears, and the model becomes trapped in specific patterns. This is similar to the phenomenon Simondon warned against: the 'closure' of technical objects. That is, the degeneracy that occurs when a technical object blocks interaction with the external environment and pursues only internal homogeneity.

These two interpretations raise a fundamental question about the direction of generative AI development. Does AI move toward richer linguistic diversity through interaction with humans, or does it become increasingly impoverished within self-referential closure? This question goes beyond a merely technical problem and demands philosophical reflection on the relationship between humans and technology.

## 5. Implications for Eco-Digital/Artificial Nature Research

### 5.1. New Conditions for Human-Technology Co-evolution

Eco-digital research is an approach that understands the interaction between humans, technology, and nature as a single ecosystem. From this perspective, generative AI is not a mere tool but part of a 'technical ecosystem' that reconfigures the human linguistic and cognitive environment. The student council president's case illustrates how the role of humans within this ecosystem is changing. Humans no longer function as 'creators' of text but as 'selectors' and 'mediators' of possibilities generated by AI. This fundamentally calls for a reexamination of traditional understandings of human creativity.

The concept of 'artificial nature' refers to the 'second nature' created by technology. Texts produced by generative AI are no longer simple reproductions of 'natural' language but a form of 'artificial language' created by statistical patterns. This artificial language gradually influences human language use, forming new styles, modes of expression, and thought patterns. This is analogous to how an invasive species interacts with native species in a natural ecosystem, creating a new ecological balance.

### 5.2. Redefining Authorship and Distributing Responsibility

This case demands a fundamental redefinition of the concept of authorship. If traditional authorship theory is based on human intention and signature, then authorship in the age of generative AI should be understood as a 'network of distributed agency.' Within this network, human actors still play an important role, but they are no longer the sole actors. The dataset, model, interface, and human editor each function as 'partial authors' contributing to text production.

However, the problem of responsibility remains an unresolved challenge. ANT distributes agency but does not provide normative criteria for distributing responsibility. In legal and institutional contexts, responsibility remains concentrated on human actors. This creates a tension between the 'distribution of agency' and the 'concentration of responsibility.' Resolving this tension requires a new type of 'distributed responsibility ethics.'

### 5.3. Evolution of Technical Objects and the Role of Humans

Simondon's concept of concretization emphasizes that technical objects are not merely passive tools but active subjects of evolution. However, the direction of this evolution is determined by human intervention. The student council president's act of revision goes beyond simple error correction, performing a function of 'value assessment' and 'selective amplification' on the model's output. This suggests that humans can still exert significant influence over the direction of technical object evolution.

At the same time, the synthetic data feedback loop warns that human intervention may not always yield positive results. When human choices repeatedly amplify specific patterns, the model risks losing diversity and falling into a degenerative closed loop. This implies that the co-evolution of humans and technology does not always proceed in a progressive direction; it may instead lead to a mutually oppressive cycle.

## 6. Conclusion: A Comprehensive Understanding of Authorship in the Age of Generative AI

The above analysis shows that the case of a university freshman welcome speech written by generative AI creates complex tensions among traditional authorship theory, the concept of authorship after Barthes, and Actor-Network Theory. Traditional authorship theory is insufficient to explain the distributed actions of the dataset, model, and editing interface that intervened in the text production process. The concepts of authorship from Barthes and Foucault deconstruct the privileged status of the human subject but do not fully consider the role of non-human actors. Simondon's concept of concretization and the phenomenon of model collapse present two contrasting interpretations of the evolution of generative AI. The interpretation as evolution of the technical object shows the possibility of AI developing into more sophisticated language models through interaction with humans, while the interpretation as a degenerative closed loop warns of the risk of self-referential cycles. These two interpretations are not mutually exclusive and can coexist in reality. That is, under certain conditions evolution may occur, while under other conditions degeneration may occur.

From the perspective of eco-digital and artificial nature research, this issue demands a fundamental reflection on the conditions of human-technology co-evolution. Generative AI is part of a technical ecosystem that reconfigures the human linguistic environment, and within this ecosystem, the role of humans is changing from creator to selector and mediator. This change requires a new ethical and philosophical framework for the redefinition of authorship, the distribution of responsibility, and the direction of technical object evolution.

In conclusion, authorship in the age of generative AI should be understood not as the expression of a single human will, but as a distributed effect created by the heterogeneous network of human and non-human actors. This understanding requires overcoming the limitations of traditional authorship theory while simultaneously reconstructing the issues of responsibility and ethics in a new way. This is not merely an academic debate but a practical task that fundamentally reexamines how we coexist with technology.
