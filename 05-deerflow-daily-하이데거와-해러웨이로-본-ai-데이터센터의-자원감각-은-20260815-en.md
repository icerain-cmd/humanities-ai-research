## Question

As a generative AI data center on the outskirts of Phoenix, Arizona draws on local water resources for cooling, applying both Heidegger’s “revealing as resource” and Haraway’s “situated knowledges” together: How does the immaterial metaphor of the “cloud” render invisible the water, electricity, and sensory labor of local residents, and how should criticism that resists this invisibility redescribe the data center not merely as an environmental polluter but as a dispositif of perception and responsibility?

## Analysis Results

# The Materiality of the Cloud, or the Perception-Politics of Invisibilization

## — A Conceptual Redescription of the Phoenix Data Center through Heidegger and Haraway

## I. Setting the Problem: The Gap between Metaphor and Materiality

The question begins with a metaphor. **The “cloud”** — what exactly does this metaphor, which names computing infrastructure, do? Something light, boundaryless, ubiquitous yet nowhere concretely located, like a mass of water vapor in the atmosphere. The metaphor succeeds because of the way it systematically **erases** the materiality of infrastructure. A data center is an extremely material facility made of concrete, steel, copper wire, and silicon chips, but under the name “cloud” it is experienced as an immaterial service. When a user asks a generative AI a question on a smartphone, the fact that the question runs tens of thousands of GPUs in some data center outside Phoenix, and that water from the Colorado River basin evaporates to cool those GPUs, lies outside the user’s horizon of perception.

This gap is not a matter of technical ignorance. It is the product of a **specific arrangement of perception and responsibility**. The purpose of this article is to analyze this arrangement conceptually. As analytical tools, we use Heidegger’s concept of “Gestell” (Enframing) and Haraway’s concept of “situated knowledges,” and as the adhesive for the case study, we refer to the water and electricity conditions of the Phoenix metropolitan data center cluster. The analysis performs a **conceptual redescription** that, alongside fact-checking the case, reveals how those facts are organized and rendered invisible conceptually.

## II. Gestell and Revealing as “Resource”: The Transformation of the Colorado River

The essence of modern technology, as analyzed by Heidegger in *The Question Concerning Technology* (1953), lies in revealing nature in the mode of “challenging forth” (Herausfordern). This is fundamentally different from earlier technologies—such as windmills or waterwheels—which conformed to natural flows and “borrowed” energy. Modern technology issues a challenge to nature, compelling it to reveal itself as a storable resource. That the Rhine River exists before a hydroelectric plant only as a provider of water pressure, that a forest is calculated only as timber—this “resourcification” is the core of Gestell. Heidegger calls the being thus revealed “standing-reserve” (Bestand).

The cooling system of the Phoenix data center reproduces the operation of this Gestell under 21st-century conditions.

Central Arizona’s water management system relies on two main sources. One is surface water drawn from the Colorado River via the Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal over 540 km; the other is groundwater from regional aquifers. These waters have historically been allocated for various uses—agriculture, ecosystem maintenance, municipal water supply. But the emergence of data centers summons this water for a new purpose.

**Water as coolant operates in the purest form of Gestell.** Water is no longer “something that flows,” nor “something that sustains life,” nor the product of a political agreement among seven states that negotiated the Colorado River basin rights just before World War II. Water is revealed only as a **cooling medium** to dissipate heat from GPUs. As of 2025, the annual water consumption of Arizona’s data centers is estimated at approximately 905 million gallons (2,777 acre-feet), equivalent to the annual usage of about 10,000 households in the Phoenix area. What matters is not the absolute magnitude of this figure, but the ontological transformation of what this water “is.” The same physical molecules (H₂O) as agricultural irrigation water, but in the data center’s cooling towers, this water is given a new mode of being: “resource.”

However, Heidegger’s Gestell alone cannot explain the **uneven distribution** of this process. When Colorado River water is directed to a data center, whose water is diverted, by what route, and by whose decision? This question leads Heidegger’s abstract ontology into a concrete **political field**. At this point, Haraway’s situated knowledges must intervene.

## III. Situated Knowledges: The Concreteness of Water, Electricity, and Sensory Labor

What Haraway criticizes in “Situated Knowledges” (1988) is the “god trick” of the Western scientific tradition—a non-situated viewpoint that claims to see everything by being located nowhere. Against this, Haraway argues that all knowledge is produced from a specific place, a specific body, under specific conditions of labor. The objectivity of knowledge comes not from non-location, but from conditions that allow one to **acknowledge and take responsibility for** one’s location.

In the discourse of data centers, the “cloud” metaphor plays exactly the role of the “god trick.” It pretends that computing takes place from a nowhere viewpoint. But through the prism of situated knowledges, the data center outside Phoenix is located in an extremely concrete place.

**Conditions of location:**

*First, the situatedness of water resources.* The water for the Phoenix data center comes from specific sources. Whether the data center directly pumps groundwater, uses CAP water, or uses treated recycled water connects it to different hydrological and political circuits. In the case of Buckeye, a 2,069-acre site previously planned for a 9,700-home residential development (Cipriani) was sold to a data center developer called Tract and converted into a 20-million-square-foot data center complex. Data center cooling water took the place of residential water. The mayor of Buckeye claims that the data center will use less water than a 2,000-home subdivision, but this comparison itself presupposes a **transformation of use** (maintenance of residential life → maintenance of computing infrastructure).

*Second, the situatedness of electricity.* The power consumption of the Phoenix data center is estimated at about 1.5 GW. This electricity comes from the Salt River Project’s thermal, hydro, and solar power grid, and the **indirect water use** in electricity production is greater than the direct use for cooling. According to a Ceres report, indirect water use linked to Phoenix-area data center electricity consumption is expected to increase by 400%, from 2.9 billion gallons to over 14.5 billion gallons. This indirect water is consumed in entirely different geographic locations—power plant cooling, reservoir evaporation at hydroelectric dams, etc. The separation between service convenience and environmental cost occurs here.

*Third, the situatedness of sensory labor.* What is most invisibilized is the sensory experience of local residents. Data centers generate noise (low-frequency hum from cooling fans and transformers), light pollution (24-hour lighting), increased traffic, and above all, anxiety about the indirect effects of large-scale water use on local water prices and availability. This sensory and emotional labor—sensing changes in one’s living environment, worrying about those changes, sometimes raising one’s voice at city council meetings or joining environmental groups—is not accounted for in the data center’s operating costs. In Haraway’s terms, this sensory labor is abandoned to the realm of “diffuse responsibility.”

## IV. Joining the Two Concepts: The “Cloud” Metaphor and the Mechanism of Separating Perception and Responsibility

Heidegger’s Gestell and Haraway’s situated knowledges operate at different levels, but they are complementary in analyzing the invisibilization mechanism of the “cloud.”

**Gestell explains the ontological transformation of nature.** The way Colorado River water is “summoned” as coolant, uranium ore as fuel for GPU power, desert air as a heat exchange medium—this belongs to the essence of modern technology that reveals nature as standing-reserve. This transformation operates at an ontological level prior to perception, making it all the more powerful. We do not see water and then call it a “resource”; under the dominance of Gestell, water is revealed from the outset only as a resource.

**But Gestell alone cannot explain the unevenness of this “revealing.”** Why does some water remain agricultural, while other water is preferentially allocated to cooling GPUs for AI training? Why did Buckeye’s residential development plan give way to a data center? To answer these questions, Gestell must be connected to the **political economy of locational inequality**. Haraway’s situated knowledges provide precisely this link.

When these two concepts are joined, the ideological function of the “cloud” metaphor is revealed.

The “cloud” metaphor performs invisibilization on three levels.

**First, the operation of a vertical metaphor.** The “cloud” is up there. It suggests a celestial realm separate from the concrete water, earth, pipes, and transformers on the ground. This vertical metaphor systematically conceals the terrestrial materiality of the data center. When a user says “save it to the cloud,” that data is actually stored on a hard disk inside some concrete building outside Phoenix.

**Second, the dissolution of boundaries.** The “cloud” is presented as a single entity covering the entire globe without borders. But actual infrastructure is distributed extremely unevenly. Phoenix lies on a major fiber-optic cable route (Texas–California), has a government-owned power utility (Salt River Project) offering low electricity rates, and the state has provided a sales tax exemption for data centers since 2013. This particular set of conditions is the real cause of data center attraction.

**Third, the deification of the user’s position.** The “cloud” user is presented as an omnipotent subject who can access from anywhere. When a user in Seoul asks a generative AI a question, and that question is processed on GPUs in Phoenix, the user experiences none of that fact. The user’s experience consists only of a **pure service interface**. This is the digital version of the “god trick” that Haraway criticized. But this time, the god is the user themselves—a god of ignorance unaware of their own location.

## V. Redescribing the Data Center: From Environmental Polluter to Dispositif of Perception and Responsibility

A crucial shift is needed here. Criticizing the data center merely as an “environmental polluter” is insufficient. Such criticism fixes the data center as a **cause** (a facility that generates pollution) and confines solutions to the realm of technical efficiency (water recycling, water-free cooling systems). This framework problematizes the data center’s water usage itself, but does not reach the question of **who, under what conditions, and within what structure of perception uses this water**.

This is why the data center must be redescribed as a **dispositif of perception and responsibility**. This raises the following questions.

**Arrangement of perception:**
- Does a path exist for the user of a generative AI service to perceive the data center’s water and electricity consumption as connected to their own usage?
- Whose senses capture the environmental impact of the data center? (Local residents sense noise, light, traffic; remote users do not.)
- Whose knowledge is recognized as “official” in data center-related decision-making? (Technical efficiency discourse vs. the lifeworld sensory experience of local residents.)

**Arrangement of responsibility:**
- Under whose responsibility does the data center’s water use fall—state government, city government, corporation, or consumer?
- Does responsibility end if a company adopts “water-free cooling” technology? (Indirect water use remains.)
- As the Ceres report notes, the indirect water footprint of electricity consumption can be four times that of direct cooling water. How does this indirectness reconfigure the circuits of responsibility?

The role of criticism is not to offer technical prescriptions to make data center water use “more efficient,” but to **make visible this very arrangement of perception and responsibility**. That is, the data center is an environmental problem, but more fundamentally, it is a field of perception-political questions: **who gets to see what, and who is held responsible for what**.

This redescription can be carried out in the following directions.

**First, deconstruct the “cloud” metaphor and reduce it to “pipes–concrete–wires.”** By redescribing data center infrastructure not as a “cloud” but in the language of material infrastructure itself (floor area ratio, power capacity, heat dissipation rate, water allocation quotas, permit conditions), the materiality that the metaphor conceals is brought to the fore.

**Second, reconstruct the cyclical connection of water–electricity–data.** Conceptually rebuild the connection between the “clean interface” displayed to the cloud service user and the hydrological and power-grid circuits operating behind it. The very fact that this connection is not displayed on the user interface is itself an object of analysis.

**Third, expand into a politics of sensation.** Do not downgrade the noise, vibration, and anxiety about water price increases sensed by local residents to mere “local issues,” but position them as testimony to the perception-political conditions of AI infrastructure.

## VI. Limitations and Open Questions

This analysis must clearly acknowledge several limitations.

**Limitations of data.** Individual data center water usage is mostly reported only as corporate totals; transparent data at the individual facility level is limited. Large operators such as Google, Microsoft, and Meta disclose some data, but data from third-party colocation providers or smaller facilities is even more opaque. This analysis relies on available reports (Ceres, Circle of Blue, APM Research Lab, Grist) and could only reference cases from a few areas such as Buckeye, Chandler, and Goodyear.

**Limitations of generalization.** The hydrological and political conditions of the Phoenix area (the Colorado River allocation system, the state-level sales tax exemption policy, the Salt River Project’s public power system, the 2024 Large User Ordinance) are unique. These conditions cannot be generalized to data center clusters in other regions (Northern Virginia, Oregon, Singapore, Chile, etc.). The analysis has the limitation of being a situated analysis confined to Phoenix.

**Tension between concepts.** Heidegger’s Gestell indicates a destiny of Being (Seinsgeschick) that pervades the entirety of modern technological civilization, whereas Haraway’s situated knowledges belong to the political and normative project of feminist science and technology studies. Hastily fusing these two philosophical traditions risks distorting the theoretical terrain to which each concept belongs. Rather than systematically integrating the two concepts, this analysis has strategically placed them side by side for a specific analytical purpose—to reveal the invisibilization mechanism of the cloud.

**Direction of technological development.** Improvements in technical efficiency—such as Microsoft’s closed-loop cooling system (pilot in 2026), CyrusOne’s water-free cooling policy (new facilities since 2024), and Edged’s waterless cooling system—may reduce the direct water consumption of data centers. However, these technical solutions do not solve the problem of the arrangement of perception and responsibility. As long as indirect water use (in electricity production) remains, and as long as the perceptual separation between service users and infrastructure costs persists, the structure of the problem continues.

## VII. Conclusion: The Politics of the Visible and the Invisible

The data center outside Phoenix embodies a paradox. It is extremely material (thousands of gallons of water, megawatts of electricity, acres of land) and extremely invisible (except to local residents and relevant experts, its existence goes unnoticed). This paradox is not a property of the technological infrastructure itself, but a product of the perception-political conditions woven together by the ontological mode of revealing constituted by Gestell and the situatedness of perception that Haraway points out.

The “cloud” is not a mere metaphor. It is an **infrastructure of invisibilization**. The very condition under which generative AI and cloud services are experienced as a “clean” interface rests upon an arrangement of perception that systematically deletes from view the water, electricity, and sensory labor that make that interface possible.

The task of criticism is to dismantle this arrangement. That is, to trace one by one the material chains hidden beneath the immaterial metaphor of the “cloud”—from the Colorado River to the cooling tower, from the cooling tower to steam, from steam to the atmosphere—and to reveal at each of those connection points what perception is possible and what responsibility is assigned. This is the work of criticism that redescribes the data center not as a mere environmental polluter, but as a dispositif of perception and responsibility.
