## 1. Layers of the Problem: The 'Affective Machine' of Platform Capitalism

The 'immediate affect and reactivity' demanded by platform capitalism is not merely a matter of user experience design. It is a **matter of conditions**. In other words:

> **The platform does not define the content of experience, but rather preemptively formalizes the conditions under which experience occurs — temporality, the arrangement of senses, the rhythm of response.**

In this regard, the affective machine of platform capitalism is structurally isomorphic with what Kittler called the 'interlocking of technical conditions.' Algorithmic recommendations, infinite scroll, push notifications, and real-time response systems are not independent functions but form a single **dispositif (arrangement of conditions)** . This arrangement forces the user to **eliminate the temporal gap for reflective distance** and **respond before the sensation is embodied**.

From this, the first axis of the question emerges: **Is 'Slow Information' merely a strategy to slow down within this arrangement of conditions, or does it demand a transformation of the arrangement itself?**

---

## 2. Slow Information: Not 'Slowness' but 'Interval'

Slow Information should not be understood simply as a 'reduction in information processing speed.' It must be understood as a **topological intervention** into the operating conditions of platform capitalism itself.

### 2.1 Conditions of Immediate Affect

The platform's affective machine operates on the following three conditions:

| Condition | Description | What is Eliminated |
|-----------|-------------|--------------------|
| **Temporal Continuity** | Elimination of the time gap between stimulus and response | Reflective distance |
| **Sensory Monolayer** | Reduction of all sensory inputs to the same response unit | Texture of sensation |
| **Affect-Action Directness** | Omission of the process of emotional embodiment | Ethical judgment |

These conditions reduce the user to a 'respondent,' which is a structural violence that deprives the user of the conditions to construct their own sensory experience as an 'agent.'

### 2.2 Topological Intervention of Slow Information

Slow Information responds to these three conditions respectively as follows:

1. **Restoration of Temporal Interval**: A 'mandatory waiting time' or 'pause' device that allows for a response after information is presented. This is not a simple delay but creates an **ontological gap** where interpretation can intervene before the response.

2. **Restoration of Sensory Texture**: Instead of the platform-standardized sensory units (likes, scrolls, clicks), the very way information is presented is aesthetically reconfigured — fonts, rhythm, arrangement, tactile interfaces. This connects to the concept of Atura, which refers to the 'rearrangement of the space of possibilities before experience.'

3. **Introduction of Affect-Action Gap**: A **reflective loop** is placed between emotion and immediate response, preventing emotion from directly leading to action. This corresponds to the 'critical reflection' stage in the four stages of AI-human contact.

Viewed this way, Slow Information is not a simple 'speed control' but a **condition intervention on conditions** — an attempt to reconfigure the very arrangement of conditions under which the platform operates.

## 3. Sensory Ethics: Responsibility Before Response

Sensory Ethics gives ethical content to the very **interval** that Slow Information conditions.

### 3.1 Platform's Sensory Desensitization

Platform capitalism paradoxically leads to **sensory desensitization** through excessive sensory stimulation. When everything is presented as equally affective, the user can no longer sense the qualitative differences in information. This is a digital transformation of what Stiegler called 'symbolic misery' — a state where sensation can no longer organize experience.

### 3.2 Three Axes of Sensory Ethics

Sensory Ethics responds to this desensitization by proposing the following:

**First, changing the conditions of sensation, not training sensation.** Sensory Ethics does not aim for individuals to 'become more sensitive.' Rather, it asks **under what conditions sensation becomes possible**. This is a sensory version of Bhaskar's critical realism — a transcendental question about the conditions of experience.

**Second, refusal of response and suspension of judgment.** Sensory Ethics does not prescribe 'what one should feel.' Instead, it makes 'what to place between feeling and response' an ethical question. This can be seen as a sensory extension of Levinasian ethics of the other — infinite responsibility for the other. Here, however, the other is not a human other but **information itself, i.e., ontological difference**.

**Third, discovering the politics of sensation.** Individual sensory experience is never private. The conditions of sensation are always already preemptively formalized by technical, social, and economic conditions. Therefore, 'how I feel' cannot be separated from 'under what conditions we feel.'

### 3.3 Difference Between Sensory Ethics and Mindfulness

Superficially, Slow Information and Sensory Ethics may seem similar to 'mindfulness' or 'digital detox.' However, there is a fundamental difference:

| Category | Mindfulness/Detox | Slow Information/Sensory Ethics |
|----------|-------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Target of Intervention** | Individual habits | Platform operating conditions |
| **Goal** | Individual adaptation/recovery | Transformation of condition arrangement |
| **Method** | Discontinuation/blocking of use | Reconfiguration of conditions |
| **Political Nature** | Apolitical (individual management) | Political (collective condition change) |

This difference is key to judging the second axis of the question — **'Can it lead to collective intervention?'**

## 4. From Individual Sensory Management to Collective Intervention: Possibilities and Conditions

Now, let's return to the essential question: Can the practice of Slow Information move beyond individual sensory management to become a **collective intervention that changes the operating conditions of the platform itself?**

### 4.1 Structural Limitations: A Warning

This transition is not easy for three reasons:

1. **Invisibility of the Condition Arrangement**: The platform's operating conditions are given to the user before experience, so the user has no means to recognize them. It's like a fish not recognizing water.

2. **Possibility of Co-optation of Individual Practice**: When Slow Information practice is performed at the individual level, the platform can co-opt it as a 'user choice.' Speed control devices already packaged as 'digital well-being' features are examples.

3. **Recovery by the Affective Economy**: There is always a risk that even resistant practices can be converted into new affective commodities by the platform. The moment 'slowness' becomes a lifestyle product, it transforms from a tool for condition change into an object of consumption.

### 4.2 Conditions for Transition: Necessary Bridges

However, it cannot be concluded that this transition is structurally impossible. Individual practice can lead to collective intervention when the following conditions are met:

**First, shared consciousness of conditions.** The practice of Slow Information must lead not just to a personal preference but to a diagnosis of the condition arrangement. That is, a cognitive shift from 'I find this interface uncomfortable' to 'This interface is designed to eliminate my reflective distance' is necessary. This exactly matches the **'critical reflection' stage in the four stages of AI-human contact** mentioned in Khoj document 1 — a movement that reflects on the conditions under which experience occurs.

**Second, shareable formalization of Slow Information.** Individual sensory practice must be formalized into shareable protocols, interface principles, and design guidelines. This is a practical turn of the **Atura concept** mentioned in Khoj document 2 — the preemptive formalization of technical conditions. If Atura is an *analytical concept*, the formalization of Slow Information is a *constructive practice*.

**Third, collective intervention into conditions.** This is the most decisive step. After recognizing the problems of the condition arrangement through the practice of Slow Information, users must attempt **collective intervention to change the platform's conditions themselves** based on this recognition. This can take the following forms:

- **Alternative interface design**: Third-party clients, extensions, or user scripts that implement the principles of Slow Information. Examples: removal of infinite scroll, introduction of mandatory waiting times, reconfiguration of sensory arrangement of information.
- **Public demands for conditions**: Collective action demanding developers change specific operating conditions (algorithmic transparency, mandatory delay in response time, sensory diversity).
- **Experiments with Slow Information platforms**: Community experiments building new forms of information environments that operate under different conditions than existing platforms.

### 4.3 Ethical Asymmetry and the Problem of Responsibility

At a deeper level, collective intervention of Slow Information raises the question of **'who' changes the conditions**. The belief that users can recognize and intervene in the condition arrangement presupposes the recognizability and intervenability of conditions. However:

> Can all users equally access the condition arrangement? Recognition of and intervention in conditions may themselves be cognitive, social, and economic privileges.

At this point, the 'AI environment vs. object' discussion connects. The view of AI as an object gives users the illusion of controllability, while the view of AI as an **environment** acknowledges that users are always already conditioned. The practice of Slow Information operates on the tension between these two perspectives: recognizing environmental conditions but presupposing that those conditions are not absolute.

## 5. Conclusion: Possibility in Tension

Slow Information and Sensory Ethics offer the following responses to the affective machine of platform capitalism:

1. **The response to immediacy demands not a reduction in speed but a rearrangement of conditions.**
2. **Individual sensory management contains the possibility of leading to collective condition intervention**, but this is not automatically guaranteed.
3. **The success of the transition depends on three conditions** — shared consciousness of conditions, formalization of practice, and collective intervention.

The most vulnerable point is the **transition from the second to the third condition**. For formalized Slow Information to become collective intervention, it requires the **formation of a political subject** beyond simple design principles. That is, those who share the practice of Slow Information must become not just a 'user group' but a **collective agent demanding changes in platform conditions**.

At this point, the practice of Slow Information extends beyond **Kittler's analysis of technical conditions** to a **constructive practice aimed at changing those conditions**. And the success or failure of this extension depends on how long Slow Information can prevent itself from being co-opted as an **individual taste or lifestyle choice**.
