## Question

On TikTok’s ‘For You’ feed, the process where a user’s micro-gesture of swiping past a video without watching it to the end simultaneously alters the arrangement of the next recommended videos and the creator’s editing rhythm—what is missed when explained through Foucault’s governmentality or Stiegler’s proletarianization of attention? Should this co-regulation be viewed as the user’s choice, or as the platform’s preemptive discipline of behavior?

## Analysis Result

This question demands a precise analysis that goes beyond a simple dichotomy. Below is an in-depth analysis conducted immediately without further confirmation.

## The Co-Regulation of TikTok: What Governmentality and Proletarianization of Attention Miss

### 1. Framing the Problem — What Each Theory Explains

**Foucault’s governmentality** captures the following in this scene: The user, without external direct coercion, experiences their scrolling choices as ‘expressions of free preference’ within the data flow produced by the recommendation algorithm. However, those choices are only possible along the trajectory of behavioral nudges pre-designed by the platform. The moment a user clicks ‘like’ or ‘skip,’ that action is recorded in the platform’s database and becomes a statistical input that constructs the next feed. This is a typical Foucauldian structure of self-discipline—the user believes they are acting freely, but that freedom operates only within a ‘field of choice’ already organized into the platform’s calculable range.

**Stiegler’s proletarianization of attention** captures the following in this scene: TikTok’s short video format (15-60 seconds) and auto-play function organize the user’s attention into a rhythm of continuous-intermittent consumption. The flow of repeated short stimuli is recorded in the user’s tertiary memory (technical memory), producing a cognitive condition where they can no longer sustain attention on long-form narratives. The user adapts to this rhythm without recognizing that their attention is being commodified. For Stiegler, this replicates in the digital age, within the realm of attention, the way the proletariat sold their physical labor power as a commodity during the Industrial Revolution.

Both perspectives have powerful explanatory force. However, **what this question precisely points out** is that the co-regulation occurring on TikTok’s For You feed is not fully captured by either of these frames.

---

### 2. What the Two Theories Miss — Three Layers

#### First, the double genesis of rhythm

Foucault’s governmentality explains the productivity of power, but it does not sufficiently account for the cyclical feedback where *the user’s gesture itself* reconfigures the next recommendation array. The governmentality model remains hierarchical—the platform designs, the user responds. But on TikTok, the ‘minuscule interruption’ of the user scrolling past a video in 1.5 seconds is not merely reception; it is an active modulation that generates the conditions for the next frame.

Stiegler’s frame goes further at this point but still leans toward emphasizing the *extraction* and *deprivation* of attention. That is, for Stiegler, the problem is that attention is taken away, and the alternative is the restoration of attention’s sovereignty. However, in the actual operation of TikTok, attention is not simply ‘taken away’; it is *co-produced* at the interface between user and platform, and the quality of that produced attention becomes the condition for further recommendations. Whether the user scrolls slowly, quickly, or stops at a particular point—all these micro-variables become conditions for the next recommendation in a cyclical system that is difficult to capture with negative terms like ‘extraction’ or ‘deprivation.’

This is because both Foucault’s governmentality and Stiegler’s proletarianization are constructed around the language of **subjection** rather than **production**. Governmentality explains subjectivation, but the process is always narrated from the perspective of power. Stiegler’s proletarianization centers on the loss of subjectivity. However, what happens on the TikTok feed is more complex than ‘loss of subjectivity’—the user’s micro-gesture is, at every moment, a **productive input** that determines the next state of the system, while at the same time that input operates only under the platform’s conditions.

At this point, **Deleuze’s control society** provides a more useful conceptual tool. Deleuze distinguishes the control society from Foucault’s disciplinary society through the concept of **modulation**. If discipline operates in closed spaces (school, hospital, prison, factory) as a ‘mold,’ control operates in open spaces as a constantly changing ‘modulation.’ TikTok’s For You feed is a perfect implementation of this modulation. The user’s gesture of swiping to the next video in 1.5 seconds is itself a modulatory act that subtly readjusts the algorithmic conditions of the feed. However, this modulation is not so much the user’s intentional choice as it is **a cyclical dynamic where the rhythm of behavior itself simultaneously creates conditions and outcomes**.

#### Second, the platform’s preemptive structure and the user’s generative adaptation

Here we return to the decisive question: Should this co-regulation be seen as **the user’s choice** or as **the platform’s preemptive discipline of behavior**?

While the Khoj RAG references mention “the difficulty of dialogue” between humanities and engineering (document 5) and the need for “convergent collaboration” (document 3), these do not directly address the precise issue of this question. What document 5 points out—that “it is not even easy to understand the ‘terms’ used by the two camps”—rather suggests that what this question demands is an awareness that **user-choice theory and platform-determinism theory form a single dynamic system through mutual constitution**, transcending the dichotomy between them.

The answer is not “both” but **“neither”**. This is for the following reasons:

(1) **Why it is difficult to see as the user’s choice**: The user’s scrolling decision is only possible within conditions already designed by the platform (video length, auto-play, infinite scroll, pre-arrangement of recommendations by algorithm). Choice is always a selection among already given conditions, and those conditions are optimized by the platform for the objective function of ‘longer dwell time.’ These conditions operate at a sensorimotor level before the user is consciously aware. The user scrolls almost reflexively, faster than a cognitive judgment like ‘I’ll skip this video because it’s not interesting.’ This speed itself problematizes the concept of ‘conscious choice.’

(2) **Why it is difficult to see as the platform’s discipline**: The platform does not simply ‘discipline’ or ‘manipulate’ user behavior. The recommendation algorithm takes the user’s previous behavioral data as input and outputs the next state. That is, the user’s ‘deviant’ behavior (lingering on an unexpected video, suddenly binge-watching a specific category) becomes feedback input that changes the next recommendation. The user is not a ‘subject of discipline’ but a **co-modulator participating in the system’s evolutionary conditions**. Of course, this participation is asymmetrical—the user does not know the specific workings of the algorithm and does not transparently recognize how their actions affect recommendations.

#### Third, the new subjectivity produced by the rhythm of adaptation

The core here is a new kind of subjectivity produced by the **rhythm of adaptation**, which is neither ‘discipline’ nor ‘choice.’

A TikTok user neither consumes passively nor chooses completely freely. Instead, in the process of **adapting to the platform’s rhythm, the user embodies a new sensory system and temporality of judgment**. This cannot be explained solely as ‘loss’ or ‘deprivation,’ as Stiegler’s proletarianization would have it. Rather, it is a process where the user develops a new sense—an **aesthetic judgment in compressed temporality**—that judges ‘worth watching’ within the first two seconds of a video.

This judgment is not ‘aesthetic judgment’ in the traditional sense. Contrary to Kant’s disinterested interest, it is an **extremely interested, almost reflexive judgment**. In two seconds, the user judges: ‘Does this video provide enough stimulation to hold me for the next 15 seconds?’ One might say this is merely reaction time, but more importantly, the boundary between reaction time and aesthetic judgment collapses at this moment.

Here, the issue from document 4 about the “privileged absolute supremacy of the natural over the artificial” becomes relevant. The dichotomy that human sensation and judgment are ‘natural’ and algorithmic recommendations are ‘artificial’ is difficult to maintain in the cycle of TikTok’s feed, where the user’s judgment is already formed within algorithmic conditions and is fed back as the next state of the algorithm. The user’s ‘natural’ aesthetic judgment and the algorithm’s ‘artificial’ recommendation are no longer separable independent terms.

### 3. Attempting a Theoretical Alternative — ‘Rhythmic Co-Composition’

To capture what Foucault and Stiegler miss, I propose a conceptual device called **rhythmic co-composition**.

This concept consists of three elements:

**a) Modulatory circulation**: The cycle where the user’s micro-gesture becomes the condition for the next recommendation, and that recommendation again becomes the condition for the user’s next gesture. This cycle is closer to Deleuze’s concept of modulation than to Foucault’s governmentality or Stiegler’s proletarianization.

**b) Double coding of rhythm**: Both the user and the platform inject their respective rhythms into the system. The platform’s rhythm is a temporality of immediacy, repetition, and intermittent stimulation. The user’s rhythm is a tension between attention and distraction, the threshold of boredom, and predictability vs. unpredictability. These two rhythms do not simply clash or merge; rather, they generate a **hybrid rhythm** at the interface.

**c) Active passivity at the interface**: The user ‘passively’ watches videos, but within that passivity, an active judgment (whether to scroll or stop) occurs. The platform ‘actively’ generates recommendations, but that activity ‘passively’ depends on the user’s past data. This paradox of ‘active passivity’ is the core of rhythmic co-composition.

---

### 4. Conclusion — Direct Response to the Question

Viewing this co-regulation as the **user’s choice** overlooks the systematic asymmetry of the conditions pre-configured by the platform. At the same time, viewing it as the **platform’s preemptive discipline of behavior** misses the cyclical nature where the user’s actions actively determine the system’s evolutionary conditions.

What this question appropriately problematizes is that **the very pressure to choose between the two explanations is an analytical error**. TikTok’s For You feed operates in a third domain beyond the ‘discipline vs. choice’ dichotomy—a domain where user and platform **co-construct rhythm in a manner that is temporal (time), asymmetrical (power), and cyclical (feedback)**.

To conceptualize this domain, what is needed is not to abandon the critical lenses offered by Foucault’s governmentality and Stiegler’s proletarianization, but to **add a new conceptual apparatus of modulation and rhythmic co-composition**. This is not merely an extension of existing theories but a response to the new theoretical challenges posed by the temporality and agency of digital platforms.
