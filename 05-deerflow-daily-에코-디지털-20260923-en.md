## Question

Taking the case of a project that recovers server waste heat from a data center in downtown Seoul to supply heating energy for a nearby indoor farm, can digital environmental governance that calculates ecological performance centered on carbon emissions and energy efficiency marginalize the local burdens of heat, humidity, water use, and labor? If so, what evaluation units should be designed to analyze the tension between the reduction of ecological value into data and the actual experience of material circulation in this case?

## Analysis Results

# Tensions in Data Center Waste Heat Recovery and Eco-Digital Governance: An Analysis Between Environmental Performance Measurement and Material Circulation

## 1. Diagnosis: Structural Marginalization of Carbon-Centered Governance

**Yes, it can be marginalized.** And this marginalization is not an accidental failure but a structural effect that carbon-energy-centered digital environmental governance inherently operates.

Let us analyze the Seoul downtown data center-indoor farm heat recovery project. This project involves raising the temperature of server waste heat (low-temperature heat around 25-35°C) from the data center using a heat pump and supplying it as heating energy for the nearby indoor farm. The performance measured by governance typically includes the following:

| Measurement Item | What Governance Captures | What Governance Marginalizes |
|-----------------|--------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Carbon Emissions** | Avoided CO₂ emissions (tCO₂-eq/year) | Embodied carbon in manufacturing/installation of waste heat recovery infrastructure, seasonal variation in heat loss rate |
| **Energy Efficiency** | Heat recovery rate (%), Heat pump COP | Energy loss due to increased heat transport distance, grid load from auxiliary power consumption |
| **Heat** | Recovered heat quantity (kWh) | Local microclimate changes, underground heat island effect, increased cooling load on surrounding buildings |
| **Humidity** | Not measured (outside reporting framework) | Changes in data center internal humidity after waste heat recovery → impact on server lifespan, localized humidity increase from indoor farm transpiration → impact on adjacent residential environment |
| **Water Use** | Only direct water usage accounted | Increased water consumption with waste heat recovery method (when switching from air-cooled to water-cooled), thermal load from indoor farm hydroponic wastewater, competition for local water resources |
| **Labor** | Not measured | Increased skill requirements for system maintenance labor, night work for 24-hour monitoring personnel, safety management labor for heat recovery equipment |

The reporting framework ontologically deletes all these marginalized items. This is not simply missing data, but a **mediating effect where the 'method of reducing environmental performance into data' itself constitutes a specific hierarchy of values.** And this point is precisely the area where local document references provide insight.

## 3. Structure of Tension: Data Reduction vs. Material Circulation

In this case, the tension operates on three levels:

### Level 1: Temporal Mismatch

| | Carbon-Energy Governance | Reality of Material Circulation |
|---|---|---|
| Temporality | Annual/quarterly reporting cycles | Seasonal and daily fluctuations (summer cooling demand vs. winter heating demand conflict) |
| Measurement Target | Total avoided emissions | System limits during peak times, heat loss during non-operating hours |
| Future Outlook | Linear reduction pathways | Equipment aging, technological changes (liquid cooling introduction would completely change waste heat recovery structure) |

### Level 2: Spatial Mismatch

Carbon accounting operates at a global unit (tCO₂-eq), but the burdens of heat, humidity, and water are local:

- Data center waste heat recovery → indoor farm heating → plant transpiration → localized humidity increase → discomfort in adjacent residential areas, condensation issues
- Additional water consumption when switching to water-cooled system → load on local wastewater treatment plants, competition for water resources
- Spatial occupation of waste heat recovery infrastructure (pipes, heat pumps, thermal storage tanks) → thermal concentration in urban underground space

### Level 3: Ontological Mismatch

This is the most fundamental level. **Environmental performance data is not the environment itself.** When ecological value is reduced to indicators like CO₂-eq, kWh, COP, the losses incurred are:

| Reduction Method | What It Captures | What It Deletes |
|-----------------|------------------|-----------------|
| Carbon Equivalent (CO₂-eq) | Contribution to global warming | Totality of local temperature, humidity, water cycles |
| Energy Efficiency (COP, % recovery rate) | Thermodynamic efficiency | Materiality of labor and maintenance required to sustain that efficiency |
| Economic Feasibility (NPV, IRR, payback period) | Return on investment | Health, comfort, equity of local residents |

## 4. Designing New Evaluation Units: Multi-Scale Multi-Indicator Framework

To analyze this tension, I propose **three levels of evaluation units**. Each level operates at different temporal and spatial scales, and they are mutually irreducible yet interconnected.

### Unit 1: Thermo-Spatial Stratification

**Purpose**: To evaluate energy flows stratified by spatial scale

| Layer | Evaluation Unit | Example Indicator | Application to Data Center-Indoor Farm Case |
|-------|----------------|------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| **Layer 1** (System Internal) | Heat flow within server room-pipes-greenhouse | Heat recovery rate, temperature gradient, heat loss rate | Server room exhaust temperature (35°C) → heat pump boost (45°C) → greenhouse heating → soil heat dissipation |
| **Layer 2** (Building-Site) | Total energy-water-heat balance of data center + indoor farm complex | Energy self-sufficiency rate, water circulation rate, heat density | Ratio of supplementary heat source after waste heat recovery, additional water consumption, underground soil temperature rise |
| **Layer 3** (Local) | Microclimate impact on adjacent blocks | Local temperature change, humidity change, heat island contribution | Changes in cooling load of adjacent buildings, increased residential humidity, groundwater temperature impact |
| **Layer 4** (Urban-Atmosphere) | City-scale carbon and energy balance | tCO₂-eq avoided, urban heat balance | Substitution effect for other heat sources, reduction in transmission and distribution losses |

**Design Rationale**: Carbon-energy indicators operate only at Layer 4 (urban-atmosphere). Layers 1-3 are marginalized by these indicators. Therefore, indicators for each layer must be placed in parallel.

### Unit 2: Cyclical Material-Labor Monitoring

**Purpose**: To evaluate material flows and labor conditions by cycle phase

| Cycle Phase | Evaluation Item | Marginalization Prevention Mechanism |
|-------------|-----------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Supply** | Embodied carbon + water + labor in heat recovery equipment production | LCA-based full life cycle assessment (not just operational phase) |
| **Operation** | Daily/weekly heat, humidity, water, labor fluctuation profiles | Enhanced temporal resolution (annual → monthly → weekly → daily) |
| **Maintenance** | Equipment inspection/replacement cycles, worker heat stress | Regular collection of labor intensity, safety, and health indicators |
| **End-of-life/Upgrade** | Material recovery rate during equipment dismantling, cost of heat recovery infrastructure decommissioning | Circularity indicators (reuse, recycling, landfill ratios) |

**Design Rationale**: Existing governance focuses on the 'operation' phase. The embodied burdens of the 'supply' phase and the subsequent burdens of the 'end-of-life' phase are overlooked. In particular, **the carbon, water, and labor burdens from the manufacturing phase of heat recovery infrastructure (pipes, heat pumps, thermal storage tanks)** are typically omitted from reporting frameworks.

### Unit 3: Ontological Tension Metric (OTM)

**Purpose**: To qualitatively record the 'gap' between data reduction and material experience

This is not a traditional indicator. It is a **meta-indicator that systematically records what governance fails to capture**.

| OTM Sub-Indicator | Definition | Application to Data Center-Indoor Farm Case |
|-------------------|------------|---------------------------------------------|
| **δ-Value** (Data-Value Gap) | Difference between ecological value reduced to data and the material conditions experienced by local residents/workers | CO₂-eq reduction data vs. humidity, heat sensation, noise felt by nearby residents |
| **Opacity Index** | Ratio of marginalized items in governance reports | Number of indicators covered by report / Number of material conditions actually affected |
| **Temporal Resolution Ratio** | Ratio of fluctuation captured by annual data to actual daily fluctuation | Annual average COP is 4.0, but does it capture the drop to 2.5 during summer peak? |
| **Spatial Exclusion Coefficient** | Scale of burdens transferred outside the project boundary | Additional cooling energy burdened on surrounding buildings due to waste heat recovery |

**Design Rationale**: This indicator visualizes the limits of 'what is measurable' in contrast to 'what is not measurable'. The delta (δ) concept integrates the continental philosophical concept of différance with the STS concept of invisibility.

## 5. Solution: Proposed Integrated Evaluation Framework

Integrating the above three units, I propose the following evaluation framework:

```
[1] Thermo-Spatial Stratification Evaluation
    ├── Layer 1: System internal heat flow
    ├── Layer 2: Complex-wide material balance
    ├── Layer 3: Local microclimate impact
    └── Layer 4: Urban-scale carbon-energy
    
    ↓ (Parallel evaluation, no hierarchical reduction)
    
[2] Cyclical Material-Labor Monitoring
    ├── Supply phase (embodied burdens)
    ├── Operation phase (fluctuation profiles)
    ├── Maintenance phase (labor conditions)
    └── End-of-life phase (circularity)
    
    ↓ (Quantitative + Qualitative hybrid)
    
[3] Ontological Tension Metric (OTM)
    ├── δ-Value (data-experience gap)
    ├── Opacity Index (marginalization ratio)
    ├── Temporal Resolution Ratio
    └── Spatial Exclusion Coefficient
```

**Core Principle**: These three units are not hierarchical. In particular, [1] and [3] are mutually irreducible. [3] records what [1] cannot measure, and the gap identified by [3] is not reduced to an improvement in [1].

## 6. Limitations and Future Research Directions

**Limitations of Khoj References**: The similarity scores from the local literature DB (0.042~0.050) were low, providing limited direct evidence for this question. The concepts of "art without work" from Document 1 and "space filled with data" from Document 5 offered metaphorical insights, but for a concrete analysis of energy-environment governance, additional literature (e.g., STS infrastructure invisibility studies, ecological economics critiques of energy accounting) is needed.

**Theoretical Issues of the Question**: The core question raised is the ontological inquiry: **"Is an environment replaced by data still an environment?"** A data center waste heat recovery project is "eco-friendly" not because its material circulation itself is sustainable, but because the data shows it that way. This is not simply a measurement error, but a problem of epistemic mediation internalized by digital environmental governance.

To deconstruct this mediation, a **meta-governance that includes the boundary between measurement and non-measurement as part of the measurement target** is needed, and the OTM proposed above can serve that role.
