# Media Aesthetics in the Age of Technological Generation: An Analysis of the Nesting Mechanism between the Machinocene and the Anthropocene

## Introduction: The Subject of the Problem

The definition that "the Machinocene emerges within the technological extremes of the Anthropocene and is a civilizational transition nested upon it" implies the duality of the temporal conditions we face. The core of this definition is the conceptual device of "nesting." Nesting does not mean simple temporal continuity or spatial juxtaposition. Rather, it refers to the way in which different temporal scales and ontological layers condition each other within a single structure. If the Anthropocene deals with changes in the material conditions of the Earth system—changes on slow temporal scales such as carbon concentration, sea-level rise, and species decline—then the Machinocene deals with conditions of real-time reconstruction of sensation, attention, and judgment—perceptual framing by algorithms, transformations in the attention economy, and automation of judgment on fast temporal scales. The relationship between these two layers is not a simple causal connection but raises the problem of ontological nesting.

The purpose of this article is to formalize this nesting mechanism through the temporal structure of the SR (stimulus-response) formula. To this end, it connects Gilbert Simondon's concept of concretization of technical objects with the synthetic data feedback loop of generative AI, comparing two interpretations: the evolutionary openness and the degenerative closure loop of technical objects. Through this analysis, the nesting mechanism between the material conditions of the Anthropocene (slow temporal scale) and the reconstruction of sensation-attention-judgment in the Machinocene (fast temporal scale) will be elucidated, and the implications of this problem for eco-digital/artificial nature research will be summarized. The following argument is based on conceptual reasoning and focuses on the reconstruction of theoretical structures rather than citations of specific detailed papers.

## 1. The Anthropocene and the Machinocene: Duality of Temporal Scales

The Anthropocene refers to an era in which human activity acts as a major geological force on the Earth system. Since this concept was proposed by Paul Crutzen and Eugene Stoermer, it has become a core concept not only in geological stratigraphy but also in humanities discourse. The core of the Anthropocene lies in the fact that changes in material conditions occur at the intersection of geological time scales (thousands to millions of years) and human historical time scales (hundreds of years). That is, human activity has caused irreversible changes to the Earth system, and these changes have long-term effects that transcend human cognitive time scales.

The Machinocene is a more specific phenomenon that emerges within the context of the Anthropocene. It refers to new conditions in which machine algorithms—especially learning algorithms and automated decision-making systems—constitute and lead the ecological environment. This concept does not simply refer to technological development but to a stage where technology possesses its own generative logic. Here, the "machine" is no longer merely a tool of humans but becomes the environment itself that mediates and reconstructs human sensation, attention, and judgment.

The temporal scales of these two layers are markedly different. Changes in the material conditions of the Anthropocene operate on a slow temporal scale. For example, the increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration progresses over decades to centuries, and its effects persist for thousands of years. In contrast, the reconstruction of sensation, attention, and judgment in the Machinocene operates on an extremely fast temporal scale. Algorithmic feedback loops operate in milliseconds, and users' attention and judgment are adjusted in real time. These two temporal scales imply not merely a quantitative difference but an ontological difference. The material conditions of the Anthropocene are understood within the continuity of causal chains, whereas the sensory reconstruction of the Machinocene operates within nonlinear and unpredictable feedback loops.

## 2. The Temporal Structure of the SR Formula: Expansion and Reconstruction

In classical behaviorist psychology, the SR (stimulus-response) formula assumes a direct connection between stimulus (S) and response (R). This model ignores temporal delays or reduces them to simple chains. However, to apply the SR formula to the analysis of technological conditions, an expansion of its temporal structure is necessary. The expanded SR formula proposed here includes the following elements: (1) temporal delay between stimulus and response (Δt), (2) recursive feedback from response to stimulus (R→S'), and (3) nesting between SR loops of different temporal scales.

This expansion connects with Simondon's concept of concretization. Simondon explains the process by which a technical object develops from an abstract state to a concrete state. In the initial stage, a technical object depends on various external elements (humans, energy sources, environment) to perform its function. This is the "abstract" stage, where each component operates relatively independently, and the entire system is sensitive to the external environment. However, as the technical object develops, the interdependence between components increases, and the internal structure becomes organized to better handle the external environment. In this "concrete" stage, the technical object achieves a higher level of internal coherence and environmental adaptability.

When this concretization process is expressed through the SR formula, the initial stage is characterized by loose S-R connections. Responses to stimuli depend on external adjustments, and temporal delays (Δt) are large. However, as concretization progresses, feedback mechanisms form within the technical object, causing responses to act recursively as stimuli. This implies a shortening of the internal temporal scale. That is, the technical object becomes able to adapt more quickly to changes in the external environment.

This analysis can be directly applied to the synthetic data feedback loop of generative AI. When a generative AI model generates output based on training data, and this output is used again as training data, a feedback loop is formed. This feedback loop can be seen as a form of Simondonian concretization. The model gradually adjusts its internal structure through its own output, adapting to the environment (data distribution) on a faster temporal scale. However, this process allows for two opposing interpretations: evolutionary openness and degenerative closure loop.

## 3. Simondon's Concretization and Evolutionary Openness

Simondon's concept of concretization views the evolution of technical objects as a positive process. Concretization allows a technical object to acquire internal coherence, adapt better to its environment, and perform its function more efficiently. This process is "open." That is, the technical object can change its structure in response to new conditions, opening up infinite possibilities for adaptation. Simondon calls this the "evolutionary openness of technical objects."

This openness appears in the temporal structure of the SR formula as follows. A concretized technical object includes SR loops operating on multiple temporal scales. Fast temporal scale loops handle real-time adaptation, while slow temporal scale loops handle structural changes. The nesting of these two loops allows the technical object to possess both stability and flexibility simultaneously. For example, the concretization of a car engine is achieved through the synchronization of multiple subsystems such as fuel injection, ignition timing, and exhaust gas treatment. Each subsystem has its own temporal scale, but through the coordination of the entire system, a higher level of adaptability is achieved.

In the context of generative AI, this concept relates to the model's learning process. In the initial learning stage, the model is exposed to various data distributions, which corresponds to Simondon's "abstract" stage. The model responds individually to each data point, and its internal structure is not yet highly organized. However, through fine-tuning or continuous learning, the model becomes specialized for a specific distribution, and its internal representations become more structured. This signifies a transition to the "concrete" stage. In this process, the model becomes able to adapt to new data on a faster temporal scale. That is, the feedback loop promotes evolutionary openness.

## 4. The Synthetic Data Feedback Loop of Generative AI and Degenerative Closure

However, the feedback loop of generative AI can lead to results opposite to Simondon's concretization. This is most clearly revealed in the phenomenon known as model collapse. Model collapse is a phenomenon in which, when a generative AI uses its own output as training data, the model's diversity gradually decreases, and it eventually loses the characteristics of the original data distribution. This suggests that the feedback loop can lead not to openness but to closure.

The mechanism of this phenomenon can be explained as follows. When a generative AI model generates output, this output is only an approximation of the original distribution. This approximation inevitably includes a loss of variance. When this output is used again as training data, the model learns a distribution with reduced variance. As this process repeats, the diversity of the original distribution gradually decreases, and the model converges to a narrow region. This is a "degenerative closure loop," in which the technical object loses evolutionary openness and moves toward self-reinforcing closure.

In the temporal structure of the SR formula, this degenerative closure is expressed as follows. The model's feedback loop operates on a fast temporal scale. In the process where output recurs as stimulus (S) to generate response (R), each iteration reduces variance. This implies temporal convergence of the loop. Initially, various responses are possible, but as iterations progress, the range of possible responses narrows. This is a phenomenon where the internal time of the loop shortens while the connection with the external environment (original data distribution) weakens.

These two interpretations—evolutionary openness and degenerative closure—are opposed, but in reality, they are two sides of the same SR structure. Simondon's concretization emphasizes the process by which a technical object improves its structure through interaction with the external environment, whereas model collapse shows that the same feedback loop can lead to internal closure. This difference depends on the degree of openness of the technical object, that is, whether new information input from the external environment exists. Simondon's concretization presupposes continuous interaction with the external environment, but the synthetic data feedback loop relies solely on internally generated data, excluding the external environment.

## 5. Nesting of the Anthropocene and the Machinocene: Temporal Formalization through the SR Formula

Now, the nesting mechanism of the Anthropocene and the Machinocene can be formalized through the temporal structure of the SR formula. The material conditions of the Anthropocene can be modeled as an SR loop on a slow temporal scale (S_long → R_long). This loop includes long-term changes in the Earth system (stimulus) and collective responses of human society (response). In contrast, the reconstruction of sensation, attention, and judgment in the Machinocene is modeled as an SR loop on a fast temporal scale (S_short → R_short). This loop includes perceptual input mediated by algorithms (stimulus) and individuals' immediate judgments and actions (response).

Nesting occurs in the way these two loops intersect. Specifically, the fast loop of the Machinocene is "nested" within the slow loop of the Anthropocene. This is not a simple relationship of inclusion but a dynamic relationship in which the temporal scales of the two loops mutually influence each other. For example, the fast sensory reconstruction of the Machinocene (e.g., personalized framing of climate information by algorithms) changes the perception of the material conditions of the Anthropocene (interpretation of the stimulus in the slow loop). At the same time, the material conditions of the Anthropocene (e.g., the physical impacts of climate change) affect the data flow of the Machinocene (e.g., input to climate models).

This nesting can be formalized through the SR formula as follows:

S_long(t) → [S_short(t) → R_short(t+Δt1)] → R_long(t+Δt2)

Here, S_long is the stimulus on a slow temporal scale (e.g., changes in the Earth system), S_short is the stimulus on a fast temporal scale (e.g., sensory input reconstructed by algorithms), R_short is the fast response (e.g., individual judgment), and R_long is the slow response (e.g., social policy). Δt1 is the temporal delay of the fast loop (milliseconds to seconds), and Δt2 is the temporal delay of the slow loop (years to decades). In this formula, the fast loop operates within the slow loop, and the output of the fast loop (R_short) affects the response of the slow loop (R_long).

This formalization allows the relationship between the two layers to be understood not as a causal chain but as a hierarchy of temporal structures. The fast loop acts as an "internal clock" for the slow loop, potentially shortening the response time of the slow loop. However, at the same time, the feedback of the fast loop can disrupt the stability of the slow loop. This can be seen as a macroscopic version of model collapse. The fast feedback loop of the Machinocene risks overwhelming the slow feedback loop of the Anthropocene, replacing long-term changes in material conditions with sensory immediacy.
