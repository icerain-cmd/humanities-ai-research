# Ontological Boundaries of Artificial Nature: A Case Study of National Park Wildfire Monitoring Sensor Networks

## 1. Problem Setting — The Topology of Nature Reconfigured by Sensors

The satellite and drone sensor network for wildfire monitoring in national parks is not a mere observational tool. This network functions as an **ontological apparatus** that determines the **conditions of visibility**. Infrared detection sensors, thermal imaging cameras, and smoke particle analysis algorithms codify which states are classified as 'wildfire risk' and which temperature changes shift from 'caution' to 'alert'. In this process, **the reality of wildfire** is reconfigured as the output of detection thresholds, classification systems, and predictive models.

Ecological changes undetected by this sensor network simultaneously occupy **two topological statuses**. On one hand, they remain as externalities of nature; on the other hand, they are systematically excluded from the reality constructed by the management system. These two statuses appear to refer to the same thing but carry ontologically different implications.

## 2. Sensor Blind Spots — Externalities of Nature or Systematic Exclusion?

### 2.1 Changes Remaining as Externalities

Ecological changes undetected by sensors certainly exist outside the management system's **operative reality**. However, this cannot be directly reduced to 'nature's externality' in the traditional sense. The externality of nature presupposed by traditional ecological philosophy refers to **an objectivity that exists autonomously, independent of human cognition**. But the blind spots of the sensor network must be distinguished from 'nature itself' in this sense.

Changes not captured by sensors are not 'pure externalities' for the following reasons:

1. **Determination of Sensor Thresholds**: Every sensor has an operating range and sensitivity limits. Satellites cannot detect subtle temperature changes at 30 cm above the ground, but this is not due to nature's 'depth' but rather **the result of technical choices**. Which temperature ranges to detect, at what intervals to scan—these are determined by human budgets, technical capabilities, and management priorities.

2. **Boundary Effects of Classification Systems**: Specific airflow changes not classified as smoke in drone footage are treated as 'non-events'. This means data does not reflect nature **as it is**, but only constructs as reality what passes through the mesh of the classification system. Phenomena caught at the boundaries of classification disappear from the system as if they did not exist.

3. **Blind Spots of Predictive Models**: Machine learning-based wildfire prediction models only learn patterns included in the training data. If climate change causes unprecedented types of ecological precursor symptoms, these never even enter the model's output space.

### 2.2 The Paradox of Excluded Reality

Here, a crucial ontological shift occurs. Changes undetected by sensors become functionally equivalent to **non-existence** at the operational dimension of the system, rather than being 'existing but invisible'. This point is the sharpest issue in artificial nature ontology.

A chunk from Khoj Document 1 hints at this problem through the concept of Artura:
> "Even if Artura is a meaningful concept for elucidating the experiential conditions of the techno-genetic era, there remain considerable unresolved issues for it to be considered a complete theory in itself."

The Artura concept contains the insight that technology shapes **the conditions of experience**. The wildfire monitoring sensor network also shapes the conditions of experience regarding nature. Changes not captured by these conditions remain **unexperienceable**. However, this does not mean the changes are ontologically nothing. It is precisely this tension — existing yet outside the conditions of experience — that is the core challenge artificial nature ontology must resolve.

## 3. Collision Between Traditional Ecological Philosophy Premises and Data Ontology

### 3.1 Traditional Premise: Nature's Autonomous Objectivity

Deep within Western ecological philosophy lies the following premise:
- Nature is an autonomous substance that exists prior to human cognition and technological intervention.
- Nature's 'authenticity' is preserved when human intervention is minimized.
- Technical observation can only distort or capture a limited view of nature's actual state.
- Therefore, an 'unobserved nature' always exists, serving as both the limit of ecological management and the basis for drawing ethical boundaries.

Under this premise, the sensor's blind spots are considered **natural** and even evidence proving nature's autonomy. Because an invisible nature exists, nature remains an object humans cannot fully dominate.

### 3.2 Data Ontology's Claim: Simultaneous Construction of Visibility and Manageability

In contrast, the ontology of artificial nature asserts the following:
- Data, classification, and prediction devices do not **passively reflect** nature but **actively construct** it.
- Without the sensor network's classification system, the category of 'wildfire risk' itself would not exist.
- Visibility and manageability are two aspects of the same ontological act.
- Nature is actualized only within the network of technology, humans, and data.

From this perspective, changes undetected by sensors are close to **non-existent**. Since they are not within the system's ontological field, no action can be taken regarding them, and thus they do not contribute to management reality.

### 3.3 The Apex of Collision: The Ontology of Mites and Satellite Temperatures

Consider an example that starkly illustrates this collision: Suppose a **drastic change in soil mite populations** occurs in a specific area of a national park before a wildfire. This change is a biological precursor to wildfire, but the satellite and drone sensor network cannot detect it.

- Traditional ecological philosophy: The change in mite populations is an intrinsic signal of nature. It is a real change, even if we fail to detect it. This proves nature's autonomy and mystery.
- Data ontology: The change in mite populations may be a scientifically interesting fact, but it is a **non-operational variable in the management system's reality**. Unless the system integrates this data into collection, analysis, and action chains, it lies outside management reality.

This example sharpens the issue. The problem is not 'whether mites really exist'. The problem lies in the **ontological hierarchy**. Mites exist biologically, but within the ontological field of the sensor-management system, they are close to a state of **non-existence**. What artificial nature ontology captures is precisely this structure of **stratified existence**.

## 4. Interpretation via Artificial Nature Ontology — Connection with Khoj Documents

### 4.1 Relationship Between Generative Wonder and Uncanny Strangeness

A chunk from Khoj Document 2 argues that the four stages of AI-human contact (generative wonder, uncanny strangeness, critical reflection, relational resonance) form a single **topological structure**. This structure can be extended as a framework for analyzing the ontological effects of the wildfire monitoring sensor network.

When the sensor network is first established, the spectacle of real-time satellite imagery transmission and drones monitoring inaccessible areas evokes **generative wonder**. The illusion "Now we can see nature completely" operates. However, soon blind spots not captured by sensors, classification errors, false positives/negatives emerge, leading to **uncanny strangeness**. The gap between the nature constructed by technology and the nature we experience becomes apparent.

This uncanny strangeness is not merely an awareness of technical limitations but an experience of **ontological instability**. The certainty that nature stably exists 'out there' is shaken, and the fact that nature is only accessible through technological-data infrastructure is realized.

### 4.2 Artura's Experiential Conditions and Undetected Nature

Khoj Document 1 points out that Artura elucidates "the experiential conditions of the techno-genetic era." From this perspective, the wildfire monitoring sensor network is an Arturan apparatus. This apparatus determines what kinds of experience are possible and which are impossible.

However, as Document 1 warns, the "risk of over-inclusiveness" of the Artura concept must be avoided. If the sensor network is simply reduced to a single Artura, the analysis of **what nature it excludes** and **what reality it constructs** may be blunted. The key is to precisely trace the boundary between the reality the sensor network **constructs** and the reality it **excludes**.

### 4.3 Relational Structure of Tension

Khoj Document 3 notes that "tensions between concepts should be presented as relationships, not cards." This insight applies directly to the analytical methodology. In the sensor network case, the tension between nature's externality and management reality should be understood as a **relational tension**, not an **oppositional dichotomy**.

Nature's externality and data-constructed reality are not statically fixed poles but exist in a dynamically co-determining relationship:
- As the sensor network becomes more precise, the scope of 'undetected nature' shrinks, but simultaneously its boundary becomes sharper.
- As the classification system becomes more granular, the ontological 'invisibility' of the unclassified is reinforced.
- As predictive models become more accurate, changes outside the model are more dramatically converted into 'unreality'.

This relational tension forms a **structure where nature's externality is paradoxically reinforced by the construction of data reality**. Traditional nature does not disappear; rather, it is redefined as the **antipode** of data-constructed reality.

## 5. Conclusion: New Tasks for Artificial Nature Ontology

### 5.1 Redefining Nature's Externality

The case of the national park wildfire monitoring sensor network demonstrates the following: Ecological changes undetected by sensors **simultaneously** remain as nature's externalities and are systematically excluded from the reality constructed by the management system. This 'simultaneity' is not a paradox but the starting point of artificial nature ontology.

The issue is not choosing between these two statuses, but **analyzing the dynamics of this dual status**. Traditional ecological philosophy emphasizes only nature's externality and overlooks the constructive power of data reality. Data-centric management discourse emphasizes only constructed reality and ignores the ontological status of the excluded. Artificial nature ontology must **directly analyze the coexistence of these two statuses**.

### 5.2 From 'Epistemic Gap' to 'Ontological Boundary'

This case also suggests that the traditional concept of an 'epistemic gap' is no longer sufficient. The epistemic humility of "we cannot fully know nature" acquires new meaning under the ontological condition that "nature is actualized only through technological-data infrastructure."

The problem is not a lack of knowledge but **the conditioning of existence**. Changes outside the sensor network exist **beyond the field of manageable reality**, more than simply being unknown to us. This is not merely a limit of knowledge but a boundary of the techno-ontological system.

### 5.3 Reconstructing Artificial Nature and Ecological Responsibility

The most practical implication is as follows: If nature is actualized only through technological-data systems, then **ecological responsibility** should begin not with preserving nature 'as it is', but with reflecting on **how this system operates**.

The blind spots of the wildfire monitoring sensor network are not technical limitations but the result of **ontological decisions**. Which changes to detect, what to place at the boundaries of classification, which predictive model to choose — these are ultimately ontological choices about **what kind of nature should become reality**. Faced with this choice, we are no longer neutral observers but **actors participating in constructing nature's reality**.

At this point, it becomes necessary to transition to the stages of **critical reflection** and **relational resonance** presented in Khoj Document 2. Critical reflection is the stage of deconstructively analyzing the ontological conditions under which the sensor network operates. Relational resonance is the stage of reconstructing the triadic relationship of human-technology-nature not as a fixed hierarchy but as a process of **co-individuation**.
