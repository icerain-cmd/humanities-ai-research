## Question

Suppose a museum in Paris in 2026 operates an exhibition that uses tactile feedback gloves and spatial tracking sensors to provide visually impaired visitors with real-time translations of a sculpture's surface, weight, and sense of distance. Is this experience a complement that extends the sensory presence of the original work into another sense, or an event that regenerates the original work as a new sensory object through sensor measurements and algorithmic transformations? In particular, examine whether the direct contact between body and world presupposed by phenomenological empiricism can be maintained in perception mediated by transformation rules and interfaces, and if so, under what conditions and limitations.

## Analysis Results

# Analysis: The Ontological Status of the Tactile Glove Exhibition — Complement or Event

---

## 1. Decomposition of the Problem: Ontological Presuppositions of the Two Interpretations

The question attempts to capture a single empirical case through two opposing ontologies. Without first explicitly decomposing each presupposition, the answer risks falling into a forced choice.

**Presuppositions of Complement Theory:** The original work possesses a complete sensory presence. This presence is not limited to vision but includes the totality of qualities belonging to the work's material reality (surface texture, sense of weight, spatial volume, etc.). The tactile glove is merely an instrumental medium that helps a portion of this presence 'translate' to the visually impaired visitor. The core of this presupposition is **the identity of the original work** — even if the mode of experience changes, the object of experience is considered identical.

**Presuppositions of Event Theory:** Between the original work and the tactile experience lies not continuity but discontinuity. The sensor **measures** the physical properties of the original work, the algorithm **transforms** these measurements into data for another sensory modality, and the glove **outputs** this transformed data. Each step in this series involves interpretation and choice — the sensor's sampling frequency, the resolution of tactile feedback, the pressure-texture mapping rules, latency, etc. The sum of these choices generates a sensory object qualitatively different from the original work. The core of this presupposition is **the discontinuity of generation** — the experience is not a transmission of the original work but an event newly occurring under technical conditions.

These two interpretations are not merely differences in perspective; they entail opposing stances on the ontological status of the object (identity/generation), the role of the medium (transparent tool/constitutive condition), and the temporality of experience (representation/event).

---

## 2. Phenomenological Grounds of Complement Theory and Its Internal Tensions

### 2.1. Merleau-Ponty and the Direct Contact of Body-World

The most powerful insight of phenomenological empiricism is that the body is not merely a receptor that receives the world, but is itself a **mode of being** in contact with the world. For Merleau-Ponty, perception is not the passive reception of sensory data, but a way of already 'participating' in the world through the body's motility (motricité) and schema (schéma corporel). "My body is my point of view on the world," and at the same time, "the medium through which the world is given to me."

Within this framework, the visually impaired visitor's experience of 'touching' the sculpture through tactile gloves can be regarded as a legitimate perceptual event in which the existence of the original work is present at the fingertips. As long as the glove transmits tactile data, the body is in contact with the world. Especially if the glove has sufficiently low latency and high resolution, allowing the visitor to focus on the surface sensation of the sculpture without being aware of the glove's mediation, the possibility opens for the glove to be **embodied** into the body schema, similar to the way a blind person's cane becomes an extension of the hand.

### 2.2. However: The Invisible Third Term Introduced by Transformation Rules

Here a crucial difference arises. The blind person's cane is a mechanical extension — the vibrations and resistance transmitted to the tip occur through a direct dynamic relationship between the hand's motility and the world's physical structure. The mediation of the cane is **transparent**. In contrast, the tactile glove system includes the following invisible layers of transformation:

1. **The sensor's selective measurement**: The spatial tracking sensor and surface scanner decide which physical properties of the sculpture to measure and which to ignore. Color, gloss, temperature, smell, etc., are typically outside the measurement range. The presence of the original work is already reduced in this selection.

2. **The algorithm's transformation rules**: How are the measured surface distances and shapes mapped into the intensity and patterns of tactile vibrations? This rule is constructed by — the software engineer's decisions, physiological assumptions about human tactile perception, and the museum's curatorial purposes. This rule is **invisible** to the visitor. They cannot know which physical property of the original work corresponds to the tactile sensation they experience, or through which algorithmic decisions it is transmitted.

3. **The interface's sensory modality transformation**: This is the most fundamental issue. The sensation provided by the tactile glove is **not the surface texture of the original work itself**. It is a tactile pattern generated by vibration motors or pneumatic actuators. This is structurally identical to a kind of **sensory substitution** that converts visual data into auditory data. There is an ontological discontinuity between the marble's coolness of the original work and the vibration pattern of the glove.

These three layers fundamentally shake the "identity of the original work" presupposed by complement theory. The sensory data that has undergone transformation is not a property of the original work, but a **new sensory fact** generated under separate technical conditions, albeit based on measurements of the original work.

## 3. Grounds of Event Theory: Artura and Media Aesthetics in the Age of Technogenesis

### 3.1. Connection with the Concept of Artura

The concept of **Artura** mentioned in Reference 2 provides direct theoretical language for this problem. In the age of technological reproducibility, Benjamin's notion of the loss of aura signified the destruction of the 'original's' uniqueness and distance by reproductive technology. Artura traces a different trajectory — instead of destroying the original's aura, technological reproduction **generates a new type of generative aura under technical conditions**.

When capturing the tactile glove exhibition through the concept of Artura:

- This experience does not restore or extend the aura of the original work (sense of distance, uniqueness, authenticity)
- It is understood as a **new mode of sensory presence** generated by the technical trinity of sensor-algorithm-interface
- This presence 'depends' on the original work but possesses ontological autonomy that is not 'reducible' to it

As Reference 2 points out, Artura has "the risk of over-inclusiveness in exchange for explanatory power," and "the distinction of layers with generative aura" needs to be more clearly refined. However, this case is an area where the explanatory power of the Artura concept is most vividly revealed — because it can capture the **ontological conjunction** between the original work (sculpture) and the technical experience (tactile glove output).

### 3.2. Application of the Four-Stage Topology of Contact

The four-stage topology of 'AI and Human Contact' from Reference 3 provides a framework for analyzing the temporal unfolding of this experience:

1. **Generative Wonder**: The wonder of sensory expansion that occurs when first putting on the glove and 'touching' the sculpture. The experience of an invisible form being reproduced at the fingertips evokes a powerful aesthetic emotion. At this stage, the visitor is likely to experience it as 'miraculous,' and the intuition of complement theory operates most strongly.

2. **Uncanny Discomfort**: The stage where, through continued experience, the visitor begins to sense the 'heterogeneity' of the sensation. Questions arise: "Is this really the surface of the sculpture?", "Where does this vibration I feel come from?" This is the point where the invisibility of the transformation rules turns into experiential discomfort.

3. **Critical Reflection**: The visitor begins to consciously examine the technical conditions surrounding this experience. They recognize that the tactile sensation is not a simple transmission but a transformed-generated one, and question its conditions (limitations of the sensor, choices of the algorithm, resolution of the interface).

4. **Relational Resonance**: This stage is conditional — the moment when the visitor, moving beyond critical reflection, accepts the technical conditions as part of the experience, and a new aesthetic experience emerges within the relational network of original work-sensor-algorithm-body. This experience is no longer 'the sensation of the original work' nor a 'purely technical construct,' but a third ontological domain arising from the **resonant relationship** between human and technology.

What this topology shows is that the experience is not **fixed** as either complement or event, but **oscillates** between the two interpretations over time. The initial experience is experienced as complement theory, but with reflection moves toward event theory, and ultimately opens the possibility of moving to a third position (relational resonance) while maintaining the tension between the two interpretations.

## 4. Conditions and Limitations of Phenomenological Direct Contact

### 4.1. Conditions Under Which Direct Contact Can Be Maintained

Within the framework of phenomenological empiricism, the conditions under which direct body-world contact can be maintained in perception mediated by transformation rules and interfaces are as follows:

**(a) Embodiment of the Interface**
When the glove meets sufficiently low latency (<20ms), natural tactile mapping, and minimal interference with wearing comfort, the visitor can focus on the sensation of the sculpture without being 'conscious' of the glove. At this point, the glove functions as an extension of what Merleau-Ponty called the **habitual body (corps habituel)** — just as a typist types without being aware of the keyboard. When this condition is met, the visitor's intentionality is directed toward the sculpture, not the glove, and the experience is direct.

**(b) Intrinsic Continuity of Sensory Modality**
A tactile-to-tactile transformation (e.g., a tactile sensor measuring the surface texture of the sculpture and transmitting it as a different kind of tactile output) is ontologically more direct than a cross-modal transformation (e.g., converting visual data into tactile output). In the latter case, color-texture mapping approaches an arbitrary symbolic relationship. In the former case, since the transformation is within the same sensory modality, the claim of continuity gains more persuasiveness.

**(c) Physical Co-presence with the Original Work**
When the visitor stands in the actual space where the sculpture is located and uses the glove within the spatial context of the sculpture, the experience is anchored in the 'site-specificity of the sculpture.' However, this condition is paradoxically the most fragile — if providing the same tactile data in a VR space without the sculpture yields no significant difference in the quality of experience, then physical co-presence is proven not to be a necessary condition for the experience.

### 4.2. Limitations of Direct Contact — The Self-Boundary of Phenomenology

Even if the above conditions are met, the following limitations constrain the claim of phenomenological direct contact:

**(a) Ontological Invisibility of Transformation Rules**
As previously noted, the transformation rules can neither be consciously perceived within the experience nor fully visualized outside the experience. The visitor cannot know, through experiential intuition alone, which physical property of the original work the sensation they feel comes from, or through which algorithmic decisions it was generated. This fundamentally violates the **self-evidence of experience** presupposed by phenomenology. The experience is direct, but the **conditions** of that experience are invisible to the experiencer.

**(b) Irreversibility of Transformation**
The visual presence of the original work (the reflection of light on marble, the visual contour of the form, the three-dimensionality in space) is 'translated' into tactile data, but this process is **irreversible**. The visual presence of the original work cannot be 'reconstructed' from the tactile data. This means the translation is not an extension but a **generative transformation**. The 'body-world reciprocity' demanded by phenomenology encounters a limit before this irreversibility — because the way the world (the original work) is given to the body is decisively shaped by technical conditions.

**(c) Problem of Algorithmic Standardization**
The transformation rules of the tactile glove are not tailored to the unique bodily sensations of a particular visitor, but are designed based on a 'general model of tactile perception.' This conflicts with the **uniqueness of the body (le corps propre)** emphasized by phenomenology — each visitor's tactile sensitivity, motor habits, body temperature, skin thickness, etc., are ignored or averaged. Direct contact should be the contact between 'my body' and 'this world,' but here the contact occurs between a 'standardized body' and a 'measured world.'

## 5. Conclusion: Neither Complement Nor Event — The Ontological Maintenance of Tension

### 5.1. Dialectical Overcoming of the Two Interpretations

The conclusion presented by this analysis is that the ontological status of the tactile glove exhibition **cannot be determined as either complement or event**. Instead, this experience exists within a **structure of tension** as follows:

- At the **phenomenal dimension** of experience (the feeling that I am directly touching the sculpture), complement theory is valid
- At the **conditional dimension** of experience (how this feeling is constructed), event theory is valid
- These two dimensions do not negate each other but coexist as **two layers of a single experience**

This aligns with what the four-stage topology of Reference 3 suggests — the experience is not fixed in a single ontological position, but oscillates between complement and event over time and according to the depth of reflection.

### 5.2. Tension Between Phenomenology and Media Aesthetics in the Age of Technogenesis

The 'tension between phenomenological empiricism and media aesthetics in the age of technogenesis,' given as the reference context of the question, is concretized in this case as follows:

The direct contact presupposed by phenomenological empiricism is partially maintained under the condition of **interface embodiment**. However, this directness is always already a mediated directness, limited by the **invisibility of transformation rules** and **algorithmic standardization**. That is, directness is an **effect** established upon the invisibility of technical conditions, not an a priori presupposition.

Media aesthetics in the age of technogenesis directly problematizes this invisibility. What the concept of Artura captures is precisely this point — the structure in which technology constitutes experience while concealing its own constitutive activity. The 'magic' of the tactile glove works most perfectly when it does not reveal itself as magic.

### 5.3. Theoretical Implications and Remaining Questions

The fundamental implication raised by this analysis is as follows:

There is an ethical dimension introduced by the context of disability accessibility. Complement theory provides an inclusive narrative of providing 'the same art experience' to visually impaired visitors, but event theory reveals that this service actually provides a separate experience. Depending on which interpretation is chosen, the design principles of accessibility technology (faithful transmission of the original work vs. respect for sensory autonomy) differ.

Furthermore, the ontological status of the original work itself is shaken. If the tactile glove experience is not 'the sensation of the original work,' then the original work is no longer a single stable entity but must be re-understood as an **ontological bundle that differentiates into various sensory objects through various technical mediations**. This suggests that for the concept of Artura to acquire theoretical precision beyond "the risk of over-inclusiveness," it must precisely describe this structure of **differentiation**.
