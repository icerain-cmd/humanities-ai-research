# The Dilemma of Generative Kiosks in Public Libraries and Ecological Legitimacy
## — An Ontological Analysis of the Costs Externalized by Expanded Accessibility

---

## 1. Structuring the Core Issue: Tensions at Three Levels

When a public library seeks to introduce generative translation and summarization kiosks to improve information access for migrants and older adults, the problem lies in a **conflict structure in which one good excludes another**. This conflict operates on at least three levels.

| Level | Content | Structure of Conflict |
|------|------|-----------|
| **Social** | Guaranteeing the right to information for migrants (language barriers) and older adults (digital literacy) | Universal access rights vs. ecological limits |
| **Technological** | Server computation for generative AI (electricity and cooling water) and device replacement (e-waste) | Real-time accuracy vs. material sustainability |
| **Institutional** | Criteria for evaluating the public value of public libraries | User numbers/satisfaction vs. distribution of transferred costs |

The most challenging aspect of this structure is that **expanded accessibility (social justice) and the externalization of environmental costs (ecological justice) do not simply conflict within the same budgetary and institutional framework; rather, they emerge in entirely different temporal and spatial zones**. This asymmetry means that users standing in front of the kiosk gain convenience, while the material conditions of that convenience are **spatially and temporally dispersed** across (1) the electricity and water resources of regions where data centers are located, (2) future waste-disposal costs, and (3) environmental damage at rare-earth mining sites used to produce the devices.

---

## 2. Three Limitations of Conventional Criteria for Evaluating Public Value

### 2.1 The Error of the Efficiency Criterion

If the introduction of library kiosks is evaluated in terms of the **service cost per user**, the project may be judged “efficient” because, although its initial installation costs are high, its unit cost declines as the number of users increases. However, this calculation omits from the project’s cost items the **carbon emissions generated in producing the electricity consumed by server computation**, **the depletion of local water resources caused by cooling-water use**, and **the disposal costs of devices that will be discarded three to five years later**. This is merely accounting efficiency, not ecological efficiency.

### 2.2 The Error of the User-Number Criterion (Accessibility Indicator)

An evaluation such as “1,000 monthly active users → success” does not ask **who bears the material conditions of that access**. This produces the following paradox:

> Information access has improved, but because the material foundations of that information—power grids, data centers, fiber-optic cables, and devices—contribute to the climate crisis, **the information infrastructure itself of the most vulnerable groups, including migrants and older adults, is threatened in the long term**.

### 2.3 The Error of the Satisfaction Criterion (Quality of Experience)

The satisfaction that “the translation is accurate and the summary is useful” is limited to the user’s immediate experience at the moment of service use. Here, **Document 2** in the Khoj references offers an important implication:

> Document 2 (The Four Stages of Human–AI Contact): the topology of “generative wonder → uncanny strangeness → critical reflection → relational resonance”

Users in front of the kiosk may experience “generative wonder” (“The translation is so smooth!”), but this wonder is possible because the material conditions required for the kiosk to operate—electricity, cooling water, and resources—have been `invisibilized`. Satisfaction can be established on the basis of **ignorance regarding the concealment of material conditions**. A genuinely public evaluation must bring the stage of “critical reflection” among these four stages into the institutional framework.

## 3. Temporal and Spatial Asymmetries: Three Forms of Externalization

The first step in designing the ecological legitimacy of digital transformation is to **conceptually distinguish the types of externalization**.

### 3.1 Spatial Asymmetry (Backend Externalization)

If the kiosk’s generative AI computation takes place in a **remote cloud data center** rather than on a server inside the library, the electricity consumption and cooling-water use occur somewhere other than the area where the library is located. In countries with high electricity demand, such as South Korea, additional server computation may trigger the operation of **marginal generating sources—typically LNG or coal**. These costs are borne not by **library users or local residents**, but by residents living near power plants and the ecosystems surrounding data centers. The benefits of the service are concentrated on the person standing in front of the kiosk, while its costs are dispersed among unspecified populations in remote locations.

### 3.2 Temporal Asymmetry (Externalization into the Future)

Kiosk terminals have an average lifespan of three to five years. The costs of processing the electronic waste generated when they are replaced are borne through **future public budgets** or **informal waste-disposal channels, such as exports to developing countries**. In addition, the carbon emissions from server computation accumulate over the long term as climate change, constraining the **conditions of survival of future generations rather than those of current users**. This temporal asymmetry is difficult to resolve through democratic decision-making structures—future generations cannot vote on the evaluation of current library projects.

### 3.3 Ontological Asymmetry (Nonhuman Externalization)

At the most fundamental level, the material conditions of digital services transfer costs to **nonhuman entities—watersheds, the atmosphere, and ecosystems—that lie beyond the scope of human decision-making**. Rising river temperatures caused by cooling-water use, soil contamination caused by rare-earth mining, and air pollution caused by the incineration of electronic waste all represent asymmetries that **exceed the framework of inter-human justice presupposed by conventional concepts of the public good**.

## 4. Designing Ecological Legitimacy: Four Directions

### 4.1 Introducing Full-Cost Accounting

The evaluation of public library projects should include an **expanded set of budget items**:

- **Electricity costs of server computation** (including carbon costs beyond direct expenditures)
- **Opportunity costs of cooling water** (considering alternative uses of the relevant water resources)
- **Full life-cycle costs of terminals** (total costs from production and operation through disposal)
- **Contributions to network infrastructure** (a portion of the costs of maintaining telecommunications networks)

This reflects the warning in **Document 1** of Khoj concerning the concept of Artura: “If the scope of a concept is not carefully defined, there is a risk of over-inclusiveness.” Full-cost accounting can likewise fail if it attempts to measure everything. It should therefore be introduced incrementally, beginning with **items that can be directly measured**, such as electricity consumption and carbon emissions.

### 4.2 A Framework of Resonant Accountability

This approach institutionalizes the trajectory from “critical reflection” to “relational resonance” described in Document 2. The following would be required in the decision-making process for introducing the kiosks:

- **Mapping asymmetries**: Visualizing whose costs are being transferred by the service
- **Participation by affected parties**: Soliciting input from residents of data-center locations and communities near electronic-waste processing sites
- **Representation for future generations**: Including impact projections for 10-, 30-, and 50-year horizons in the project plan

Under such an arrangement, the kiosk could function not merely as a convenience device but as **a device that reveals its own material conditions**. One possible measure would be to display information on the kiosk screen such as: “This translation was processed at the △△ Data Center, generating approximately ◇ grams of CO₂ in the process.”

### 4.3 Ontological Expansion: Connecting with WE and Co-Resonating Subjects

The concept of **WE/co-resonating subjects** mentioned in Document 1 plays a central role in this analysis. WE (Wired Ecology) is a framework that understands the entanglement of humans, technology, and ecosystems as a single system. A generative translation service on a kiosk exists only within a WE composed of **terminal (UI) → network (fiber-optic cables and routers) → server (CPU/GPU computation) → power grid (power plants and transmission lines) → cooling water (local water resources)**.

If this WE becomes the basic unit of project evaluation, it is no longer possible to evaluate only the “user standing in front of the kiosk.” A **co-resonating subject** refers to an entity that detects and responds to the vibrations occurring at each node of this WE—electricity consumption, heat generation, data flows, and user experience—as a single event. Public value should be evaluated according to whether this co-resonating subject can attend to all the nodes of the WE.

### 4.4 The Slow Kiosk as a Device for Modulating Speed

One concrete proposal is that generative kiosks need not always operate in their “fastest and most accurate” mode. The following **tiered operating modes** could be designed:

- **High-precision mode** (real-time translation and summarization; intensive server computation; high carbon emissions): For complex legal or medical information requests
- **Lightweight mode** (a lightweight local model; limited functions; lower carbon emissions): For everyday information searches
- **Basic offline mode** (pre-cached content; minimal power consumption): During periods of high electricity demand or power outages

By modulating speed in this way, it becomes possible to seek a **dynamic balance between accessibility (speed) and ecological sustainability (slowness)**. Public value is redefined not as **providing the fastest service to everyone**, but as **the capacity to distribute appropriate speeds and burdens according to need**.

---

## 5. Conclusion: Toward a Redefinition of Public Value

The case of generative kiosks in public libraries calls for the following shift in how public value is evaluated:

| Conventional Public Value | Proposed Ecological Public Value |
|------------|---------------------|
| Measured by the number of users | Measured by the distribution of all affected entities |
| Efficiency evaluated through unit service costs | Evaluated through full life-cycle costs |
| Quality of experience evaluated through immediate satisfaction | Evaluated by the degree to which material conditions are made visible |
| Human users considered exclusively | Humans, nonhumans, and future generations included |
| Pursuit of synchronized convenience | Dynamic speed modulation according to conditions |

The direction of “connecting with WE/co-resonating subjects” in Document 1 and the trajectory from “critical reflection → relational resonance” in Document 2 converge at this point. **Genuine public value should be evaluated not by how many people a service reaches, but by how transparently the material conditions of its existence are revealed and how many subjects are able to respond to those conditions through resonance.**
