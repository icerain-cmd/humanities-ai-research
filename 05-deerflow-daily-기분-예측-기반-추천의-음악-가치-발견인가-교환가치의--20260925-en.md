# Media Aesthetics-Value in the Age of Technogenesis: Generative Recommendation Systems and the (Im)material Transformation of Musical Value

## 1. Introduction: The Shift in the Coordinate System of Value

On a streaming platform equipped with generative music features, users' listening history and mood prediction data are not mere byproducts of consumption but operate as core fuel that determines the exposure order and recommendation value of tracks. This signifies a new phase in which music is no longer enjoyed as a 'work,' but rather relational attributes such as 'situational fit' and 'stickiness'—calculated by algorithmic predictive models—replace aesthetic value. While traditional aesthetics sought value in the formal novelty within a work or the author's intention, today's platform economy takes efficiency itself—making user responses predictable—as the criterion of value. If so, does this process 'discover' users' latent tastes, or does it disguise exchange value designed by the platform in aesthetic language? This question goes beyond mere technological ethics, revealing a shift in the ontological ground that media aesthetics faces in the age of technogenesis.

This paper examines this issue by connecting Gilbert Simondon's concept of 'concrétisation' (concretization) with generative AI's 'synthetic data feedback loop' and 'model collapse.' Through this, it will argue that the recommendation system is not simply a tool for discovering existing value, but a 'technical object' that constructs its own evolutionary conditions through interaction with users. Furthermore, by critically comparing whether this evolution of the technical object moves toward the open concretization Simondon envisioned or becomes trapped in a regressive closed loop of data self-consumption, this paper proposes a new framework of aesthetic interpretation that goes beyond the dichotomy of 'discovery of value' and 'disguise of exchange value.' In conclusion, this issue must be reinterpreted from the perspective of an eco-digital environment—an artificial nature in which human cultural practices are entangled with technological infrastructure. In this context, aesthetic value is no longer an attribute of the work but a relational product that the platform's infrastructure produces and distributes.

## 2. The Crisis of Traditional Aesthetics: The Death of the Work and the Birth of Response

The axiology of traditional aesthetics, as clearly shown in Immanuel Kant's 'disinterested pleasure,' is based on pure aesthetic judgment separated from subjective interests or practical purposes. Clive Bell, often called the father of formalist aesthetics, argued that 'significant form' is the sole criterion of value in a work of art, and Edmund Hanslick held that the beauty of music lies in the inherent lawfulness of acoustic form. From this perspective, the value of a work is immanent in the work itself, independent of the author's intention, social context, and the viewer's subjective response. Th. W. Adorno, while inheriting this formalist legacy, criticized the capitalist culture industry for 'standardizing' music and paralyzing the listener's autonomous judgment through 'pseudo-individuality.' For him, authentic music was 'serious music' that resists reality by internalizing social contradictions within its form.

However, in a streaming environment dominated by generative recommendation systems, this traditional axiology faces a fundamental crisis. First, the boundary between the 'inside' and 'outside' of the work collapses. Recommendation algorithms analyze the formal attributes of a track (tempo, key, instrumentation), but the purpose of this analysis is not to elucidate the work's immanent meaning but to extract 'features' for predicting user response. Here, music is no longer a self-sufficient object but is reduced to an 'event' that acquires meaning within a network of user-platform-algorithm relations. Second, the author's intention disappears. As generative AI can infinitely produce music of a specific genre, 'who made it' can no longer serve as a criterion for value judgment. Instead, 'how it is classified and exposed' determines the mode of existence of the work. Third, the listener's 'response' becomes the full-scale measure of aesthetic value. Behavioral data such as skips, repeated plays, listening duration, and likes become indicators not of a work's formal completeness but of the user's 'stickiness.'

This change is not merely an expansion of aesthetic judgment criteria but a transformation of the ontological status of value itself. In traditional aesthetics, value was 'discovered.' That is, the aesthetic attributes of a work exist objectively, independent of subjective response, and the viewer acquires aesthetic experience through them. However, in the platform economy, value is 'produced.' Algorithms predict future behavior based on users' past actions, and the higher the accuracy of that prediction, the more dwell time and ad exposure are generated. Here, 'situational fit' is a strategic concept that increases prediction accuracy by providing music that best matches the user's current mood, activity, time of day, and location. The problem is that this fit does not 'reflect' the user's aesthetic taste but rather 'constitutes' taste by learning and reinforcing probabilistic expectations of what the user will listen to. This follows a circular logic similar to how the market does not discover consumer preferences but creates them through advertising and marketing.

## 3. Simondon's Concretization and the Evolution of Recommendation Systems

In *On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects*, Simondon argues that a technical object is not merely a passive tool but a 'technical individual' that evolves with an internally coherent structure. He explains the evolution of technical objects not as 'discovery' but as 'concrétisation' (concretization). Early technical objects remain in an 'abstract' state where various disparate functions are externally combined, but as they evolve, they transition to a 'concrete' state where each function presupposes and reinforces the others. For example, in a car engine, functions such as cooling, fuel supply, and power transmission were initially separate, but gradually the parts become integrated in a way that each becomes a condition for the existence of the others. This process of concretization is an 'open' process in which the technical object solves its own problems and evolves in relation to its environment.

Applying this concept to recommendation systems, early music recommendation algorithms started from simple rule-based approaches (e.g., 'play music from a genre the user likes') and evolved through collaborative filtering, content-based filtering, and current deep learning-based generative models. This process can be interpreted as a Simondonian 'concretization.' Early recommendation systems had user data and music attribute data separated, but current systems integrate user behavioral data, acoustic features of music, text metadata, and even user biometric signals (e.g., heart rate) into a single predictive model. Here, each data source is not simply combined but integrated in a way that compensates for each other's deficiencies to increase the model's prediction accuracy. For example, the 'cold start' problem for new users is solved by transfer learning features from popular music, which is a form of concretization where the technical object improves its own structure through interaction with the environment.

From this perspective, the process by which a recommendation system predicts a user's mood and provides music accordingly is not simply a discovery of 'already existing' user tastes but an event of 'technogenesis' that creates new entanglements among user, machine, and music. According to Simondon, the concretization of a technical object proceeds not only according to the object's own internal laws but also through co-evolution with its 'associated milieu.' The associated milieu of a recommendation system is precisely the user's listening behavior data and the cultural context in which that data is generated. Therefore, the algorithm's prediction of the user's mood and generation of matching music can be interpreted as a process in which the system creates its own survival conditions (user attention), much like a living organism adapts to its environment. Here, 'value' is no longer found in the formal attributes of the work but arises from the degree of 'fit' that the system successfully constructs through interaction with the user. In other words, value is not discovered but 'produced' through the concretization process of the technical object.

## 4. Generative AI's Feedback Loop and Model Collapse: Signs of Regressive Closure

However, the optimistic outlook of Simondonian concretization cracks in the face of generative AI's 'synthetic data feedback loop' and 'model collapse.' Model collapse refers to a phenomenon where, when a generative AI model repeatedly uses its own synthetic data as training data, the model loses the tail of the original data distribution and eventually suffers a drastic reduction in diversity, failing to reproduce the original data distribution. This concept was formally established in a 2023 study by Shumailov et al. and has since been empirically observed in various generative models. (This paper discusses this phenomenon at a conceptual level without citing specific experimental figures from particular studies.)

Applying this concept to music streaming platforms, the following regressive cycle occurs. First, the platform trains a 'mood prediction' model based on users' listening history. Second, this model generates or recommends music that the user might listen to when in a specific mood. Third, when the user repeatedly listens to these recommendations, that behavioral data is fed back as training data. The problem is that this feedback operates in a direction that simply reinforces the user's existing tastes. The more generative AI creates music tailored to the user's predicted tastes, the more the user is exposed only within that prediction range. Here, the user's listening history is no longer a record of 'natural' musical preferences but a record of choices made within the 'narrow gate' created by the algorithm. As this contaminated data is used to train the model again, the model learns increasingly extreme probability distributions.

This process should be called not Simondonian concretization but rather a 'regressive closure loop.' Simondon's concretization is an 'open' process in which the technical object integrates more functions and opens up broader possibilities through interaction with the environment. In contrast, the feedback loop of generative AI 'closes' by eliminating diversity and maximizing only predictability through data self-consumption. This is an 'artificial degeneration' similar to an ecosystem where a particular species' population grows excessively, causing the food chain to collapse. The model that predicts the user's mood may initially generate diverse musical expressions, but as the feedback loop repeats, it produces only 'safe' choices—music similar to what the user has already responded to. This is a new form of the 'standardization' Adorno criticized, but with a crucial difference: while Adorno's standardization was driven by industrial production methods, today's standardization is 'voluntarily' internalized through the user's behavioral data.

This regressive closure renders the claim of 'discovery of value' fictitious. If the recommendation system were discovering the user's latent tastes, it would need to present musical territories the user has not yet experienced. However, a system trapped in a feedback loop optimizes 'predictability' by repeatedly presenting only what the user already knows and has already responded to. Here, 'situational fit' is not a concept that expands the user's aesthetic experience but a mechanism of control that fixes the user as a predictable consumer. Listening to music that matches a specific mood is certainly a 'fitting' experience, but this fit operates by blocking the 'openness' of how the user might interpret and express that mood, subordinating the user to the 'standardized pattern of mood' learned by the algorithm. Therefore, this process is not a discovery of value but rather an 'aesthetic disguise of exchange value,' where the structure of value production-distribution is subordinated to the platform's revenue model.

In this case, 'situational fit' is a positive element that enriches the user's aesthetic experience, and value is understood as a 'relational attribute' newly created through the interaction between algorithm and user.
