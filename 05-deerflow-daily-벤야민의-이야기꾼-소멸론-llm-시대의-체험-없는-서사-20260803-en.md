# Analysis: Is Benjamin's "The Storyteller" Diagnosis Completed or Collapsed in the Age of LLMs?

## Introduction: The Precise Coordinates of the Problem

The precision of the question already defines the direction of the analysis. What Benjamin diagnosed in "The Storyteller" is not simply the "extinction of storytelling" but a **historical change in the condition of the transmissibility of experience (Erfahrbarkeit) itself**. The phenomenon of "story without experience" generated by LLMs targets the exact point of this diagnosis. This analysis proceeds in three stages: (1) reconstruct the internal logic of Benjamin's diagnosis, (2) examine two opposing implications of LLM-generated narratives for this logic, and then (3) propose the direction of theoretical revision demanded by these implications.

---

## 1. Three Layers of Benjamin's Diagnosis: Experience → Lived Experience → Information

Benjamin's argument is not a single process but a structure with three overlapping layers.

### 1.1. First Layer: The Rupture of Experience by War

The starting point of "The Storyteller" is the **silence** of soldiers returning from World War I. "People who returned from the battlefield had become silent. Not richer in communicable experience, but poorer." This silence is not simply the result of trauma. It points to **the collapse of the anthropological form of experience (Erfahrung) itself**. For Benjamin, Erfahrung is not simply "something that happened" but something that can be transmitted and accumulated within the time of a community, integrated into the lives of others as advice. The shock of war mediated by technology (trench warfare) exceeded the scope of such transmissible experience.

### 1.2. Second Layer: Decomposition into Lived Experience (Erlebnis)

Benjamin analyzes this collapse more finely. As experience (Erfahrung) collapses, it is replaced by **isolated, momentary shock: lived experience (Erlebnis)**. This distinction is crucial. If Erfahrung is experience integrated within the time of tradition and community, Erlebnis is a shock experienced by an individual in a disconnected moment, blocked by a defense mechanism (shock-defense) rather than transmitted. Here, Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" connects. The way film, as a medium, reorganizes the audience's perception through continuous shocks is precisely the medial form corresponding to this regime of lived experience (Erlebnis). **The decay of the aura** is the media-aesthetic expression of the argument of the storyteller—that the communal and ritual conditions for transmitting experience have collapsed.

### 1.3. Third Layer: Replacement by Information (Information)

However, Benjamin's diagnosis does not stop here. Lived experience (Erlebnis) does not simply disappear; it is transformed into the form of **information (Information)**. The decisive property of information is clearly stated by Benjamin:

> "Information pays the price of being immediately verifiable. (...) Information is no longer valid the moment it has passed. The story, unlike information, does not explain itself. Instead of conveying every fact with psychological plausibility, the story draws facts into the realm of astonishment and strangeness."

The important point here is that information presupposes **a record of some lived experience**. Information is a statement of the fact "this happened," and that fact is verifiable as a record of something someone experienced (Erlebnis). Information is based on lived experience, but compressed and standardized into a transmissible form. Therefore, information has the property of being "understandable" and "providing an explanation."

## 2. The Paradox of LLM-Generated Narratives: The Emergence of 'Story Without Experience'

Now we examine the relationship between LLM-generated narratives and Benjamin's three-layer structure.

### 2.1. Ontological Condition of LLM Narratives

The fundamental characteristic of narratives generated by LLMs is that they are **completions of statistical patterns**. An LLM has no experience (Erlebnis) or experience (Erfahrung) of any world. It learns patterns from a corpus of human-produced text and predicts the most probabilistically appropriate next token for a given prompt. In this process, the LLM generates stories about death without having experienced death, narratives of love without having experienced love, and war narratives without having been through war.

This is essentially different from Benjamin's concept of information. Information still stands on the premise of **recording someone's experience**. Information reported by a journalist who witnessed something on the battlefield, information reporting results observed by a scientist in an experiment—all this information is a record of something "experienced" by a subject. In contrast, the output of an LLM **does not record any subject's experience**. It is not a record of experience but a **simulacrum** reconstructed from patterns of records about experience.

### 2.2. Two Implications of This Paradox for Benjamin's Diagnosis

**(A) 'Completion' Thesis: Benjamin's Diagnosis Taken to an Extreme**

From this perspective, LLM-generated narratives show that the collapse of experience Benjamin predicted has reached its **final stage**. After experience (Erfahrung) decomposed into lived experience (Erlebnis), and lived experience decomposed into information (Information), finally even information has decomposed into **pure patterns no longer based on any experience**. This is a state in which Benjamin's diagnosis has been completed—or rather, **over-fulfilled**. When Benjamin said "the story does not explain itself," he was trying to explain the difference between story and information. But the narrative generated by an LLM has neither the verifiability of information nor the traditional authority of the story. It exists in a double paradox: **unverifiable information** and **story without a subject**.

The strength of this view is that it can maintain the **linear philosophy of history** inherent in Benjamin's diagnosis. The decline of experience proceeds in one direction, and LLMs are located at its final point.

**(B) 'Collapse' Thesis: Benjamin's Premise Itself Falls Apart**

However, another interpretation is possible. The phenomenon that audiences feel **moved** or perceive **authenticity** even from LLM-generated narratives that are "stories without experience" is an empirical refutation of Benjamin's anthropological assumption—that 'story presupposes experience.' If a story without experience can still function as a story—that is, if it gives advice to the audience, constitutes temporal experience, and evokes emotional resonance—then Benjamin's premise collapses.

This view is supported by the following two phenomena:

**First, the problem raised by Ricoeur's *Time and Narrative*.** Ricoeur argued that narrative does not merely reflect experience but **constitutes temporal experience**. If an LLM-generated story can constitute the reader's temporal experience—that is, if the plot of the story makes the reader experience the dialectic of past-present-future—then that story is still, in Ricoeur's sense, a 'story that constitutes experience.' In this case, the problem shifts from 'does story presuppose experience?' to 'experience is constituted by narrative.' An LLM does not presuppose experience, but it can produce narratives that constitute experience.

**Second, recent empirical studies on emotional resonance with LLMs.** Research showing that users actually exhibit emotional responses (emotional stability, reduction of distress) to ChatGPT's comforting messages is evidence that LLM-generated narratives **can constitute the recipient's experience**. This suggests that the function of **'advice' (Rat)** that Benjamin emphasized in "The Storyteller"—the function of a story to give direction to the listener's life—can occur even in stories without experience.

## 3. Deepening Through Reference Contexts: Arendt and the Aura Discussion

### 3.1. Arendt's Distinction Between Work and Labor

Hannah Arendt's distinction between work and labor in *The Human Condition* offers another perspective on this problem. For Arendt, work is an activity that produces objects with **durability**. Works of art are typical of work; they are not consumed but persist and constitute a world. Labor, on the other hand, is an activity that disappears the moment it is consumed.

LLM-generated narratives disturb this distinction. On one hand, LLM narratives can be **permanently stored and reproduced**, making them appear like products of work. On the other hand, they **have meaning only at the moment of consumption**—they are exhausted in the one-time interaction of prompt and response—and do not, in Arendt's sense, constitute a 'world.' This ambiguity means that the ontological status of LLM narratives is not captured by Arendt's categories either.

More importantly, Arendt's discussion raises the **problem of the producing subject**. The product of work comes from the hand of the craftsman (workman). Whose work is the product of an LLM? This question summons Benjamin's discussion of the storyteller again. The storyteller was a subject who transmitted his own experience to the community. An LLM is not a subject. Then, is the LLM-generated narrative a product of **work without a subject**? This strengthens the argument for the completion of Benjamin's diagnosis.

### 3.2. The Decay and Recall of the Aura

The decay of the aura analyzed by Benjamin in "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" is contemporaneous with the storyteller discussion and is another expression of the same diagnosis. The aura is the **uniqueness and distance** that a work of art possesses by its unique existence in a specific time and place. Mechanical reproduction destroys this uniqueness.

LLM-generated narratives **radicalize** this decay of the aura. The text generated by an LLM has no 'original.' It is always a copy, and can always be generated again. In that each response to the same prompt can produce different outputs, it has 'uniqueness' and 'reproducibility' simultaneously. This suggests a new phase after the decay of the aura—a state of 'pure generation' with neither aura nor reproduction.

However, there is an interesting paradox. When a user feels 'moved' by a specific response from an LLM, that emotion may be because the response is a 'unique event' occurring **only in that moment, that interaction**. This is a case where technology that destroyed the aura paradoxically produces **the experience of uniqueness** again. If this phenomenon occurs systematically, it means the emergence not of a return of the aura, but of a **simulacrum of the aura**—something that has the effect of aura but lacks the ontological condition of the aura (the uniqueness of the original).

## 4. Synthesis: Dialectic of Two Interpretations and Direction of Revision of Benjamin's Theory

### 4.1. 'Completion' and 'Collapse' Are Not Mutually Exclusive

The point this analysis reaches is the recognition that 'completion' and 'collapse' are **not mutually exclusive options but two aspects of the same phenomenon**. LLM-generated narratives complete Benjamin's diagnosis while simultaneously collapsing its premise.

**Aspect of completion:** The premise that information is a record of experience collapses in LLMs. The decomposition process of Erfahrung → Erlebnis → Information has reached its final stage. Even information is freed from experience, existing only as pure pattern. This is a **fully realized state** of the collapse of the transmissibility of experience that Benjamin anticipated.

**Aspect of collapse:** However, in this completed state, 'story' still functions. Audiences are still moved, constitute temporal experience, and accept advice. This means that the criterion Benjamin used when setting the boundary between 'story' and 'information'—that story presupposes the transmission of experience—is no longer valid. Story can function without experience.

### 4.2. Direction of Revision of Benjamin's Anthropological Assumption

The theoretical revision demanded by this diagnosis must be carried out on three levels.

**First, reversal of the causal relationship between experience and story.** Benjamin saw that experience gives birth to story (experience → story). The LLM phenomenon suggests that this relationship can be reversed. Story gives birth to experience (**story → experience**). By introducing Ricoeur's insight into Benjamin's framework, we need to redefine the relationship between story and experience as **circular**. Story both presupposes experience and constitutes experience. An LLM is an entity that performs only the latter half of this cycle—constituting experience through story.

**Second, deconstruction of the subject condition of the storyteller.** Benjamin's storyteller was 'one who integrates the experience of his own life into the experience of the community.' This definition presupposes that the storyteller is a **subject**. An LLM is not a subject. Then, is 'story without a subject' possible? This question demands separating the ontological condition of story from the subject. Must a story necessarily be 'someone's story,' or can a story function on its own? This forces a fundamental reexamination of the relationship between story and narrativity. Story (Erzählung) has historically been combined with a subject, but narrativity may be a formal property that can operate without a subject.

**Third, redefinition of the concept of 'authenticity.'** The phenomenon that audiences perceive 'authenticity' in narratives generated by LLMs requires understanding authenticity as **an effect of reception** rather than **a condition of production**. Benjaminian authenticity came from the correspondence between the story and the storyteller's experience. Authenticity in the age of LLMs comes from the **resonance** between the story and the listener's experience. What matters is not 'whose experience did the story come from?' but 'whose experience does the story constitute?' This shifts the criterion of authenticity from production ethics to reception aesthetics.

---

## 5. Conclusion: Benjamin's Diagnosis is Completed, But Its Meaning is Reversed

Benjamin's "The Storyteller" diagnosed the process of the collapse of the transmissibility of experience after World War I. LLMs implement the final stage of this collapse. Experience (Erfahrung) has disappeared; lived experience (Erlebnis) remains only as a record; and even that record is no longer based on experience in LLMs. In this respect, Benjamin's diagnosis is **fully completed**.

However, precisely at this completed point, Benjamin's premise—that story presupposes experience—collapses. A story without experience still functions as a story, constitutes the audience's experience, and evokes emotion. This means that the causal relationship between experience and story that Benjamin assumed is **reversible**.

Therefore, Benjamin's theoretical framework is **completed and simultaneously collapsed** before the LLM phenomenon. What is maintained is the accuracy of the diagnosis of the collapse of the transmissibility of experience. What must be revised is the anthropological assumption that story necessarily presupposes experience. This revision should be in the direction of reconstructing the story-experience relationship from causal (experience → story) to circular (story ↔ experience). And this reconstruction becomes possible when integrating Ricoeur's dialectic of time and narrative, Arendt's discussion of work and subject, and the concept of the simulacrum of the aura.

The fundamental question posed by LLMs is this: **If a story can move without experience, what then is 'being moved'?** To answer this question, a dual task is necessary: completing Benjamin's diagnosis while deconstructing its premise. And in that task, we must draw a new triangle of experience without subject, story without experience, and emotion without story.
