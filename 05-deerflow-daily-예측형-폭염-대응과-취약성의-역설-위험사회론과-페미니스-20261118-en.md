## Question

Suppose that, during a heat wave, a local government combines smartphone location data, household electricity consumption, and weather forecasts to identify households vulnerable to heat and automatically determine the locations and operating hours of mobile cooling shelters. This system transforms heat risk from a matter of post hoc assistance into one of real-time prediction and proactive allocation. Yet migrant workers, unhoused people, and irregular residents who refuse location tracking or have no electricity-consumption data may instead be erased from the calculation of risk. Drawing together Ulrich Beck’s theory of the risk society and feminist critiques of technology, analyze whether the digital efficiency of disaster response signifies the increased visibility of vulnerability or produces a new form of invisibility. Focusing especially on the moment when residents’ detours, refusals, and mutual care modify the allocation after the predictive model has deployed cooling resources, argue whether ecological publicness can be designed not as an average risk score but as a care procedure that includes needs not captured by datafication.

## Analytical Results

Before proceeding with the analysis, I will first organize the theoretical coordinates of eco-digital publicness and then analyze each layer of the question in sequence.

## Analysis: The Digital Efficiency of Disaster Response and the (In)visibility of Vulnerability

### 1. The Structure of the Problem: Whom Does Predictive Allocation Make into an “Existing Subject”?

The core of the question is not simply a critique of technology. The fact that a heat-wave response system “transforms heat risk from a matter of post hoc assistance into one of real-time prediction and proactive allocation” means that **the temporality of risk is being fundamentally reorganized**. As Ulrich Beck’s theory of the risk society warns, risk no longer exists merely as an event that can be experienced; it exists as a **calculable probability**. Yet in this system, the condition for “calculability” is data existence. Only those who provide location information and whose electricity consumption is recorded can be classified as “households vulnerable to heat.”

A decisive problem arises here. **Migrant workers, unhoused people, and irregular residents have little or no data existence.** Ontologically, they are erased from the system’s “risk calculation.” This is a different form of inequality from what Beck describes as the “boomerang effect of risk”—the idea that those who produce risk eventually become its targets. Rather, it is a structure in which **data poverty leads to the invisibility of risk**. The more efficiently the system identifies vulnerable households, the further datafied vulnerability is pushed into the shadows.

This also connects to the problem raised by the paradigm of techne-generation. **When technology functions not merely as a tool but as an environment that constitutes the conditions of existence**—as the system in this question reconstructs the environmental risk of extreme heat as an object that can be predicted and allocated—some subjects come to exist in ways that cannot be captured by that environment’s computational grid.

### 2. Feminist Critiques of Technology: The Illusion of “Average Vulnerability”

Feminist critiques of technology emphasize, in particular, **that vulnerability is not distributed uniformly**. The “risk score” used by this system inherently contains the violence of averaging. Household-level electricity consumption cannot reflect the individual needs of older people, infants, or people with chronic illnesses who may be more vulnerable to heat even within the same household. More fundamentally, **the category of the “household” itself is designed in a way that cannot capture the temporary shelters of migrant workers or the living spaces of unhoused people.**

The implications of Source 1, “The Transparency of Friction and the Transparency of the Subject,” are important here. This system is designed to minimize friction: data collection is automated, the predictive model calculates the need for intervention in advance, and cooling shelters are deployed automatically. Yet the greater the transparency of friction becomes—that is, the more smoothly the system operates—**the lower the transparency of the subject becomes**. In other words, the smooth operation of the system makes it impossible to see why subjects outside the data grid have been omitted from the risk calculation.

This is not simply a problem of “data bias.” **When the system operates smoothly, that smoothness itself conceals the evidence of exclusion.** It is the paradox in which success, rather than failure, produces the invisibility of vulnerability.

### 3. The Core of Ecological Publicness: The Political Moment of “Post-Allocation Modification”

The point most sharply identified by the question is **the moment when residents’ detours, refusals, and mutual care modify the allocation after the predictive model has deployed cooling resources**. This moment has two theoretical implications.

**First, it is the membrane where the “technical environment” and the “lifeworld” come into contact.** The system’s predictive model presupposes an average, statistical human, whereas actual residents possess concrete needs that are not captured by the data—for example, the schedules of care workers who can visit a shelter only at certain times, the physical-access problems faced by people with mobility impairments, or cultural aversions to particular locations. Residents’ detours constitute **a friction that reveals the blind spots of the data model**.

**Second, mutual care can disable the system’s logic of efficiency.** For example, residents of an apartment complex might jointly operate a shelter space and reorganize it into a play area for children or a resting area for older people—uses that the system did not predict. This is **the moment when the procedure of care replaces the efficiency of resource allocation**.

The concept of “sensorial surplus” mentioned in Source 5, “Principles of Digital Ecology,” can be interpreted differently here. The data produced by a system always leaves something behind: sensations that were not collected, relationships that were not measured, and care that was not quantified. This “surplus” is not a defect in the system but **the condition under which ecological publicness can operate**.

### 4. Redesigning Ecological Publicness: A Care Procedure Rather Than an Average Risk Score

The question ultimately asks **whether ecological publicness can be designed as a care procedure that includes needs not captured by datafication, rather than as an average risk score**. The key lies in the meaning of the verb **“to include.”**

Data-driven systems inherently determine in advance what can and cannot be included. To say that “needs not captured by datafication should be included” is not simply a matter of expanding the system’s boundaries. The illusion that unhoused people can be captured if more data are collected **overlooks the violence exercised by capture itself**.

An alternative design should be based on the following principles:

**(1) Recognition of incompleteness — The fact that the system cannot capture every form of vulnerability should serve as the starting point for its design.** In other words, the system’s predictions must always be treated as “provisional hypotheses,” and the possibility of on-the-ground modification must remain open.

**(2) The right to refuse — Institutional mechanisms must ensure that the right to refuse data provision does not lead to exclusion from risk calculations.** This means more than a simple opt-out. It means creating channels through which the system can recognize the existence of those who refuse and support them through alternative means—for example, manual reporting through community networks.

**(3) Infrastructure for mutual care — The system must be structured to process residents’ detours and modifications as “feedback,” rather than as “bugs.”** In other words, there should be a circular structure in which the history of residents redefining the locations and hours of shelters is accumulated as data and incorporated into the training of subsequent predictive models. This is also connected to the transition from techne-edit to techne-generation.

Sources 2 and 3 present “responsiveness” as a core condition of the age of techne-generation. A system should not merely be a tool that executes commands; it must become **an entity capable of responding to changes in the environment and to the needs of subjects**. For the system described in the question to realize ecological publicness, this condition of responsiveness must be satisfied.

### 5. Conclusion: The Relationship Between Digital Efficiency and Vulnerability

The digital efficiency of disaster response simultaneously performs two functions: it makes vulnerability visible and produces new invisibilities. Vulnerabilities for which data exist can be captured more precisely than before, while vulnerabilities for which no data exist are placed in an even deeper state of invisibility. This may be called the **“boomerang effect of digital efficiency”**: as efficiency increases, the areas beyond its reach remain as more clearly defined blind spots.

Designing ecological publicness as a procedure of care is possible not by eliminating this boomerang, but **by acknowledging its existence and managing it**. In other words, there is no single optimal solution that can pursue efficiency and equity simultaneously. What is needed instead is a **multi-path design** that transparently discloses the system’s incompleteness, institutionalizes residents’ authority to modify it, and maintains non-data-based pathways for beings and needs that have not been datafied.

Ultimately, this leads to the core proposition of the eco-digital framework. **The condition under which a digital environment can possess ecological publicness depends on whether that environment recognizes the limits of its own calculations and maintains points of friction through which it can come into contact with beings beyond those limits.** The media aesthetics of the age of techne-generation requires us to reevaluate these points of friction not as defects, but as indicators of ecological health.
