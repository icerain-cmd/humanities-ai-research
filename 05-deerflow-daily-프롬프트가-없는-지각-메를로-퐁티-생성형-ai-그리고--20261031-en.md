## Question

Can Maurice Merleau-Ponty's concept of embodied perception explain the phenomenon of generative AI mediating language and vision? — What challenge does the absence of bodily sensation between prompt and output pose to perception theory?

## Key Findings

# Merleau-Ponty's Embodied Perception and Generative AI: The Structure of Explainability and Challenge

### Introduction: Decomposing the Layers of the Question

This question actually contains three distinct layers: (1) Can Merleau-Ponty's concepts explain **generative AI's mediation of language and vision**, (2) what is the **absence of bodily sensation** between prompt and output, and (3) what **challenge does this absence pose to perception theory itself**. These three layers demand different answers. In brief: **it partially explains, but fails at the primordial level, and precisely this failure becomes the challenge demanding an expansion of perception theory** — this is the gist of my response.

---

### 1. What Can Be Explained: Prompting as "Speaking Speech"

Merleau-Ponty's theory of language is exceptionally well-suited to explaining generative AI's linguistic mediation. In *Phenomenology of Perception*, he distinguishes two layers of language: **speaking speech (parole parlante, the creative use of language)** and **spoken speech (parole parlée, ready-made linguistic and cultural sediment)**. The former is "language at the moment of the advent of meaning, when thought emerges," while the latter is "secondary expression dependent on the speaker's linguistic patrimony and cultural heritage" (to borrow the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's formulation).

This distinction structurally aligns with the phenomenological description of the prompting experience. The act of designing a prompt is not a recombination of ready-made tokens, but a gesture toward meaning not yet formed. The user does not yet know, or knows only incompletely, what meaning they desire; the prompt is part of an intentional arc toward that indeterminate intention. The moment one reads the output, one *finally* discovers what one wanted. This is precisely the same structure as Merleau-Ponty's paradox of expression — "expression creates the thought that makes it possible."

Equally important is that prompting is **an extension of an embodied way of being-in-the-world entirely mediated through language**. Recent research supports this. The thesis of *Algorithms, language, and poetry* (AI and Ethics, 2025) refuses to see "computationally modeled language as a neutral medium of information transmission," instead viewing it as "a historical crystallization of a more primordial field of embodied expression." This is an AI application of Merleau-Ponty's thesis in *The Prose of the World* that "language is one of the fundamental ways we inhabit the world." At this level, generative AI mediation as linguistic interaction falls **within** the scope of embodied perception theory.

---

### 2. What Cannot Be Explained: The Rupture with the Primordial Structure of Perception

However, when Merleau-Ponty's perception theory moves beyond language to address perception itself, the "absence of bodily sensation" in generative AI becomes an insurmountable barrier. This is concretized at four points.

**First, the absence of the intentional arc.** For Merleau-Ponty, perception is a sensorimotor achievement operating on the intentional arc. Visual perception is the result of a circular entanglement of eye movement, head orientation, vestibular sensation, and tactile anticipation. It is not the "brain" that perceives, but the **entire body**, and the body is "my means of being-in-the-world." LLMs possess no such sensorimotor loop whatsoever — no physical sensors, no actuators that transform the world, and no sensory changes arising through this loop. As *Transforming agency* (Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences, 2025) precisely notes, this system "cannot autonomously recognize failure" and lacks the conditions of normativity and individuality. Recognition is linked to embodied emotional states such as regret or surprise, and no such internal states exist for LLMs.

**Second, the absence of pre-reflective self-awareness.** Merleau-Ponty's perception rests on the implicit self-awareness of the perceiving body, which does not involve "perceiving that one is perceiving." This "pre-reflective" dimension is entirely different from any post-hoc self-check. As *AI Meets Philosophy* Vol.6 argues, the essential difference between human perception and LLM "perception" is precisely the presence or absence of this pre-reflective self-awareness, and this gap may be in principle unbridgeable. An LLM's "identification" of its own errors in response to instructions is a retrospective process, not the self-feeling of an embodied lifeworld.

**Third, the rupture of the flesh (chair) and chiasm.** In the later Merleau-Ponty, perception rests on the intertwining (entrelacs) of world and body touching and being touched — a chiasmic structure. *Technological Intentionality and the Concept of Flesh* (Philosophy & Technology, 2026) applies this concept to generative AI, proposing to reinterpret technology's autonomous activity as an "intrinsically connected" flesh with the human pole. However, this argument establishes technology as a "pole" without claiming that technology *perceives*. Flesh is the ontological condition of felt corporeality (the experiencing body), not the condition of information processing. Generative AI does not exist within the *feeling* of the world; it exists only computationally within the statistical structure of text corpora.

**Fourth, the problem of negative capability.** For Merleau-Ponty, the body's vulnerability (vulnérabilité) and finitude (finitude) are conditions of perception. Because the body can ache, tire, age, and fail, it has a specific horizon toward the world. Human sequential reading — the inability to process everything simultaneously — is both a limitation and the condition of meaning's sedimentation. An LLM's global, one-shot processing of text is a mode of being devoid of such finitude, and we may even ask whether its processing results can be called "perception."

---

### 3. Is "Absence" a Challenge or an Opportunity for Reconfiguration?

Here the third layer of the question arrives — the challenge to perception theory. The core argument is this: **The absence of bodily sensation is not a counterexample to perception theory, but a device that reveals its boundary conditions.** Absence paradoxically illuminates what the minimal requirements of perception are.

Yet a more interesting reconfiguration is available. Borrowing from the concept proposed by *Transforming agency*, the LLM as a "library-that-talks" lacks sensorimotor embodiment but possesses three unique forms of embodiment: **textual embodiment**, **digital extended interface embodiment**, and **resource-intensive computational embodiment**. The human–LLM combination produces neither traditional instrumental extension nor full intentional agency, but **midtended agency**. This concept allows us to see the user–prompt–output cycle as a distributed perceptual system. Like a cane (an extended body schema), the AI interface is incorporated into the user's perceptual circuit — with the difference that instead of "transmitting" the world's texture as a cane does, it **transforms and transmits the statistical structure of the world through linguistic surfaces**.

An important theoretical precision is needed here. The difference between a tool (a cane) and generative AI is that the former operates *within* the sensorimotor circuit, while the latter generates linguistic output *outside* the circuit to intervene in the user's sensorimotor circuit. Thus AI cannot be reduced to the phenomenological tool theory of HCI since Dourish (tool transparency, backgrounding). AI is an **anomalous tool that does not recede into the background**, but rather a medium that reorganizes the user's perceptual background itself.

---

### 4. The Final Form of the Challenge to Perception Theory

Synthesizing, the challenge can be stated in three forms:

1. **Ontological challenge**: Does perception necessarily presuppose flesh? If so, the concept of LLM "perception" is a category mistake, and the problem is not perception theory but metaphorical language use. If it is contingent, a new category of "disembodied perception" is needed. — The latter is the more productive direction.

2. **Mediological challenge**: Unlike the media perception theory has hitherto addressed (tools, art, language), generative AI is a **medium that creates output**. This deconstructs the perception–expression distinction itself. For Merleau-Ponty, perception and expression were circular; AI media synthesizes expression without perception. The question that "expression without anything to express" poses to perception theory is novel.

3. **Normative challenge**: The absence of embodied perception leads to the absence of responsibility. Because embodied perception is responsiveness to the world. (This point connects directly with the asymmetric responsibility structure addressed in the professor's AI-environment paper — how an environment lacking agency becomes the object of responsibility.)

### Conclusion

Merleau-Ponty's concept of embodied perception can explain generative AI's linguistic mediation **at the level of expression theory**. The three axes of prompting as "speaking speech," the expression-discovery circuit, and the embodied origin of language are valid tools for describing the phenomenology of generative AI interaction. However, **it cannot explain at the primordial level of perception (intentional arc, sensorimotor circuit, pre-reflective self-awareness, flesh)**, and precisely this failure reveals the boundaries of perception theory. The "absence of bodily sensation" is not a refutation of perception theory, but an event that historically makes visible what the minimal conditions of perception are. Ultimately, the encounter with generative AI is most productively read as a challenge demanding the expansion of perception theory from "phenomenology of the human body" to "phenomenology of distributed, medially mediated, disembodied perceptual systems" — and at that point of expansion, the professor's ongoing ontological turn toward seeing AI as environment rather than object can make an important theoretical contribution.
