## Question

Suppose that a wildfire-monitoring platform combines satellite thermal imagery, drone footage, and weather forecasts to present ignition risks and evacuation routes in real time. When this system turns the forest into a set of sensor-detectable risk indicators and predictive probabilities, are the topographical sensibilities of on-site firefighters and residents’ seasonal memories merely supplementary calibration data, or are they perceptual practices that jointly constitute a hazardous environment? In particular, analyze the moments when smoke undetected by the model and shifts in wind that depart from the forecast reveal failures of response, and argue that knowledge of the environment is established not through the accurate representation of data but through the repeated cycle of sensing, interpretation, contestation, and revision.

## Analysis Results

The question can be read as an attempt to test, through the concrete case of wildfire monitoring, the central ontological claim of an ongoing paper in AI environmental theory, rather than as a matter of purely academic curiosity. The argument is developed below.

## Knowledge of the Environment Is Not Representation but an Iterative Process of Sensing, Contestation, and Revision: The Ontology of a Wildfire-Monitoring Platform

### 1. Framing the Problem: What Does the Platform Make the Forest Into?

A wildfire-monitoring platform that combines satellite thermal imagery, drone footage, and weather forecasts transforms the forest into a **set of computationally tractable risk indicators**. NDVI (the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index), soil moisture, predicted wind direction and speed, and temperature thresholds for detecting heat sources—all of these compress the forest’s “probability of ignition” into probabilistic values. At this point, the platform is not merely a tool but an ontological apparatus that summons the forest into a **specific mode of existence**. The forest is no longer a “place one walks through, smells, and remembers,” but rather a **field of signals that sensors can capture and patterns that algorithms can process**.

Yet this is where the problem begins. What ontological status do the topographical sensibilities of on-site firefighters—“This valley is where the flames suddenly leap when the summer southerly winds blow”—and residents’ seasonal memories—“Around this time last autumn, the humidity suddenly dropped”—have in relation to the platform’s outputs?

### 2. Two Positions: Supplementary Data or Perceptual Practice?

**Position A (the representationalist position):** The knowledge of firefighters and residents is corrective data that compensates for the “incompleteness” of the data. The platform’s predictions constitute primary knowledge, while field experience is a secondary corrective input that makes them “more accurate.” From this perspective, truth is measured by how closely one approaches “reality” when the platform’s knowledge and human knowledge are added together.

**Position B (the constructivist position):** The perceptual practices of firefighters and residents jointly constitute a hazardous environment **with** the platform. Here, “knowledge” is established not through the accurate representation of data but through a recurring cycle of sensing, interpretation, contestation, and revision. Smoke missed by the platform and shifts in wind that depart from the forecast—the “moments of failure”—are privileged sites that reveal knowledge to be a process of **co-constitution**, rather than a problem of representation.

This argument defends Position B.

### 3. Theoretical Resources: Technological Editing and the Transparency of Friction

Here, the conceptual cluster associated with Lee Yong-wook, identified through the Khoj search results, provides an important theoretical resource.

According to the concept of **technological editing**, digital technologies are not merely tools; they create media conditions in which the capacity to select, arrange, and reconfigure is central [Reference 4]. A wildfire-monitoring platform likewise functions as an editing apparatus that determines **what to capture through sensors** among the forest’s innumerable properties (capturing temperature but not the smell of soil), **what to arrange as risk indicators** (including humidity and wind speed but excluding bird-migration patterns), and **how to output the results** (visualizing them as a probability map). This editing is never neutral. The conditions of editing make only certain layers of the forest visible while excluding others.

At this point, the concept of **the transparency of friction** becomes important [Reference 5]. The transparency of friction refers to the condition in which the errors, unpredictable operations, and algorithmic limitations of a technological system shift from being “invisible” to becoming “visible.” When a wildfire-monitoring platform is operating “normally,” the conditions of its editing become transparent: users do not consciously attend to what the platform sees and what it does not see. However, when **smoke undetected by the model** is perceived by an on-site firefighter’s eyes, or when **a shift in wind that departs from the forecast** corresponds to residents’ seasonal memories, friction becomes transparent. At that moment, the platform’s conditions of editing are revealed, and knowledge becomes an object of interpretation and contestation rather than a simple data output.

### 4. Moments of Failure: The Cycle of Sensing, Contestation, and Revision

Let us make this concrete through examples.

**Case 1: Smoke undetected by the model.** Satellite thermal imagery classifies only heat sources above a certain temperature as “ignition.” Under unusual conditions—for example, when damp moss burns slowly and fails to reach the temperature threshold—smoke may be produced without being detected by the platform. At this moment, when an on-site firefighter reports, “There’s a strange smell,” the act is not simply the addition of information absent from the data as a form of “calibration.” It is a **perceptual practice that challenges the very category of “risk” defined by the platform**. The question, “There’s smoke here, so why didn’t the alarm go off?” is not merely a report of an error; it demands an ontological redefinition of “what counts as dangerous.”

**Case 2: A shift in wind that departs from the forecast.** Suppose the weather model predicts “northeasterly winds at 3 m/s at 2 p.m.,” while a local resident says, “In this valley, the valley wind always shifts to the southwest in the afternoon.” This constitutes another ontological collision. The weather model projects atmospheric equations calculated at a global scale onto the local area, whereas the “valley wind” is embodied knowledge acquired through decades of experience with a particular terrain. This knowledge does more than judge the model’s forecast to be “wrong”; it **questions the very conditions of the forecast**: “Does this model adequately represent the microclimate of this area?”

The key point to grasp in both cases is this: a moment of failure is not simply a “malfunction” of the platform. It reveals an **ontological discrepancy concerning how knowledge of the environment is constituted**. The gap between the platform’s world—the forest captured by satellites, drones, and models—and the world of field experience—embodied sensations, seasonal rhythms, and topographical memory—becomes visible for the first time.

This gap matters. It demonstrates that knowledge is **not simply a matter of a single representation**. Rather, knowledge emerges through the following cycle:

1. **Sensing:** The platform detects a heat source, or a firefighter detects the smell of smoke. Each belongs to a different “sensory regime.”
2. **Interpretation:** The platform’s detection is already algorithmically interpreted and output as “Alert Level 2.” A firefighter’s perception is interpreted through embodied experience as the judgment, “We need to investigate.”
3. **Contestation:** Contestation arises when these two interpretations conflict. This is not simply a determination of whether “the model is wrong” or “your senses are wrong,” but a practical choice concerning **which sensory regime should be trusted**.
4. **Revision:** As a result of the contestation, the platform’s threshold may be adjusted, on-site firefighters may receive new training, or nothing may change, leaving the system to await the next failure.

What matters is that this cycle **never ends**. Knowledge of the environment is only provisionally stabilized through the repetition of this cycle; it is never completed in an absolute sense.

### 5. Knowledge Structuring and Co-Oscillating Subjects in the Technogenetic Era

If we expand this discussion into a broader framework, Lee Yong-wook’s concept of the **technogenetic era** comes into view [Reference 2]. Knowledge structuring in the technogenetic era understands knowledge as “a distributed order that includes not only a list of individual propositions but also similarities among concepts, criteria for differentiation, the peripheries of incorrect answers, and contexts of use.” In the case of a wildfire-monitoring platform, knowledge of “risk” does not reside only in the platform’s output value—78 percent probability. It is **distributed** across the embodied judgments of on-site firefighters, residents’ seasonal memories, the platform’s algorithmic classification system, and the practical contexts in which all of these elements collide and are coordinated.

In this process, humans are not merely data providers or recipients of outputs. They function as **co-oscillating subjects** who critically interpret and redistribute the knowledge order constituted by the platform [Reference 3; the concept of “co-oscillating subject” in Reference 2]. When firefighters disregard the platform’s alert and follow their own senses, or when residents’ memories lead to modifications of the platform’s predictive model, humans and technology enter into a relationship of **collaborative generation**, in which knowledge is produced together rather than through a merely instrumental relation.

### 6. Conclusion: From Representation to Environmental Constitution

When a wildfire-monitoring platform transforms the forest into a set of risk indicators, the forest comes to exist in two ways. One is the **calculated forest** captured by the platform; the other is the **embodied forest** apprehended through field experience. It is a mistake to ask which of these is “more real.” What matters is that these two forests **jointly** constitute a hazardous environment, and that this process of constitution unfolds through an unceasing cycle of sensing, interpretation, contestation, and revision.

Smoke undetected by the model and shifts in wind that depart from the forecast are **privileged moments** in this cycle. They expose the fact that knowledge is not a matter of representational accuracy but a practical decision about **which ontological order to adopt**. Knowledge of the environment is not the degree to which data “correctly” reflects reality; it is the **process** through which different sensory and interpretive systems coexist, collide, and are coordinated.

This also connects to the central claim of the ongoing paper in AI environmental theory: AI should be understood not as an object but as an environment. The way a wildfire-monitoring platform makes the forest into an “environment” is structurally identical to the way AI makes the conditions of our perception into an “environment.” Both make the world exist in particular ways through infrastructures of sensing, classification, and prediction. In this process, humans become beings who resonate within that environment, contest it, and recalibrate it.

### Stated Limitations

The Khoj search results provided Lee Yong-wook’s conceptual framework—technological editing, technogenesis, co-oscillating subjects, and the transparency of friction—but did not identify literature applying these concepts specifically to wildfire monitoring or disaster management. The examples involving firefighters and residents in the argument above are hypothetical scenarios constructed by the author (counterfactual scenarios). To strengthen the empirical validity of the argument, the following additional materials would be necessary: (a) ethnographic studies of actual wildfire-monitoring systems in the field, (b) research on situational awareness in disaster management, and (c) literature from the field of computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW). Moreover, given that Lee Yong-wook’s concepts originated primarily in the study of digital media art and games, theoretical caution is required when applying them to the domain of emergency and disaster management.
