# The Paradoxical Aura of Generative AI Images: From Mechanical Reproduction to Statistical Generation

## 1. Introduction: Formulation of the Problem

The concept of aura presented by Walter Benjamin in his 1935 work *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* is one of the most influential concepts in 20th-century art theory and media theory. For Benjamin, aura refers to the 'unique presence' of an artwork, stemming from its authenticity and authority, uniquely situated in a specific time and space. Technical reproduction media such as photography and film dismantle this aura, transforming art from ritual value to exhibition value.

However, images produced by generative AI show a paradoxical reversal of this. Although every output is statistically unique, the recipient experiences it as 'already seen,' 'feeling reproduced.' In other words, it is not mechanical reproducibility (technical reproduction) but statistical originality (technical generation) that produces the same phenomenological effect as the withering of the aura. This paradox suggests new technical conditions that are difficult to explain solely within Benjamin's framework.

This paper develops the discussion on three levels to analyze this paradox. First, it reexamines Benjamin's concept of aura and clarifies the technical conditions it presupposes. Second, it analyzes how the statistical generation mechanism of generative AI differs from mechanical reproduction through Gilbert Simondon's concept of concretisation. Third, it examines how the synthetic data feedback loop and model collapse phenomenon enable two interpretations of this paradox: as the evolution of a technical object and as a regressive closure loop. Finally, it summarizes the implications of this issue for eco-digital/artificial nature research.

## 2. Benjamin's Concept of Aura and Its Technical Conditions

### 2.1 Phenomenological Structure of Aura

For Benjamin, aura is a complex concept beyond mere 'originality' or 'authenticity.' He defines aura as "a unique phenomenon of a distance, however close it may be." This definition contains three core elements. First, the 'sense of distance' signifies that the artwork possesses authority and cult value derived from its ritual context. Second, the condition 'however close it may be' refers to a metaphysical distance that is maintained regardless of physical accessibility. Third, the 'unique phenomenon' implies the work's inherent temporality and spatiality.

Benjamin saw this aura as being dismantled by technical reproduction media such as photography and film. Technical reproduction renders the distinction between original and copy meaningless, creating conditions where copies can circulate without an original. In this process, the 'cult value (Kultwert)' of the artwork is replaced by 'exhibition value (Ausstellungswert).' Importantly, Benjamin viewed this transformation not simply as a loss but as an opportunity to open up the politicization of art.

### 2.2 Technical Conditions of Mechanical Reproduction

The mechanical reproduction that Benjamin analyzed presupposes specific technical conditions. First, reproduction always presupposes an 'original.' A photograph is an optical reproduction of its subject, and a film is a reproduction of the filmed scene. Second, the product of reproduction maintains an 'indexical relationship' with the original. The photograph bears an indexical character, proving "that-has-been (ça a été)." Third, the reproduction process is mediated by 'mechanical causality.' The physical process of the camera's shutter opening and the film being exposed operates independently of the artist's intention.

Under these conditions, the withering of aura is understood as the process of dismantling the authority of the original. When a copy offers the same visual experience as the original, the 'here and now (Hier und Jetzt)' of the original no longer holds a privileged status. However, this dismantling paradoxically conceals the fact that the copy still 'depends' on the original. A photographic copy presupposes the original photograph; a film copy presupposes the original film. In other words, mechanical reproduction dismantles the hierarchy of original-copy but does not completely eliminate that hierarchy itself.

### 2.3 Extension to Digital Reproduction and Its Limits

Hito Steyerl's discussion of the 'poor image' is a representative example of extending Benjamin's framework to the digital environment. Steyerl argued that digital images that have undergone low resolution, compression, and re-uploading, as 'poor images,' create new modes of circulation and political possibilities. This is similar to the democratization potential of art that Benjamin saw in the expansion of exhibition value.

However, generative AI images fundamentally depart from this framework. AI images do not presuppose an 'original.' The images generated by DALL-E or Stable Diffusion are not reproductions of specific original photographs or paintings but are results sampled from the statistical distribution of training data. This signifies an entirely different technical condition: 'generation' rather than 'reproduction.' Moreover, AI images do not have an 'indexical relationship.' While a photograph proves "that-has-been," an AI image merely shows "that such a thing could exist."

This difference suggests that Benjamin's framework is limited to the specific technical condition of 'mechanical reproduction.' The 'statistical generation' of generative AI introduces new technical conditions that Benjamin did not presuppose, implying that the phenomenon of the withering of aura can occur through entirely different mechanisms.

## 3. Technical Conditions of Statistical Generation: Focusing on Simondon's Concept of Concretisation

### 3.1 Simondon's Concretisation and the Ontology of Technical Objects

In his work *Du mode d'existence des objets techniques* (1958), Gilbert Simondon analyzes technical objects not merely as tools or means but as entities with their own ontological status. For Simondon, technical objects evolve through a process called 'concretisation.' Concretisation is the process by which an initially abstract and segmented technical object acquires internal coherence and functional integration.

For example, in early internal combustion engines, each part functioned separately, but through evolution, the parts adjusted and integrated their functions, forming an organic system. In this process, the technical object experiences 'individuation,' moving beyond a mere collection of tools to acquire its own 'technical nature.'

Simondon's concept of concretisation provides important insights for understanding the technical conditions of generative AI. Generative AI is not merely a collection of algorithms but a concretised technical object in which training data, model architecture, learning processes, and inference processes are intricately intertwined. In particular, the 'statistical generation' of generative AI reveals a key characteristic of this concretisation process.

### 3.2 The Concretisation Process of Statistical Generation

The statistical generation of generative AI undergoes the following concretisation process. First, 'concretisation of data': Vast amounts of training data are 'concretised' into the model's weights and parameters, not merely as a collection of information. In this process, data loses its original context and meaning, being reduced to pure statistical patterns. Second, 'concretisation of architecture': Architectures such as Transformers or Diffusion Models are not mere computational structures but concretised structures that have evolved to capture the statistical characteristics of data. Third, 'concretisation of the generation process': When a trained model generates new images, this is not mere random sampling but 'concretised generation' from the learned statistical distribution.

The core of this concretisation process is 'statistical averageness.' What generative AI learns is not the unique characteristics of individual images but the statistical distribution of hundreds of millions of images. Therefore, the closer an image generated by the model is to the 'average' of this statistical distribution, the more it is evaluated as a 'plausible' image. This paradoxically explains why, despite every output being statistically unique, it feels 'already seen' to the recipient.

### 3.3 Ontological Difference Between Mechanical Reproduction and Statistical Generation

Viewed through Simondon's concept of concretisation, mechanical reproduction and statistical generation have fundamentally different ontological conditions. Mechanical reproduction presupposes a hierarchical relationship of 'original-copy.' The copy 'depends' on the original, and the ontological primacy of the original is maintained. In contrast, statistical generation presupposes a triadic relationship of 'data-model-output.' Here, there is no 'original'; the output is a result 'sampled' from the statistical distribution of data.

This difference leads to the following phenomenological differences. First, in mechanical reproduction, the withering of aura is experienced as the 'dismantling of the authority of the original.' The audience experiences the copy through the awareness that "this is not the original." Second, in statistical generation, the 'absence of aura' is experienced as the 'absence of an original.' The audience cannot answer the question "Where did this come from?" and cannot trace the origin of the image.

This difference shows the limitations of Benjamin's framework in explaining the paradox of statistical generation. Benjamin's withering of aura refers to a specific phenomenological effect—the 'dismantling of the authority of the original'—but the 'already seen' feeling of generative AI images stems from an entirely different phenomenological condition: the 'absence of an original.'

## 4. Synthetic Data Feedback Loop and Model Collapse

### 4.1 Structure of the Synthetic Data Feedback Loop

One of the most important phenomena caused by the statistical generation of generative AI is the 'synthetic data feedback loop.' This refers to a cyclical structure in which images generated by AI are used again as training data. Specifically, images generated by Stable Diffusion or DALL-E are circulated on the internet, and these images are then included in the training data for the next generation of models.

This feedback loop proceeds through the following stages. First, the initial model is trained on large-scale human-generated data. Second, the trained model generates new images. Third, these generated images are circulated on the internet without human verification. Fourth, the next generation model is trained on data that includes these circulated images. Fifth, this process repeats.

The core of this feedback loop is the 'amplification of statistical averageness.' Each generation of the model, by learning from the outputs of the previous generation, generates increasingly 'average' images. This is similar to the phenomenon where repeated use of a copier gradually degrades the sharpness of the original, but the underlying mechanism is fundamentally different.

### 4.2 Phenomenology of Model Collapse

Model collapse is the extreme result of the synthetic data feedback loop. This phenomenon exhibits the following characteristics. First, 'loss of diversity': The diversity of images generated by the model gradually decreases. A model that initially produced images with various styles and compositions converges over time to a specific 'average' style. Second, 'disappearance of specificity': Rare patterns or unusual cases present in the training data gradually disappear. The model generates only 'typical' images. Third, 'amplification of errors': Small errors or biases in the initial model are amplified through the feedback loop, generating increasingly distorted images.

This model collapse is the key mechanism explaining the 'already seen' feeling of generative AI images. The reason recipients experience AI images as 'feeling reproduced' is because these images are actually converging toward statistical averageness. In other words, 'statistical originality' is merely an illusion; in reality, 'statistical averageness' dominates.

### 4.3 Evolution of the Technical Object and Regressive Closure Loop

The synthetic data feedback loop and model collapse enable two contrasting interpretations of the evolution of the technical object.

First, the 'evolution of the technical object' interpretation: According to Simondon's concept of concretisation, technical objects evolve toward internal coherence and functional integration. From this perspective, the synthetic data feedback loop can be seen as a process where generative AI generates and learns its 'own data,' evolving into an 'autonomous technical system' that does not depend on human data. This is similar to 'genetic drift' in biological evolution, where genetic diversity decreases.

Second, the 'regressive closure loop' interpretation: On the other hand, model collapse shows the 'regressive closure' of the technical object. As the feedback loop repeats, the model becomes increasingly trapped in a narrow statistical space, losing the ability to generate new patterns or innovations. This is a process where the technical object does not evolve toward 'individuation' but regresses toward 'homogenization.'

These two interpretations suggest that the technical conditions of generative AI are inherently contradictory. On the one hand, generative AI evolves toward autonomous generation capability independent of human data, but on the other hand, this evolution results in the regressive outcome of convergence toward statistical averageness.

## 5. Comparison with Jean Baudrillard's Simulacra

### 5.1 The Three Orders of Simulacra

In his work *Simulacres et simulation* (1981), Jean Baudrillard presents three orders of simulacra. The first order is the 'imitation (mimésis)' of the classical era, an attempt to faithfully reproduce the original. The second order is 'production' of the industrial age, which dismantles the distinction between original and copy through mass reproduction of the original. The third order is 'simulation' of the postmodern age, where reality is constituted by models and codes that operate without an original.

Do generative AI images correspond to this third order of simulacra? On the surface, yes. AI images do not presuppose an 'original' and are generated by a 'model'—the statistical distribution of training data. This is similar to the condition of the 'hyperreal' that Baudrillard speaks of: images more real than reality.

### 5.2 Simulacra as Statistical Average

However, generative AI images show an important difference from Baudrillard's simulacra. Baudrillard's simulacra are characterized by 'excess' and 'exaggeration.' The hyperreal is an image that exceeds reality: sharper, more perfect, and more seductive. In contrast, generative AI images are simulacra as 'statistical average.' They are characterized not by excess but by 'leveling' and 'averaging.'

This difference produces the following phenomenological effects. Baudrillard's hyperreal captivates the recipient through 'fascination' and 'seduction.' In contrast, generative AI images numb the recipient through 'indifference' and 'boredom.' This suggests that the 'already seen' feeling is not merely a cognitive error but a phenomenological response to statistical averageness.

### 5.3 Two Modalities of Reproduction Without an Original

Both Baudrillard's simulacra and generative AI images share the commonality of being 'reproduction without an original.' However, the modality of this 'absence of the original' is fundamentally different. For Baudrillard, the absence of the original is an ontological event: the 'disappearance of reality.' Reality no longer exists; everything is constituted within the game of simulacra.

In contrast, in generative AI images, the absence of the original is a technical event: 'reduction to a statistical distribution.' The reason the original does not exist is not because reality has disappeared, but because individual images have been dissolved within a statistical distribution. This requires an analysis of technical conditions, distinct from Baudrillard's ontological claim.

## 6. Intersection with Katherine Hayles' Cognitive Frame Theory

### 6.1 Cognitive Frames and Pattern Recognition

Katherine Hayles develops the concept of the cognitive frame in her work *How We Became Posthuman* (1999) and subsequent writings. According to Hayles, human cognition constitutes the world through specific frames, and these frames are altered by the technical environment.

The 'already seen' feeling of generative AI images can be explained by Hayles' cognitive frame theory. Human visual cognition relies on pattern recognition. When we see a new image, we compare it with similar patterns in memory to construct meaning. Due to statistical averageness, generative AI images 'too well' match the patterns in human memory. Paradoxically, this blocks the experience of 'newness.'

### 6.2 Phenomenology of Statistical Familiarity

Extending Hayles' frame theory, the phenomenological effect of generative AI images can be explained as 'statistical familiarity.' This has the following characteristics.

First, 'excess of pattern': AI images 'over-satisfy' human pattern recognition mechanisms. All elements are arranged in a 'predictable' manner, so no cognitive tension or surprise occurs. Second, 'absence of deformation': Images created by humans, whether intentionally or unintentionally, contain 'deformation.' Such deformation attracts cognitive attention and generates new meaning. In contrast, due to statistical averageness, deformation in AI images is minimized. Third, 'illusion of memory': The statistical averageness of AI images causes the recipient to have the illusion that "I have seen this image before." This is not actual memory recall but the 'familiarity' of statistical patterns creating a memory illusion.

### 6.3 Technical Conditions of Cognitive Frames

Hayles' theory emphasizes that cognitive frames are shaped by technical conditions. The proliferation of generative AI images has the potential to change human cognitive frames themselves. In other words, repeated exposure to 'statistical familiarity' may cause human pattern recognition mechanisms to increasingly adapt to 'average' patterns.

This is similar to Benjamin's analysis of changes in perception in the age of mechanical reproduction. Benjamin argued that film editing and montage changed human perception. Likewise, the proliferation of generative AI images may change human visual cognitive frames in the direction of adapting to 'statistical averageness.'

## 7. Implications for Eco-Digital/Artificial Nature Research

### 7.1 The Paradox of Artificial Nature

The paradox of generative AI images demands a new understanding of the concept of 'artificial nature.' Artificial nature refers to the phenomenon where a technically constructed environment by humans acquires natural characteristics. Generative AI images vividly demonstrate the paradoxical nature of this artificial nature.

On the one hand, generative AI images are 'artificial.' They are not created by humans but generated by algorithmic statistical computation. On the other hand, these artificial images acquire 'natural' characteristics. An 'image ecosystem' is formed, where images seem to influence and evolve with each other. The synthetic data feedback loop is the core mechanism of this artificial ecosystem.

### 7.2 Tasks for Eco-Digital Research

Eco-digital research is a new interdisciplinary field analyzing the relationship between digital technology and ecosystems. The paradox of generative AI images raises the following tasks for this research field.

First, 'conceptualization of a statistical ecosystem': Generative AI images form a 'purely informational ecosystem' that does not consume physical resources. How does the operating principle of this ecosystem differ from a physical ecosystem? Second, 'conditions of artificial diversity': In natural ecosystems, diversity is an indicator of health. How can 'diversity' be defined and maintained in the ecosystem of generative AI images? Model collapse shows a crisis of this diversity. Third, 'ethics of human-machine co-evolution': The proliferation of generative AI images changes human cognitive frames. What are the direction and ethical implications of this change?

### 7.3 A New Ontology of Technical Objects

The paradox of generative AI images demands a new ontology of technical objects. Simondon's concept of concretisation provides a starting point for this new ontology, but it is not sufficient to explain the specificity of generative AI.

What is needed is the development of a 'statistical ontology.' This ontology regards statistical distributions and patterns as the fundamental conditions of existence, rather than the uniqueness of individual objects. Generative AI images are a concrete example of this statistical ontology. Each image exists only as a 'sample' of a statistical distribution, and its uniqueness is merely statistical variability.

This statistical ontology requires a fundamental reexamination of Benjamin's concept of aura. Benjamin's aura is based on 'uniqueness' and 'authenticity.' However, in statistical ontology, uniqueness is an illusion, and authenticity loses its meaning. The 'already seen' feeling of generative AI images shows the effect of this statistical ontology on human phenomenological experience.
