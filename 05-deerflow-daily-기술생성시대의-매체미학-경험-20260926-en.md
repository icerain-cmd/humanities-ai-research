# The Presence Dilemma of Industrial Heritage Exhibitions Intervened by Generative Sound

## — An Analysis of the Tension Between Phenomenology and Interactive Media Aesthetics

---

## 1. Problem Setting: The Conflict Terrain of Two Experience Paradigms

Industrial heritage sites such as closed factories, power plants, and mines inherently possess a dual temporality. They retain the once-living rhythms of labor and machinery within their materiality, yet exist as silent, static 'ruins' in the present. The moment a generative sound model intervenes in such a place — an experience where on-site sounds and synthesized 'memory sounds' are blended in real time according to walking speed and停留 location — we face a fundamental question about the ontological status of place experience.

Does this experience restore the **unmediated presence** of the place, or does it replace it with an **individual sensory path** designed by an algorithm?

This question is not merely a technical evaluation. It reveals an ontological conflict between the 'common perceptual world' as described by **phenomenology** and the 'construction of variable experience' presupposed by **interactive media aesthetics**. Below, we systematically analyze this conflict and conceptually clarify its terrain.

## 2. Phenomenological Frame: Conditions of Place Presence

### 2.1. The 'Common World' of Perception and the Unmediatedness of Place

The basic thesis of phenomenology is simple. Perception is always perception **of 'something'**, and that 'something' is a part of the world that exists independently of my consciousness. Husserl's 'return to the things themselves' implies that perception is always a direct access to the world. Merleau-Ponty radicalized this issue further, showing that perception is not merely an act of consciousness but the movement of the **lived body (le corps vécu)** within the world.

> The space of industrial heritage, in Merleau-Ponty's terms, is not a 'thing (chose)' but part of the 'flesh of the world (le monde de la chair)'. When I step into it, my body already resonates with the gravity, temperature, reverberation, and material texture of that place. This resonance is **unmediated**. It is pre-reflective; it is a relationship my body already has with the place before I consciously 'choose to listen'.

From this perspective, the real-time intervention of generative sound raises a fundamental problem. If the sound I hear is no longer the sound of the place itself, but the output of an algorithm determined by my walking speed and停留 time — has my body's 'direct access' to that place already been replaced by a structured mediation?

### 2.2. Sartre's Analogon: A Device That Makes the Absent Present

Among the local search results, **Document 4** (Sartre's theory of the image) provides an important conceptual tool for this issue.

> For Sartre, the image consciousness (conscience imageante) is fundamentally different from perceptual consciousness (conscience perceptive). While perception makes the object present 'as it is', image consciousness posits the object as **absent** while simultaneously making it 'present'. Here, the **analogon** is the material ground that substitutes for the absent object. The face in a portrait is merely paint on canvas, but it functions as an analogon that makes the person I love 'present'.

The 'memory sounds' synthesized by the generative sound model perform precisely the role of this analogon. The sounds of machinery no longer heard in the closed factory, the footsteps of past workers, the vanished industrial rhythms — these are absent from the site, but are made 'present' through AI-generated sounds.

However, there is a crucial difference here. The analogon in painting is **fixed**. The viewer stands before the canvas and has the freedom to unfold the image consciousness at their own pace. In contrast, the analogon of generative sound is **dynamic and responsive**. If the viewer walks fast, the synthesis of sound changes accordingly; if slow, it changes differently. This creates an inverted relationship where the viewer does not 'use' the analogon, but the analogon transforms itself according to the viewer's actions.

> **Issue 1**: Does the dynamic analogon violate the spontaneity of perception? Or does it rather enable a new kind of image consciousness?

## 3. Interactive Media Aesthetics Frame: The Logic of Variable Experience

### 3.1. Interactivity and the Proliferation of Space

**Document 1** offers an important insight. The claim that 'space proliferates' through user interaction is linked to the proposition that cyberspace experience is determined by user actions. Applying this logic to the industrial heritage exhibition yields the following structure:

> Each visitor's walking speed and停留 location generate different 'experiential paths' through the exhibition space. The synthesized sounds heard by Visitor A, who walks slowly, are qualitatively different from those heard by Visitor B, who walks quickly. This difference is not merely a matter of personal taste; it constitutes **different sensory universes**.

Interactive media aesthetics affirms this difference. Rather, it sees it as creating the value of personalized experience. In the frameworks of Brenda Laurel or Mark Hansen, the structure where user action feeds back to transform system output positions the user not as a mere receiver but as a **co-agent of meaning creation**.

### 3.2. The 'Distribution of the Sensible' Performed by the Generative Model

At this point, a more fundamental problem emerges. The generative sound model does not simply provide a 'personalized audio guide'. It performs the **distribution of the sensible (le partage du sensible)** — to borrow Jacques Rancière's concept — itself. That is, the algorithm decides which sounds deserve to be heard, which memories are worth synthesizing and replaying, and which past rhythms should be included in the present auditory experience.

This decision is never neutral. When synthesizing the 'memory' of a closed factory, which sounds are selected and which are excluded? The shouts of workers, the noise of machinery, or the sounds of nature surrounding the factory? This selection already involves a specific **politics of memory**.

## 4. Core of the Conflict: Common Perceptual Experience vs. Variable Sensory Paths

### 4.1. The Demand of Phenomenology: The Commonality of Perception

The 'common perceptual experience' in phenomenology does not simply mean 'several people see the same thing'. It is a more fundamental claim:

> The world is essentially **our world (unser Welt / notre monde)**. When I perceive an object, I always-already (immer schon) presuppose that the object can be given to others in the same way. For Merleau-Ponty, the 'intersubjectivity (inter-subjectivité)' of perception is a transcendental condition of perception. I and the other can communicate only by participating in the same 'flesh of the world'.

Let us see how this condition changes with the intervention of generative sound:

| Dimension | Condition of Unmediated Presence | Condition of Mediated Experience | Point of Conflict |
|-----------|----------------------------------|----------------------------------|-------------------|
| Object of perception | Fixed material place (same) | Place overlaid with individual synthesized sounds (different) | Is it the same place or a different place? |
| Role of the body | Direct resonance with the place | Double feedback of place + algorithm | Who (what) constitutes the experience? |
| Relation to others | Sharing the same place | Hearing different sounds in the same place | What is shared? |
| Temporality | The place's own time (layers of industrial history) | The 'real-time' synthesized by the generative model | Is the 'authentic' time of the past represented? |

The table shows a clear tension. Under the **condition of unmediated presence**, the place is a common ground given equally to everyone. Under the **algorithmically mediated experience**, the place becomes a variable sensory field constructed differently for each visitor.

### 4.2. Direction of Resolution: Distinguishing Layers of Experience

However, a more precise analysis of this conflict opens a path to partial resolution through distinguishing layers of experience, rather than a simple binary choice.

**Layer 1 — Material Foundation (Unmediated):**
Visitors still share the same physical space — concrete floors, rusted steel frames, high ceilings, specific temperature and humidity. At this layer, the presence of the place remains unmediated. Everyone experiences the same gravity and the same visual environment in the same space. In Merleau-Ponty's terms, the material infrastructure of the 'flesh of the world' is maintained.

**Layer 2 — Auditory Experience (Mediated + Variable):**
However, at the auditory layer where generative sound intervenes, experience sharply diverges. Each visitor experiences a different auditory texture according to their movement patterns. At this layer, the 'common place' no longer exists.

**Layer 3 — Integrated Experience (Interactive):**
At the deepest layer, the visitor's body constantly moves between the material space and the auditory synthesis, constructing an integrated experience. At this layer, the problem shifts from the dichotomy of 'unmediated vs. mediated' to **how the visitor's body processes the tension between the two**.

This layer distinction allows the following conclusion:

> **Generative sound neither completely replaces nor completely restores the unmediated presence of the place.** It overlays a layer of auditory variability on the material foundation, forcing the visitor's body to construct experience within the tension between the two layers. This tension itself is the core characteristic of this exhibition experience.

## 5. Theoretical Deepening: Bachelard, Lacan, and the Problem of the 'Desiring Subject'

### 5.1. Bachelard's Material Imagination — When the Place Speaks to You

**Documents 2** and **5** relate to Gaston Bachelard's concept of 'material imagination'. For Bachelard, water, fire, air, and earth are not mere physical elements but fundamental repositories of 'material images' through which human imagination operates. Just as water and fire generate dramatic meaning in the theater of Necrosius, in the space of industrial heritage, iron, concrete, coal dust, and the smell of machine oil are themselves material images that trigger imagination.

From this perspective, the role of generative sound takes on an interesting duality:

- **Positive possibility**: Generative sound can auditorily activate the 'material imagination' Bachelard spoke of. The sound of the factory's steel structure 'whispering', the sound of coal dust 'singing' — such synthesized sounds can trigger the imaginative potential inherent in the material itself.
- **Negative risk**: At the same time, these synthesized sounds risk **replacing** the visitor's own material imagination. For Bachelard, imagination is the spontaneous activity of the subject. Listening to AI-synthesized 'memory sounds' may short-circuit the unique imaginative relationship the visitor forms with the material of the place.

### 5.2. Lacan and the Genealogy of Desire — What Does Personalized Experience Desire?

**Document 3**'s discourse on desire (Lacan-Deleuze-Freud) extends this issue to the dimension of the 'desiring subject'. The personalization mechanism of generative sound appears on the surface to respond to the visitor's 'desire'. The technical description "provides a personalized auditory experience tailored to your walking speed and停留 time" implies a promise to fulfill each individual's unique sensory desire.

However, from a Lacanian perspective, this 'personalization' is paradoxical. For Lacan, desire can never be fully satisfied; rather, it is structured around the lack of the object. No matter how sophisticated the 'personalized' auditory experience becomes, it fundamentally cannot replace what Lacan calls 'objet a' — the true cause of desire that can never be captured.

> **Issue 2**: Does generative sound fulfill desire, or is it a device that produces and manages desire? In the latter case, this exhibition may function not as a liberation of the visitor's sensory desire, but as a 'desire management system' that controls desire to operate only within the algorithm's framework.

## 6. Stiegler's Thesis: Tertiary Retention and Industrial Temporal Objects

One of the most powerful theoretical resources in this analysis is Bernard Stiegler's theory of techno-temporality. Stiegler analyzes human temporal experience into three layers of retention:

1. **Primary retention**: Short-term retention of the moment currently being perceived
2. **Secondary retention**: Long-term retention of past experience stored as memory
3. **Tertiary retention**: Memory externalized in technical media (writing, recording, digital storage)

For Stiegler, the problem of modern technology is that tertiary retention is industrially produced and **colonizes** the individual's primary/secondary retentions. Film, television, and today's algorithmic platforms erode the individual's ability to construct their own unique temporal experience.

The industrial heritage exhibition intervened by generative sound stands at the apex of this problem:

> The 'memory sounds' heard by the visitor in the exhibition are neither secondary retention of the place's past (the visitor's own memory) nor primary retention of the site (sounds heard here and now). They are entirely products of **tertiary retention** — technical memory stored and synthesized within the AI model. And by responding to the visitor's walking speed — a biological rhythm — this tertiary retention pretends to be a 'natural' experience.

Here lies the deepest irony. Generative sound, introduced with the goal of 'restoring the presence of the place', according to Stiegler's diagnosis, risks completely subsuming the place experience into the system of tertiary retention, thereby eliminating the **true alterity** of the place. The place is no longer a strange other encountered by my body, but a familiar environment managed by an algorithm.

## 7. Tentative Synthesis: Three Possible Positions

Based on the above analysis, three positions on this question can be constructed.

### Position A — 'Replacement' Thesis (Critical Perspective)

Generative sound **replaces** the unmediated presence of the place. The sounds the visitor hears are no longer the sounds of the place itself, but products of an algorithmically designed sensory path. Under the name of 'personalization', this experience protects the visitor from encountering the alterity of the place. Everything is familiar, predictable, and optimized for the individual. It is a complete case of what Stiegler warned as 'industrial temporal objects'.

### Position B — 'Restoration' Thesis (Affirmative Perspective)

Generative sound rather **restores** the presence of the place. Closed industrial spaces conceal their past in silence. Generative sound auditorily summons this hidden past, helping the visitor encounter the temporal depth of the place. In Sartre's terms, the absent past is made present through the AI-generated analogon. This is not mere nostalgia, but a work of restoring the ontological fullness of the place.

### Position C — 'Tension' Thesis (Dialectical Perspective)

The position supported by this analysis is **C**. Generative sound neither simply restores nor completely replaces the presence of the place. It generates experience within the **tension** between two layers — the unmediated presence of the material foundation and the algorithmic construction of auditory variability.

The core of this tension is summarized as follows:

> Visitors stand in the same space but hear different sounds. Does this difference destroy the common perceptual world? Certainly, yes. But at the same time, this difference **sensorily reveals the multi-layered temporality of the place** — the different layers of time coexisting in the same space.

The decisive issue here is whether this tension is **consciously experienced** or **concealed**. Most generative sound exhibitions conceal this tension. The visitor is designed to experience the sounds as if they were 'natural' sounds of the place. However, a truly critical exhibition should instead make this tension **the content of the exhibition itself**. That is, it should include meta-awareness as part of the experience: "The sounds you hear are synthesized by AI. Others are hearing different sounds. Think about what this difference means."

## 9. Conclusion

Industrial heritage exhibitions intervened by generative sound are a striking case that reveals the tension between presence, commonality, and personalization in place experience. The 'common perceptual world' demanded by phenomenology stands in an ontological tension with the 'variable sensory paths' of generative sound.

This tension should not be read as a 'problem' to be resolved, but as an **indicator that the techno-perceptual condition has changed**. The moment digital media intervene in place experience, the phenomenological ideal of 'unmediated presence' can no longer be maintained. However, this does not mean that experience is entirely reduced to an algorithmic construct. The material space still functions as a common ground, and the visitor's body encounters its own auditory path on that ground.

> The real question is not "unmediated presence or algorithmic construction?" but "how to consciously experience and critically reflect on this tension?"

The answer depends on the design of the technology. The more generative sound renders its intervention transparent and packages it as a 'natural' experience, the more it operates as a violence that eliminates the alterity of the place. Conversely, the more it reveals its mediated nature and allows the visitor to maintain a critical distance from that mediation, the more it can function as a device that sensorily opens up the multi-layered temporality of the place.

The latter direction is precisely the path to productively utilizing this tension, and at the same time, a starting point for triggering a critical imagination about techno-mediated place experience.
